# sample-02

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with Nell, a bank statement and a folder of cinema invoices spread between them. Jo occupies the end nearest the counter, working through supplier prices beside a mug of black coffee.

You bring your lunch over and take the empty chair. The accounts papers are put aside while people eat.

“What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?” you ask Bea.

She considers this while unwrapping the other half of her sandwich.

“We aren’t open yet, so I may be tempting fate. But I want to see the sign lit. I approved more money for that restoration than a treasurer ought to admit over lunch.”

Nell continues eating without rescuing her from the admission.

“I used to see it from the top deck of the bus after an evening shift,” Bea says. “Then for years it was just a dark shape over an empty building.”

She folds the sandwich paper into a smaller square.

“Mostly, I’m looking forward to somebody coming in who hasn’t attended a fundraiser and doesn’t know any of the trustees. They’ll buy a ticket, choose a seat and perhaps complain that the trailers are too loud. My mother cleaned here and I worked the aisles. It was never important every minute of the day. It was just somewhere people used.”

After a sip of water, she adds, “My granddaughter is coming in October. We send each other appalling films on old discs, so we’ve agreed to choose whatever looks most ill-advised on the programme. She’s forbidden me from giving her a historical tour while we watch it.”

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Bea finishes her sandwich, and the conversation gives way to the accounts spread across the table. Nell matches two receipts to the bank statement while Jo returns to comparing wholesale prices. You take your lunch things away and go upstairs.

The afternoon’s programme box covers several months in 1986. Its sheets list changing features, Saturday matinees and local advertisements set in narrow columns. Admission prices have been corrected by hand on some copies. One programme has a screening time crossed out with **NO MATINEE — HEATING** written across the margin; on the reverse, somebody has made a list of portable heaters and rows of abbreviated initials.

You describe the annotation without assigning it an author. Elsewhere, duplicates show small differences: a late show added in blue pencil, an advertisement pasted over another, a date corrected after printing. The variations receive separate notes rather than being treated as identical copies.

During the afternoon, the rain stops. Damp light on the shelves gives way to a brighter strip across the worktable. Nell comes up once, reads several completed entries and says, “These are at the right level. Carry on,” before returning downstairs.

You work until the end time agreed during the induction. Rather than begin another group of programmes, you finish the record already open and mark where the next entry should start.

Downstairs, you enter your finishing time on the hours sheet. Nell glances at it from the office doorway.

“Good. Same start tomorrow.”

The rest of the afternoon is your own.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office with the first public-weekend rota spread across her desk. Names and availability notes have been shifted into narrow columns, with several gaps still pencilled in.

You ask what she wants you to take next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Finish the 1986 programme box, then start the blue box beside it. That runs from 1987 to 1989. Group exact duplicates together; give altered copies their own notes.”

She finds the sheet from yesterday’s yellow tray.

“I checked these against the transfer papers. The paperwork still doesn’t identify the material clearly enough, so leave ownership unconfirmed. We may find a better reference elsewhere.”

Nell returns the sheet to the tray.

“No change to your hours or access today. I’ll be downstairs most of the day. Dev and Hana completed the receiver test last night, so there isn’t another sound test hanging over next week.”

The office telephone rings. Nell reaches for it while turning back to the rota.

“Breakwater Picture House, Nell speaking.”

You have your assignment for the morning.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

You wait until Nell finishes the telephone call.

“Just so it’s clear, I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

“Understood,” Nell says. “You’re not on this rota. Your contract is for the archive.”

She turns back to the front-of-house names on her desk, and you go upstairs to begin the programme box.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

At eleven, you leave the programme box at a stopping point and go downstairs.

Owen is at one of the cafe tables with a mug of tea. A screwdriver and two lengths of metal leaflet rail lie on the counter nearby, but he has put the work aside for his break.

You take a seat and ask, “How’s your week going?”

“Better than it looked on Monday.” He turns the mug between his hands. “I’ve got an interview tomorrow. Permanent exhibition technician at a museum inland. I replied this morning and Nell’s moved my shift, so I can actually get there.”

He takes a drink.

“I told Dad earlier. He offered to drive me before I’d finished giving him the address. Then he looked up the museum car park while we were still on the phone.”

Owen sets the mug down. “It’s only an interview. Still, it’s proper work if it gets that far.”

He glances towards the staff staircase. “How’s the archive going?”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday, probably. I’m not due back here after the interview.” He reaches for his tea again. “But I’ll let you know how it went.”

You leave him to his break and step out through the side entrance. The lane brings you onto Harbour Street, and from there the road drops quickly towards the inner harbour.

The rain has left the paving dark. Fishing boats sit against the quay with their decks nearly level with the pavement at the high tide. One crew is unloading blue crates beneath a rattling winch; farther along, a man hoses scales and weed towards a drain. Nobody calls to you.

You follow the harbour wall past stacked lobster pots and coils of orange rope. Across the water, work carries on inside the boatyard sheds. Metal rings against metal, irregular and sharp, while a refrigerated lorry idles outside the food-packing depot beyond them.

The wind catches the hem of your charcoal coat. Gulls balance on the harbour railings with their heads drawn low against it.

At the boatyard gates, you turn back. The Breakwater’s restored sign appears between two roofs as you climb from the quay, unlit in the pale morning. Your walk takes the rest of the break, and you return through the cinema’s side door alone.
