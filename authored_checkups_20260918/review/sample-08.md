# sample-08

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you go down to the foyer with your lunch. Bea is not there. Nell sits at one of the cafe tables with a sandwich, and Jo has brought over black coffee and a slice of last night’s pie. Supplier price sheets lie folded beside her calculator.

You join them and eat.

“Hana can do seven tonight,” Nell says. “Dev confirmed before noon, so the auditorium’s booked.”

Jo nods. “I won’t be here by then.”

For a while, the conversation stays with Jo’s menu. She has recalculated the pie twice after a supplier changed the pack size of the cheese.

“Eight portions works,” she says. “Any more and it looks mean. Any fewer and I’d have to charge nearly five pounds.”

Nell considers the slice on Jo’s plate. “Eight looks right.”

“That’s what I’ve written.”

The rain thins against the front windows. Jo watches a pale patch open over the harbour.

“If that clears tonight, I might take the telescope out past the headland. It hasn’t had a proper run since Dev fixed the tracking motor.”

“Fran off?”

“Late shift. I’ll go on my own if I’m still awake.”

She finishes her coffee before unfolding the supplier sheets again. Nell carries her plate back towards the office, and you finish lunch at the table while brighter light moves slowly across the stacked opening programmes.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

You return upstairs when lunch is over and reopen the programme box.

The afternoon’s sheets are more varied than their box label suggests. One week has been printed in blue ink, the next in purple. A children’s matinee gains an extra showing in somebody’s pencil, while a late film has its title cramped into the margin after the rest of the programme was typeset. Small advertisements run along the bottoms: a fishmonger, a driving instructor, a shop offering television repairs.

Several weeks are missing. You record the gaps without filling them by inference. Annotated programmes receive their own entries; unmarked duplicates stay grouped together.

Later, you return to the transfer correspondence. It includes a terse exchange about shelving measurements and a collection-day list marked with muddy fingerprints. The unsigned inventory remains unresolved in the yellow tray, while the signed papers provide clear provenance for the programme box you have been working through.

The rain stops during the afternoon. Sunlight reaches the workroom in brief reflections from the wet roofs across Harbour Street, then shifts away again.

When the agreed end of your shift arrives, you finish the record already open rather than beginning another. The transfer files are complete, and the first programme box now has a box-level description with separate entries for its annotated sheets. You save the catalogue and leave the remaining work ready for tomorrow.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office with the staff rota spread across her desk. Several names have been moved between shifts in pencil.

You ask what archive work she wants you to take next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Take the programme box marked 1987 to 1990,” she says. “There’s a brown folder wedged down one side. It’s booking correspondence, not part of the programme run, so give it a separate record.”

She finds the yellow slip you left yesterday.

“I checked for another copy of this inventory. There isn’t one in the office, so leave the ownership unconfirmed. Sam may know whether a signed attachment exists when he’s back, but don’t hold up the rest of the box for it.”

Nell sets the slip aside for follow-up.

“Dev and Hana finished the receiver test last night. His report says the coverage is good. Rosa emailed this morning as well; her photographs are usable, so she doesn’t need another auditorium slot.”

She glances towards the service-yard door.

“Jo has a dry-goods delivery coming through there at ten. The corridor will be crowded for a while, but nothing upstairs has changed.”

Then she turns back to the rota, leaving the shelf list open at the reference for your next box.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you say. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell’s pencil pauses above the rota.

“Understood. Your name isn’t on this, and I won’t add it. You’re here for thirty hours of archive work.”

She returns to the staff columns without crossing out or changing anything. The shelf list for your next archive box remains open beside the rota.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

Midmorning, you leave the 1987–90 programme box open at a stopping point and go downstairs for your break.

Owen is in the service yard beneath the lean-to, sitting on the low step with a mug beside him. A tape measure remains clipped to his belt, but his tools are closed in their case. Through the open side door, a newly fitted shelf is visible beneath the cafe counter.

“How’s your week going?” you ask.

“All right. Mostly doors and shelves.” He rubs a smear of sawdust from his sleeve. “It’s better than trade-show work. You could spend three days building a room and watch somebody tear it down before you’d been paid for it.”

He drinks some tea.

“Tomorrow’s a bit awkward. I’ve got to be inland in the morning. Nell’s moved my shift to Friday, so that part’s sorted.”

A delivery van reverses somewhere in the lane, its warning tone sounding between the buildings. Owen moves his mug away from the edge of the step.

“How are you finding the archive?”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he says. “I won’t be here tomorrow.”

He picks up his mug as you leave the yard.

You go down the lane and cross Harbour Street towards the inner harbour. The rain has left the paving dark and the iron railings bright with water. Diesel, salt and wet rope sharpen the air.

The tide is high enough to lift the fishing boats close to the quay. Two crew members sort plastic crates beside a blue-hulled trawler while its engine idles. Across the basin, a grinder sends brief orange sparks from a boat raised in the yard. The sound reaches you a moment later, thin and metallic.

You follow the quay past the chandlery and the lifeboat station. Beyond it, a public-footpath sign points along the coast towards the headland. Gulls crowd the roof of an ice store, falling silent together when a forklift emerges beneath them.

Nobody stops you. You walk as far as the harbour mouth, where the water changes from the sheltered brown-green of the basin to a harder grey beyond the breakwater. Then you turn back, completing the circuit before your break ends.
