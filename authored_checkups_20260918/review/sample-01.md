# sample-01

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you lock the archive workroom and go downstairs with your lunch.

Bea is already at the joined tables, still wearing her boatyard identification card over a rust-coloured jumper. She has come across on her lunch break with a bright yellow food container and a padded envelope beside it. Martin sits opposite her, his toolbox under his chair. Owen is at the end of the table, while Jo checks supplier prices behind the cafe counter.

Nell comes out of the office carrying the paper diary.

“Dev confirmed at eleven fifty,” she says. “Hana can do seven tonight. The auditorium booking is in ink now.”

Martin nods. “Good.”

Owen takes out his phone. “I sent the Thursday shift change as well.”

Nell checks her email. “I have it. Thursday off, Friday ten until four. Confirmed.”

“What have you got inland?” Martin asks.

“An appointment.”

“Do you need a lift?”

“No, I’ve got it sorted.”

Martin accepts that answer for a moment. “Friday will suit us. We can look at the loose trim by the counter once you’re in.”

“If it’s on my work list,” Owen says.

“It needs doing.”

“I know. I’m front-of-house, though.”

Nell closes the diary. “Friday morning is training. We’ll decide the rest after that.”

Martin looks at Owen rather than Nell. “There’ll always be another thing here that needs your hands.”

Owen folds his sandwich wrapper into a smaller square. “While I’m contracted here, yes.”

The qualification hangs there. Martin reaches for his mug but doesn’t argue with it. Jo continues marking figures on her supplier sheet.

You eat while the conversation moves to less immediate matters. The padded envelope beside Bea’s lunch has come open enough to show a secondhand DVD case with a badly painted sea creature rising behind a city skyline.

You ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

She rests both hands around her food container. “The actual using of it, I think. I don’t want it preserved into somewhere everybody has to whisper.”

She nudges the padded envelope.

“My granddaughter and I post dreadful films to each other. She finds one, sends it to me, and waits for my list of complaints. Then I retaliate. Once we’re open, she’s coming for a Saturday and choosing something here. We’ve never watched one of our bad films in the same room.”

Bea looks towards the auditorium doors.

“And I like seeing the work credited properly. Martin painted some of those boards. June Mercer painted others. My mother cleaned this place for years. When I was an usher, all of that was just ‘staff.’ This time the names don’t have to disappear.”

Martin turns his mug slowly on the tabletop. “Provided we get the right names.”

“That too,” Bea says. “Especially that.”

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Bea’s phone alarm sounds at five to one. She seals the secondhand DVD inside its padded envelope, says goodbye to the table and heads back towards the boatyard. Martin carries his toolbox to the loose trim by the counter, and the lunch conversation breaks up without anyone drawing you into another subject.

At one, you return to the archive workroom.

The afternoon settles into a steady sequence. You finish the programme-box description, then catalogue two more files from the transfer papers. One receipt can be matched to the storage cartons now on the shelves. The typed inventory marked **assorted cinema papers** remains too vague to establish ownership of anything by itself, so its status stays unresolved in the yellow tray.

Nell comes up once to review the flagged material. She finds a reference in the acquisition correspondence that settles one item, but leaves the broad inventory as you recorded it.

“That one stays unconfirmed,” she says. “The paperwork may turn up later. We don’t improve the evidence by wanting it to be complete.”

She returns downstairs, and you continue working.

By the agreed end of your shift at four, the first transfer box has a complete set of file-level records and the programme box has a usable date range, extent and condition note. You return every sheet to its original order, close both boxes and put them back in their marked positions. The yellow tray holds only the unresolved inventory. The loan cupboard has remained locked all afternoon.

You save the catalogue, shut down the laptop and lock the workroom.

As you reach the bottom of the stairs with the archive key, Owen is standing in the open office doorway. His voice carries into the foyer.

“The Thursday appointment is an interview,” he tells Nell. “Permanent exhibition technician at a museum. I replied this afternoon and accepted the interview. Nothing else—I haven’t been offered a job.”

Nell sets down the rota. “All right. Your Friday shift is confirmed.”

“I haven’t told Dad yet.”

“I won’t mention it to him.”

“Thanks. I will.”

Owen steps away from the office as you arrive. He gives you a brief nod but doesn’t turn the disclosure into a conversation. Nell passes you the hours sheet, and you record the end of your shift.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office with the opening-weekend rota spread across her desk. The paper diary lies beside it, yesterday’s provisional auditorium hold overwritten in ink and marked **COMPLETED**.

You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether there are any practical changes you need to know about.

“Continue with the programme boxes,” she says. “The two marked P-three and P-four. Their ownership is covered by the transfer schedule. Record each programme, keep duplicates together and flag annotations or damage. Don’t rearrange them by date yet.”

She checks the shelf list and adds a note beside the box numbers.

“If you finish both, go back to the next transfer-paper folder. Leave the old payroll bundle where it is. The trust owns it, but it contains personal information, and I want to set an access rule before it enters the catalogue.”

Nell closes that file.

“No loans today, so the cupboard stays locked. I’ll be downstairs all morning finishing the rota. If you find another ownership problem, use the yellow tray.”

She then gives you the updates from the previous evening. Dev and Hana completed the auditorium test within their booked half hour. Reception worked throughout the seating area, although one receiver failed its charging check and has been tagged for return to the supplier. Rosa emailed early this morning to confirm that Monday’s photographs are usable, so she does not need a retake or another room booking.

“The archive room is yours for the day,” Nell says. “Nothing else has changed.”

She hands you the updated shelf list, leaving the next assignment clearly marked.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you say. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell looks up from the rota. “Understood. And to be clear, I wasn’t adding you to this. You’re employed for thirty hours of archive work. The room upstairs doesn’t make you available for cinema shifts.”

She moves the front-of-house papers aside.

“If somebody asks you directly, you can still say no. Living in the building isn’t the same as being on call.”

Nell opens the diary to October and draws a line through 9–11 October.

“Gina—my wife—has booked us a walking weekend then. We’ve already postponed two holidays for this project. I’m marking those days as leave now, before I build another rota around myself.”

She writes **NELL AWAY** across the three dates, closes the diary and returns to the opening-weekend schedule. Your archive assignment remains unchanged.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

Mid-morning, you come downstairs for a break. Owen is at one of the cafe tables with a mug of tea, his phone face down beside it. A half-assembled programme stand waits near the front doors, but he has put the tools away for the moment.

You sit nearby and ask, “How’s your week going?”

“Busy.” He turns the mug between his hands. “I replied to the museum, so the interview’s definitely happening tomorrow. I want to go. I’m not making decisions about a job they haven’t offered me.”

He glances towards the side corridor.

“I told Dad last night. He offered to drive me, then started saying the cinema might keep me on permanently. I had to tell him Nell hasn’t offered that, and I can’t plan around a job that doesn’t exist.”

Owen flattens one corner of his tea bag’s paper wrapper.

“He went quiet. But he texted me good luck this morning, so we’re speaking.”

The side door opens, and Martin comes in from the yard with a coil of cable over one shoulder. He pauses when he sees Owen.

“The offer of a lift tomorrow still stands,” he says.

“I’ve got the journey sorted. But thanks.”

“All right. Let me know when you’re finished.”

“I will.”

Martin accepts that and carries the cable towards the office without trying to reopen the decision. Owen drinks the rest of his tea, then takes his tools back to the programme stand as your break ends.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he says. “I’m off here tomorrow for the interview.”

He returns to the programme stand, and you go back to the catalogue until the midday break.

Then you leave the Picture House alone and walk down Harbour Street. The inner harbour lies beyond the next row of buildings, enclosed by stone arms darkened at the waterline. Fishing boats sit against the quay with their decks crowded by crates, rope and orange floats. A crew in waterproof trousers unloads insulated tubs while a forklift carries empty pallets towards the packing depot.

You follow the harbour road. The morning rain has passed, but shallow water remains in the joins between paving stones. Wind lifts the hem of your charcoal coat and brings the mixed smell of diesel, salt and fish scales washed from the quay.

Past the boatyard gates, masts and scaffold poles form a shifting grid against the sky. Hammering carries from inside one of the sheds. Farther out, the harbour wall narrows towards the breakwater, where waves strike the seaward blocks and spill white spray through the gaps.

You walk as far as a bench overlooking the harbour entrance. A small trawler comes in against the tide, engine labouring, with gulls following close behind its stern. Nobody joins you or asks for your attention.

After a few minutes, you retrace the quay and return towards Harbour Street on your own.
