# sample-04

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

When you come downstairs for lunch, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with a blue accounts folder open beside her. She and Nell are matching two payments to their invoices, speaking in low voices over columns of dates and reference numbers.

You take a place at the other end of the table and begin eating. Once the folder closes, you ask Bea, “What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?”

“Technically, it isn’t open yet.” She looks around the foyer. “But I like that it has ordinary problems again. Somebody needs a room, somebody has moved the scissors, and there are never enough teaspoons. For years, the only thing happening here was rain coming through the roof.”

She folds her glasses and sets them on the accounts folder.

“I liked last night. Rosa asking who painted each board and refusing to accept ‘one of the old staff’ as an answer. When I worked here, those boards were changed between shows and nobody imagined anyone would care who held the brush. Martin did some. June Mercer did some. Other people altered them when dates changed. I want their names kept with the work where we can manage it.”

Bea glances towards the locked front doors.

“Once the public comes back, I’m looking forward to the few minutes after a film ends. I used to stand near those doors while people came out arguing about what they’d just seen. You could tell which couples had loved it and which ones were going to disagree all the way home.”

Nell gathers the invoice copies. “And the sign.”

“Yes, the sign,” Bea says. “I spent two years telling everyone we had to be sensible, then approved more than we planned because I wanted to see it lit again. They’re testing it after dark tonight.”

She reopens the accounts folder to check one final figure. You finish your lunch while Bea and Nell return to the cinema’s payments.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

You let Bea and Nell finish checking the remaining payment. Bea clips the invoices into her folder, leaves it in the office and heads back out through the front doors.

After clearing your lunch things, you return upstairs.

The afternoon settles into the programme box. You separate nothing permanently and preserve the order you found, but the catalogue makes its disorder intelligible: a run of weekly sheets, three duplicate issues, missing weeks and several items filed under the wrong year. A stiff card marks your place whenever you remove a group for description.

One programme has been folded into quarters and used for pencilled arithmetic. Another carries a circular stain over the admission prices. You record those features without attempting repairs. Where a date has been altered by hand, you distinguish the printed date from the amendment.

Later, you return to the yellow tray. The unsigned inventory remains useful as evidence of how the material was collected, but it cannot settle ownership by itself. You add that limitation to its record and leave the broader ownership question flagged for Nell.

The rain stops during the afternoon. Light from the landing changes from grey to pale gold, while the sounds below come and go: the office telephone, the front doors, a delivery trolley crossing the foyer.

When the clock reaches the finish time agreed for your shift, you complete the entry already open and do not begin another. The programmes return to their box, the place card stays with the next unlisted group, and both boxes go back to their shelf locations. You save the catalogue and leave the worktable clear.

Downstairs, you enter your finishing time on the hours sheet. Nell is in the office with the yellow-tray notes beside her, ready to review without holding you past the end of the shift.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, you find Nell in the office with yesterday’s catalogue entries open on her screen and the yellow-tray note beside them.

You ask what she wants you to take next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Finish the programme box you started,” she says. “Then take the next box on the shelf, the late-eighties programmes. Begin with a box-level record before describing individual runs. Count exact duplicates, but don’t discard or separate them.”

She turns to the unresolved inventory.

“The unsigned list belongs with the transfer papers, but it still doesn’t prove ownership of every loose item it mentions. Keep that limitation in the record. I’ll check the older handover correspondence when Sam’s back.”

Nell writes two box references on a slip for you.

“No loans today. Stay with the trust-owned programme material unless I bring you something else.”

There is one practical change. During Tuesday night’s test, the restored sign lit successfully except for one lamp whose colour was visibly colder than the others. Martin had been right about it.

“The sign contractor is coming between ten and eleven,” Nell says. “The front entrance will be closed while the ladder is up, so use the side door if you go out. It shouldn’t affect power upstairs.”

She gives you the assignment slip and returns to the office rota. The archive workroom and its locked loan cupboard remain available under the same arrangements as yesterday.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

With Nell’s front-of-house rota open on the desk, you make your position clear.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

“That’s fine,” Nell says. “Your contract is thirty hours of archive work. The room doesn’t put you on call, and I’m not adding you to the public rota.”

She closes the rota folder.

“If anyone asks you directly to cover the foyer or help with an event, send them to me. You can decide about a separate offer if one is ever made, but it won’t be treated as part of this job.”

Nell returns to her paperwork without trying to negotiate an exception. Your assignment remains the two programme boxes upstairs; nothing has been added to it.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

During your midmorning break, the front doors are taped off while the sign contractor works from a ladder outside. Martin stands on the pavement below, comparing the replacement lamp with the others.

Owen is inside at one of the cafe tables. A cordless drill and a closed tool case sit beside a newly fitted programme rack, but he has put the work down and is drinking from a mug.

You ask, “How’s your week going?”

“Uneven.” He looks across at the rack. “That’s level now, at least. The wall isn’t, but I’ve stopped taking it personally.”

He turns the mug between his hands.

“I’ve got an interview tomorrow. Permanent exhibition-technician job at a museum inland, eleven o’clock. The email came on Monday, and I replied last night. Nell’s moved my Thursday shift to Friday.”

His gaze goes briefly towards the glass doors, where Martin is still occupied with the sign.

“It’s only an interview. Nobody’s offered me a job, and I haven’t decided anything that can actually be decided yet.” He takes another drink. “I do want to see what they’re offering. I like working here more than I expected, which hasn’t made the timing simpler.”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he corrects. “I’m not here tomorrow.”

With the front entrance still closed beneath the contractor’s ladder, you leave through the side door and yard gate. The lane brings you back onto Harbour Street, then downhill towards the inner harbour.

The tide is partway out. Dark weed shines along the lower stones, and the fishing boats sit beneath the level of the quay with their ropes drawn at steep angles. Two people in waterproof aprons unload grey crates from a boat while a forklift moves between the sheds farther along. Across the water, hammering carries from the boatyard in irregular bursts.

You follow the harbour railings alone. The rain has cleared, but the paving remains damp and the wind coming between the buildings lifts your unruly hair. September visitors occupy only one of the benches; everyone else along the quay appears to be working, delivering something or walking with a definite destination.

At the turn by the harbour office, you loop back. A gull drops a mussel onto the paving behind you and descends to inspect the result.

The walk takes the remainder of your break. When you return through the service yard, the contractor is folding the ladder and the cinema sign now holds the same warm colour across every lamp. Owen has gone back to fitting the programme rack, and you pass without extending the conversation.
