## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

When you come downstairs at half past twelve, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with a calculator, a ledger and a stack of invoices. She has come in to reconcile the trust’s recent payments with Nell. Jo is behind the counter, checking supplier prices and reheating the last of the chickpea stew.

There is enough for four bowls. You join them at the table, and when the account papers have been pushed safely aside, you ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“We’re not quite open yet,” she says. “Ask me again after the twenty-sixth, when somebody has complained about the seats.”

She tears bread into her stew before continuing.

“But I like that it’s being used. When we first took the lease, coming in meant buckets under the roof and meetings with surveyors. Last week I arrived and Jo had onions cooking, Owen was looking for a screwdriver, and there were children outside reading the opening poster. It sounded like a workplace again.”

Jo glances up from her bowl. “The onions were for stock, not atmosphere.”

“They did both.”

Bea looks towards the front windows.

“I’m glad we restored the sign. It cost more than I wanted it to cost, which is an awkward thing for the treasurer to admit. My mother cleaned here, and when I was an usher I could see that sign from the top of Harbour Street on my way to an evening shift. I want to see it lit again.”

Nell says, “You argued for it at the vote.”

“I also recorded my objection to the final invoice. Both things can be true.”

She takes another piece of bread.

“And I’m pleased there are paid jobs in the building. Not enough of them, perhaps, but real ones. People say ‘community project’ when they mean several different things. Sometimes they mean they care about a place. Sometimes they mean they expect somebody else to work for nothing.”

Jo gives a brief nod at that and returns to her supplier sheet. Bea’s answer ends there, with the four of you eating while rain freckles the foyer windows.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

The lunch conversation runs down naturally. Bea returns to the trust ledger, Jo gathers her supplier sheets, and Nell takes the invoices back towards the office. You leave your bowl at the kitchen counter and return upstairs when the half-hour break ends.

The programme box occupies the rest of the afternoon. You record its overall date range, then work through the smaller groups within it without changing their existing order. Duplicates receive separate counts. Missing weeks remain gaps rather than invitations to guess. Pencilled alterations to performance times go into the descriptions, along with torn folds, rust marks and patches of old water damage.

Shortly after two, Nell comes up for the yellow tray. She checks the unsigned inventory against the transfer schedule.

“Keep its ownership unconfirmed,” she says. “I’ll look for the missing attachment in the office files. Until we find it, this list tells us what somebody expected to collect, not what was legally transferred.”

She takes the query slip downstairs and leaves the document in its original folder.

You continue until half past three, the agreed end of today’s shift. Rather than begin another group of programmes, you finish the record already open, save the catalogue and return every item to its marked box and shelf. The loan cupboard has remained locked throughout the afternoon.

Downstairs, you enter **09.00–15.30**, with the half-hour lunch break, on the hours sheet. In the paper diary beside it, Dev’s former pencil hold has been replaced in ink: **19.00–19.30 — DEV / HANA, AUDITORIUM**.

Nell glances at the clock and then at your entry.

“That’s your six hours. Leave the rest for tomorrow.”

The working day ends without stretching beyond the hours agreed.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office with the staff rota spread across her desk. The paper diary lies open beside it.

You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether there are any practical changes you need to know about.

“Same hours as yesterday,” she says. “Finish the programme box you started, then take the box labelled **PROGRAMMES — 1987–1990**. Make the box-level record first. If you find letters, invoices or photographs mixed into the programmes, leave them where they are and flag them rather than creating a new arrangement.”

The unsigned inventory remains unresolved. Nell has checked the office files but has not found the missing attachment.

“Keep its ownership marked unconfirmed. Sam may have another copy with the grant records, but he’s away this week. We wait for evidence.”

She turns the diary so you can see today’s building note.

“An engineer is testing the fire alarm at eleven. I’ll warn you immediately beforehand, then the sounders should run twice. If the alarm goes on beyond the test or sounds at any other time, come straight out.”

Nothing has changed with the archive access: your workroom key remains with you, while the loan-cupboard key stays in the office.

“Dev and Hana completed the receiver test last night,” Nell adds. “They’ve signed off the auditorium coverage, so there’s no follow-up in your workspace.”

She closes the diary and returns to the rota. Your next programme box is waiting on its marked shelf upstairs.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you say. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell looks up from the rota.

“That’s understood. Your contract is for thirty hours of archive work. You’re not on the front-of-house rota, and living upstairs doesn’t make you available after your shift.”

She turns the sheet enough for you to see that your name does not appear in any of its columns.

“I won’t ask you to cover those shifts. At half past three, your time is your own.”

Nell returns to the rota without making the boundary into an argument. You take the archive key and go upstairs to the programme box assigned for the morning.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

You spend the first part of the morning finishing the earlier programme box and beginning the box marked **1987–1990**. Shortly before eleven, Nell calls up the stairs to warn you that the alarm test is about to begin.

The sounders run twice, each burst loud enough to vibrate through the metal shelving. After the engineer confirms the system has reset, you take your morning break.

Owen is in the foyer. He has been fitting a wooden display rail beneath the opening programmes; his tools are closed in their case while he sits at the cafe table with a mug. With the work paused, you ask how his week is going.

“Busy,” he says. He turns the mug slowly between his hands. “I confirmed an interview this morning. Permanent exhibition-technician job at a museum inland. It’s tomorrow at eleven.”

He keeps his voice low, though the foyer is otherwise empty.

“Nell knows now. She’s moved my Thursday shift to Friday. Jo knew already—I told her when we were unloading on Monday.”

Owen glances towards the auditorium doors.

“My dad doesn’t know yet. I want to tell him myself.” He pauses. “And it’s only an interview. Nobody’s offered me a job.”

His tool case remains shut beside the chair. For the moment, he has nothing else demanding his attention.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday, probably,” he says. “I’m not due in tomorrow now that Nell’s moved the shift.”

He does not turn the correction into a longer exchange. You leave him with his tea and take the rest of your break outside.

Harbour Street runs downhill between wet brick buildings. At the bottom, the inner harbour opens around a basin of dark water, with fishing boats tied two and three deep along the quay. The tide has fallen far enough to expose green weed on the stonework. Ropes creak against bollards, and a diesel engine idles aboard a blue-hulled boat while plastic fish crates are passed ashore.

You walk along the harbour alone. Beyond the boats, the breakwater reaches into a grey strip of sea. The rain has moved offshore, leaving the paving slick and the air sharp with salt, fuel and the food-packing depot farther along the waterfront.

A forklift crosses one of the boatyard entrances. Two workers in orange waterproofs coil a length of cable beside an open shed. Nobody stops you. Near the end of the quay, a shuttered summer kiosk faces a row of empty benches, their seats still beaded with rain.

You turn back towards Harbour Street. By the time you reach the cinema, the alarm engineer’s van has gone. Owen is at the foyer wall again, checking the display rail with a spirit level, and you return upstairs before your break runs over.
