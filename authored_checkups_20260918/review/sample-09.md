# sample-09

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

When you come downstairs, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with Nell, Jo and Owen. She has brought a cheese sandwich, an apple and a small jar of pickled walnuts. Jo is drinking black coffee beside a sheet of pencilled menu costs.

Owen stands at the end of the table with a folded note.

“Thursday moved to Friday,” he tells Nell. “Same hours. Ten till four.”

Nell reads it, opens the staff rota and moves his name into Friday’s column.

“That’s confirmed. I’ll email you a copy this afternoon.”

“Thanks.”

He sits down with his lunch. Martin is not here to hear the exchange, and nobody asks Owen about the inland appointment.

You take a place at the table. With Bea present, you ask, “What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?”

She considers this while adding two pickled walnuts to her sandwich.

“The sign,” she says first. “We spent more on restoring it than I was comfortable putting in the budget. Then I approved it anyway. I’ve wanted to see it lit again since the place closed.”

Nell glances up from the rota. “Martin’s testing the lamps after dark.”

“He’s already warned me that one may be the wrong shade of white.”

Bea closes her sandwich and continues. “My granddaughter’s coming in October, too. We send each other terrible films on secondhand discs. She writes categories on the cases. Worst wig. Least convincing monster. Things like that.”

“She’s very systematic,” Jo says.

“Only when the film is bad. I want to bring her here and let her choose something we can complain about together.”

Bea takes a bite, then looks across the foyer towards the locked auditorium.

“And I want this to become ordinary again. Not another fundraiser, not people coming in because they’ve been asked to save the building. Someone finishes work, sees what’s on and buys a ticket. That part.”

“And coffee,” Jo says.

Bea reaches for her apple without answering the addition.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Lunch winds down without another subject taking hold. Bea caps her jar of walnuts, Jo returns to her menu figures, and Owen carries his plate to the kitchen. Nell takes the amended rota back into the office.

You return upstairs and continue with the programme box.

The afternoon’s sheets are small, folded listings printed in one or two colours. Several carry advertisements for Harbour Street shops that no longer occupy their addresses. Matinee times have been corrected in pencil; one programme has a coffee ring obscuring half the ticket prices.

Between two programmes from October 1986, you find a narrow scrap of paper used as a work list:

**BEA — LATE DOOR**  
**MARTIN — LAMP 2**  
**JUNE — FRONT BOARD**

It has no date or signature. Its position suggests a connection with the surrounding programmes but does not prove one. You record the wording and where the scrap was found without assigning it a date or identifying the writer.

The rest of the box takes you through children’s matinees, short-notice programme changes and several weeks represented only by duplicate sheets. By half past three, today’s agreed finishing time, the transfer file is catalogued and the programme box has a complete preliminary description.

You save the records and go downstairs to enter your hours.

Nell is in the office updating the paper diary. “Dev confirmed before noon,” she says. “Hana can make seven tonight, so you may hear speech and test tones from the auditorium for half an hour.”

She inks over the provisional booking: **19.00–19.30 — DEV / HANA — HEARING ASSISTANCE TEST**.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, you find Nell in the office with the opening-weekend rota spread across her desk.

“What’s my next archive assignment,” you ask, “and are there any practical changes I need to know about?”

“Finish the programme box first. Give yesterday’s scrap its own item record—handwritten work list, undated. Don’t call it a rota when we don’t know that it was one.”

She checks a note beside the keyboard.

“After that, take the blue business box from the second shelf. Staff rotas, supply invoices and maintenance papers, mostly 1985 to 1989. Keep the files in their existing groups. The names on that scrap may turn up again.”

Nell intends to send Rosa the catalogue reference once the item record exists. The wording about June and the front board may be relevant, but it does not identify a particular board or establish who wrote the list.

“As for changes, Dev and Hana completed the receiver test last night. Everything passed, so there’s no repeat booking today.” Nell caps her pen. “I’m leaving at three. Gina and I have the same evening free, and I’m keeping it that way. Bring down anything needing a decision before then.”

A figure pauses beyond the office doorway. Owen has a phone in one hand and his work jacket folded over the other arm.

“Nell, when you’ve finished here, could I have a word?”

“Yes. Give me two minutes.”

He waits in the foyer while you take the archive key and head upstairs with your next assignment.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before leaving the office, you say, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell looks up from the rota. “Understood. You’re not included on this. Your contracted hours are for the archive.”

She returns to the availability sheet without asking you to justify it.

Owen moves aside as you pass through the doorway. Once you reach the staff stairs, he goes into the office and closes the door behind him. Whatever he has come to discuss remains downstairs as you return to the archive workroom.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

By quarter to eleven, the handwritten work list has its own catalogue entry and the first maintenance file is under way. You leave it at a natural break and go downstairs.

Owen is at one of the cafe tables with a mug of tea. A half-assembled programme stand lies beside him, along with a screwdriver and a little heap of wood shavings. He has stopped working long enough for the tea to remain hot.

“How’s your week going?” you ask.

“All right. Complicated, maybe.” He turns the mug once between his hands. “I’ve got an interview tomorrow. Permanent exhibition-technician post at a museum inland.”

He corrects himself. “An interview for the post. There’s no job offer. I replied this morning and said I’d come.”

The Thursday-to-Friday shift change now has its explanation.

“I told Nell when you went upstairs. Jo already knew.” Owen looks towards the foyer, though Martin is nowhere in it. “My dad—Martin—doesn’t know I applied. He’s started talking as if I’ll still be working here after October.”

He takes a drink of tea.

“I want to tell him myself. I just haven’t done it yet.”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he says. “I won’t be here tomorrow.”

He picks up the screwdriver again, and you leave him with the programme stand.

You use the rest of your break for a walk alone. Harbour Street drops quickly towards the water, its paving still dark from yesterday’s rain. At the bottom, the inner harbour opens between warehouses and repair sheds.

The tide is low enough to expose weed on the stone walls. Two fishing boats sit beneath the quay with their decks below pavement level. A crew in orange waterproofs passes plastic crates ashore while a forklift waits beside the packing-depot van. Diesel, salt and wet rope carry on the wind.

You follow the railings towards the boatyard. Most of the summer visitors have gone; the benches face empty water, and the few people on the quay are working, walking dogs or carrying shopping. Across the harbour, welding light flashes white inside an open workshop.

Near the boatyard gate, a weathered council map marks the coast path east along the cliffs and the longer route around the outer breakwater. You reach the map, then turn back.

A chandlery clock shows eleven as you climb Harbour Street again. You return to the Picture House before the end of your break, alone as intended.
