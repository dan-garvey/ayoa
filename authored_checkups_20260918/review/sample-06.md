## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

Downstairs, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with Nell. A folder of cinema invoices has been closed and pushed aside for lunch. Bea has a cheese sandwich, a small jar of pickled walnuts and a fork wrapped in a paper napkin.

You sit with them and begin eating.

“What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?” you ask Bea.

She finishes the bite she is chewing before answering.

“Ask me after the public have actually come through the doors.” She spears a walnut. “But I’m looking forward to sitting in the audience. I’ve spent two years in this foyer looking at invoices and funding conditions. I’d like to watch a film here without needing to count anybody.”

“You’ll count them,” Nell says.

“Afterwards.”

Bea closes the jar and wipes a drop of vinegar from its rim.

“My granddaughter and I send each other terrible films on secondhand discs. The worse the sea monster, the better. I want to bring her here and let her choose our seats. Eventually I’ll persuade the older-film group to programme something dreadful.”

“Not until the project can survive the refund claims.”

“She has excellent judgment about bad films.”

A delivery van passes the front windows, briefly whitening the foyer with reflected light. Bea looks towards the unlit sign above them, though only its shadow reaches the glass.

“And I want to see the sign tonight,” she says. “Martin’s testing it after dark. Restoring the lamps cost more than repainting the lettering and leaving them dead. Everyone expected the treasurer to object.”

“You made us price it three times,” Nell says.

“I wanted to be certain before I said yes.”

Bea plans to return at eight, after the receiver test, and watch from across Harbour Street when Martin switches the sign on. Then she takes another pickled walnut and returns to her lunch.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Lunch ends when Bea screws the lid onto her walnut jar and Nell reopens the invoice folder. You return upstairs to the catalogue.

The afternoon passes through the programme box. You establish its date range, group obvious duplicates without changing their sequence, and record gaps where weekly sheets appear to be missing. Pencilled alterations remain part of the descriptions rather than being erased or interpreted. A handful of badly creased programmes receive individual condition notes; the rest are covered by the box-level record.

Nell comes up once to examine the query in the yellow tray. The unsigned collection list cannot be matched to a signed schedule, so she leaves the ownership field unconfirmed and initials the query sheet for later review. Then she returns to the office.

At four, the agreed end of your shift, you finish the record already open. The programmes go back onto their marked shelf, the catalogue is saved, and the workroom is secured.

Downstairs, the paper diary lies open beside Nell. Dev’s pencilled hold has been replaced by a confirmed booking for him and Hana from seven until half past. Beneath it, Nell has added **SIGN TEST — 8.00, MARTIN/BEA**.

You enter your hours on the sheet. Nell checks the time and says, “That’s you finished for today. Same start tomorrow.”

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, you find Nell in the office with the paper diary, a mug of tea and Dev’s test sheet spread across the desk.

“What should I take next,” you ask, “and are there any practical changes I need to know about?”

“The hearing receivers passed last night. Hana found one weak patch at the back left, but Dev adjusted the transmitter and the second test was clear. They don’t need the auditorium again today.”

Nell turns over the test sheet.

“The sign was less cooperative. Bea made Martin switch it off and on twice so she could watch it properly. On the third attempt, one lamp stayed dark. Martin’s coming at eleven to look at the fitting, and Owen will help him with the access panel. They’ll be working at the front of the building. No power interruption upstairs, but you may hear drilling.”

She gives you a shelf slip marked with two programme-box references.

“Take these next. They cover 1987 to 1991, though the dates overlap and neither run is complete. Make a box-level record first, then give separate condition notes only where an item needs one. When those are finished, stop there. Don’t start the photograph cartons until we’ve looked at their existing labels together.”

The archive-room key is already on your ring. Nell returns to the receiver report while you take the shelf slip upstairs.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before leaving the office, you say, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell looks up from Dev’s report.

“All right. I haven’t put you down for any. You were hired for the archive, and that’s where your thirty hours go.”

She closes the front-of-house rota lying beside the diary.

“The upstairs room doesn’t make you the caretaker, either. Outside the hours we agreed, your time is yours.”

Nell returns to the receiver report, and you take the programme-box references upstairs.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

At your morning break, the drilling at the front of the building has stopped. Martin and Owen sit at one of the cafe tables with two mugs between them. A lamp fitting rests on a folded cloth beside Martin’s screwdriver.

You ask Owen how his week is going.

“More complicated than it was on Monday,” he says. “I’ve got an interview tomorrow. Exhibition technician at a museum inland. I replied this morning.”

Martin’s hand stops halfway towards his mug.

“Permanent?” he asks.

“That’s what they’re advertising.”

“When were you going to tell me?”

“Now.”

Owen doesn’t dress the answer up. Martin sits back, looking first at him and then at the dismantled fitting.

“I thought you were staying here after the reopening.”

“I said I liked it here. My contract still ends next month.”

“They’ll need people.”

“Nobody’s offered me anything permanent. And this is only an interview. I haven’t got a decision to make unless they offer me the job.”

Martin rubs his thumb along the handle of the screwdriver. “Does Nell know?”

“I told her this morning. She’s moved my Thursday shift to Friday.”

For several seconds the only sound in the foyer is the refrigerator behind the cafe counter.

“What time’s the interview?” Martin asks.

“Eleven.”

“I can drive you.”

“I’ve arranged the train.”

“If it’s cancelled, ring me.”

Owen nods. “I will.”

Martin takes up the faulty fitting and turns it over to expose the wiring. “Permanent’s worth going for.”

“Yes,” Owen says. “It is.”

Their break ends when Martin carries the fitting back towards the front doors. Owen follows with the tools, leaving his empty mug on the table to collect when the job is finished.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

As Owen gathers the remaining tools, you tell him, “I’ll see you tomorrow.”

“Maybe Friday,” he says. “I’m off the rota tomorrow. I might come by after the interview.”

You leave through the front doors and walk down Harbour Street alone.

The inner harbour is busy without being crowded. A small fishing boat has come in on the morning tide, and two crew members are unloading shallow crates onto the quay. Water slaps against the stone beneath them. Diesel, wet rope and seaweed sharpen the air.

You follow the harbour rail past stacked lobster pots and a row of boats nosing against their lines. Across the water, work rings from the boatyard: metal struck twice, a pause, then the whine of a power tool. A packing-depot lorry waits beside the road with its engine running.

September has thinned the visitors. Most of the people along the quay are carrying tools, shopping or paperwork rather than cameras. Nobody stops you. At the end of the inner harbour, the north sea shows between the breakwaters, roughened by wind beneath a pale sky.

You turn there and retrace the water. By the time you climb Harbour Street towards the Picture House, Martin’s drill has started again above the entrance.
