At half past twelve, you lock the archive workroom and go downstairs with your lunch.

Bea is already at the joined tables, still wearing her boatyard identification card over a rust-coloured jumper. She has come across on her lunch break with a bright yellow food container and a padded envelope beside it. Martin sits opposite her, his toolbox under his chair. Owen is at the end of the table, while Jo checks supplier prices behind the cafe counter.

Nell comes out of the office carrying the paper diary.

“Dev confirmed at eleven fifty,” she says. “Hana can do seven tonight. The auditorium booking is in ink now.”

Martin nods. “Good.”

Owen takes out his phone. “I sent the Thursday shift change as well.”

Nell checks her email. “I have it. Thursday off, Friday ten until four. Confirmed.”

“What have you got inland?” Martin asks.

“An appointment.”

“Do you need a lift?”

“No, I’ve got it sorted.”

Martin accepts that answer for a moment. “Friday will suit us. We can look at the loose trim by the counter once you’re in.”

“If it’s on my work list,” Owen says.

“It needs doing.”

“I know. I’m front-of-house, though.”

Nell closes the diary. “Friday morning is training. We’ll decide the rest after that.”

Martin looks at Owen rather than Nell. “There’ll always be another thing here that needs your hands.”

Owen folds his sandwich wrapper into a smaller square. “While I’m contracted here, yes.”

The qualification hangs there. Martin reaches for his mug but doesn’t argue with it. Jo continues marking figures on her supplier sheet.

You eat while the conversation moves to less immediate matters. The padded envelope beside Bea’s lunch has come open enough to show a secondhand DVD case with a badly painted sea creature rising behind a city skyline.

You ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

She rests both hands around her food container. “The actual using of it, I think. I don’t want it preserved into somewhere everybody has to whisper.”

She nudges the padded envelope.

“My granddaughter and I post dreadful films to each other. She finds one, sends it to me, and waits for my list of complaints. Then I retaliate. Once we’re open, she’s coming for a Saturday and choosing something here. We’ve never watched one of our bad films in the same room.”

Bea looks towards the auditorium doors.

“And I like seeing the work credited properly. Martin painted some of those boards. June Mercer painted others. My mother cleaned this place for years. When I was an usher, all of that was just ‘staff.’ This time the names don’t have to disappear.”

Martin turns his mug slowly on the tabletop. “Provided we get the right names.”

“That too,” Bea says. “Especially that.”
