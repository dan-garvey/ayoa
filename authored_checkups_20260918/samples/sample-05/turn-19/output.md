At half past twelve, Bea is at one of the foyer tables with a sandwich tin and a small jar of pickled walnuts. Her boatyard lanyard hangs from the back of her chair; two payment forms lie signed beside Nell’s diary.

Nell has changed Dev’s pencilled auditorium hold to an ink booking.

“Dev confirmed at eleven fifty,” she says. “Hana can do seven until half seven.”

She takes the signed forms into the office, leaving you and Bea with your lunches.

You ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“The sign, for one,” she says. “Which is a poor answer from the treasurer. I know exactly what that restoration cost.”

She adds a pickled walnut to her sandwich and considers the rest.

“Mostly, I want it to stop being a project. My mother cleaned here, and I worked usher shifts. People remember the premieres and the packed shows, but plenty of evenings were just wet Tuesdays with thirty people in and sweets ground into row F.”

She closes the sandwich.

“I want those evenings back too. Children making too much noise at a matinee. Somebody coming in alone after work. People choosing a film because they fancy it, not because we need them in a photograph for a grant report.”

Bea turns the little jar between her hands.

“My granddaughter and I send each other terrible films on secondhand discs. She finds things with covers that look actively hostile. I’d like her to come here, pick something from the programme, and spend the journey home explaining why my choice was worse.”

She smiles slightly. “Not the opening photographs, really. The ordinary week after.”
