Bea screws the lid onto her jar, gathers the signed payment forms’ carbon copies and returns to the boatyard. The conversation ends there.

You go back upstairs and resume with the programme box.

The afternoon’s papers give a more particular picture of the old cinema than the transfer schedules do. Weekly listings share space with adverts for fish suppers, carpet cleaning and coach trips. Several show times have been changed by hand. One children’s matinee is marked **FULL** in blue pencil.

Between two programmes from 1987, you find a folded duty sheet. Bea Finch is assigned to the front doors and interval checks; Martin Vale is listed in the projection booth. The remaining notes concern a temperamental kettle, a missing torch and who must sweep row F after the late show.

You record the duty sheet separately, including where it was found, then return it to the same position among the programmes. The names come from the document itself; no recollection is needed to establish them.

The rest of the shift passes in the same steady work: identifying date runs, recording gaps and distinguishing printed programmes from handwritten enclosures. At the agreed finishing time, you complete the entry already open, save the catalogue and return the programme box to its shelf.

The afternoon leaves a completed box-level record, several item descriptions and a short list of dates missing from the surviving run. You sign out at the end of your shift.
