“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he corrects. “I’m not here tomorrow.”

With the front entrance still closed beneath the contractor’s ladder, you leave through the side door and yard gate. The lane brings you back onto Harbour Street, then downhill towards the inner harbour.

The tide is partway out. Dark weed shines along the lower stones, and the fishing boats sit beneath the level of the quay with their ropes drawn at steep angles. Two people in waterproof aprons unload grey crates from a boat while a forklift moves between the sheds farther along. Across the water, hammering carries from the boatyard in irregular bursts.

You follow the harbour railings alone. The rain has cleared, but the paving remains damp and the wind coming between the buildings lifts your unruly hair. September visitors occupy only one of the benches; everyone else along the quay appears to be working, delivering something or walking with a definite destination.

At the turn by the harbour office, you loop back. A gull drops a mussel onto the paving behind you and descends to inspect the result.

The walk takes the remainder of your break. When you return through the service yard, the contractor is folding the ladder and the cinema sign now holds the same warm colour across every lamp. Owen has gone back to fitting the programme rack, and you pass without extending the conversation.
