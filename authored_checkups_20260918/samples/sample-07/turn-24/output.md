“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday, probably,” he says. “I’m not due in tomorrow now that Nell’s moved the shift.”

He does not turn the correction into a longer exchange. You leave him with his tea and take the rest of your break outside.

Harbour Street runs downhill between wet brick buildings. At the bottom, the inner harbour opens around a basin of dark water, with fishing boats tied two and three deep along the quay. The tide has fallen far enough to expose green weed on the stonework. Ropes creak against bollards, and a diesel engine idles aboard a blue-hulled boat while plastic fish crates are passed ashore.

You walk along the harbour alone. Beyond the boats, the breakwater reaches into a grey strip of sea. The rain has moved offshore, leaving the paving slick and the air sharp with salt, fuel and the food-packing depot farther along the waterfront.

A forklift crosses one of the boatyard entrances. Two workers in orange waterproofs coil a length of cable beside an open shed. Nobody stops you. Near the end of the quay, a shuttered summer kiosk faces a row of empty benches, their seats still beaded with rain.

You turn back towards Harbour Street. By the time you reach the cinema, the alarm engineer’s van has gone. Owen is at the foyer wall again, checking the display rail with a spirit level, and you return upstairs before your break runs over.
