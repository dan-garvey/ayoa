At half past twelve, you go downstairs. Bea is at one of the cafe tables with a paper bag, a boatyard lanyard still around her neck and a folder of supplier invoices beside her chair. She has come over during her lunch break to sign two payments with Nell.

Jo has left a container in the refrigerator marked **STAFF — TUESDAY**. Nell reheats the remaining stew and brings three bowls to the table. Bea unwraps a cheese sandwich cut thickly with pickled walnuts.

You eat with them. For several minutes, the talk stays with the meal: whether the stew is better after a night in the refrigerator, and the loaf Nell bought that morning from a bakery which has sliced it too thin for dipping.

Then you ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“It isn’t open yet,” she says. “At present, I enjoy that the heating works and the ceiling stays above us.”

She takes another bite of her sandwich before continuing.

“I’m looking forward to the first children’s matinee. Not the invited guests or the photographs outside. A proper audience, with somebody arriving late and somebody else dropping sweets under a seat we’ve only just cleaned.”

Nell tears a narrow slice of bread in half. “You can clean the sweets.”

“I know how. I did it for four years.”

Bea glances towards the front windows.

“My mother cleaned here too. I don’t want it made into a shrine to the way things were. Half the old shifts were dull, and some were dreadful. But when the sign was lit and you could hear the audience settle from the foyer, the place felt used. I want that again.”

“You mainly wanted the sign,” Nell says.

“I read the restoration estimate.”

“You approved it very quickly.”

“I knew exactly what it cost.”

There is no embarrassment in Bea’s answer. She bites into a pickled walnut with an audible snap.

“And I want to bring my granddaughter to something appalling,” she adds. “We send each other terrible films on disc. She thinks she’s found the worst one ever made. She’s fourteen, so naturally she’s wrong.”

Nell laughs, and Bea returns to her sandwich. The invoice folder stays closed until the bowls are empty.
