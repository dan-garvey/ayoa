Professor Stone sets down his magnifying lens.

“Food and clothes,” he repeats. “Yes. Someone should have answered those before they sent you a signet and an acceptance notice.”

He pulls a narrow lending ledger from beneath his papers, writes **GARVEY** beside the date, and slides the brown volume fully across the table.

“The house is supplied. Your room and meals are part of the Conclave arrangement. As for the inheritance itself, make the lawyers send you accounts, not assurances. I’ve seen too many institutions assume an heir has somebody else reading the small print.”

“Thank me after you’ve read it. It may not deserve it.”

He returns to his brittle papers.

The opening section does not make the human seat sound grand. It is full of practical work: translations of contracts between parties who use the same word differently; disputes over bridge tolls and harvest rights; notes on who must be consulted before a treaty can be called valid. One account describes a human delegate spending three weeks determining whether an elven lease had actually been explained to the mortal family expected to honor it.

The prose assumes you already know more than you do. Still, it offers something firmer than the primer’s thin praise: the seat has duties, procedures, limits. It is not merely a vacant chair waiting for someone to decorate it.

Beyond the library wall, the card game carries on for a while—Dena objecting loudly to a play, someone else accusing her of inventing rules, Rashid laughing once before the voices blur together. Later, chairs scrape and the front door opens and shuts behind departing guests.

Stone packs away his lens at last. Without looking up, he says, “Mr. Garvey, bring one question to my seminar on Thursday about what the human seat is for—not what everyone expects it to be.”
