Rashid's fork pauses over the rice. “They gave you Article Nineteen before they showed you where the library is?”

Aldric turns his glass by the stem. “It is not an incidental law. People should understand it before they arrive here.”

“I said it mattered.” Rashid sets the fork down. His voice remains courteous, though the line of his shoulders has stiffened. “I only think the Academy could have managed both.”

Ashara reaches for the peppers. “They could have. Caelindra, tell him where the library is before Aldric starts reciting the primer.”

Caelindra looks past her at Aldric, then back to you. “Through the common room, left at the first arch. The iron stair takes you to the upper shelves. The window tables are tolerable, provided no one from Arcana has claimed them with six open books and a sleeping head.”

“The west terrace is worth seeing after dusk,” Ysolde says. “It is colder than the rooms. You can stand at the rail and watch the lightning below the plateau.”

“The chapel is open most evenings,” Aldric says. “It is quieter there than the terrace, if that is what you prefer.”

Seraphel smooths the fold in her napkin with one finger.

“The gardens are quiet
for a little while
after dinner.
The roses near the east wall
are still flowering.”

Thessaly considers this. “There are benches, too. If you sit on one alone, people tend to come and ask what is wrong. I have not learned how long you must sit before that happens.”

An attendant places saffron rice, glazed roots, and peppered roast before you, then moves on to the next setting. Steam lifts from the rice in soft curls. You eat while the conversation finds its own course around the table.

Caelindra tastes the roast and pauses with her fork halfway down. “Oh. This is good.”

Ashara glances over. “You sound disappointed.”

“I expected the pears to have set the tone for the evening.”

“The roast needs more pepper,” Ashara says.

“There is a dish beside you. You may use as much as you like.”

Rashid breaks a roll in two. “Please do not persuade the cook to alter the whole pot. Last term you did, and none of us could taste anything but pepper for the rest of the meal.”

“You put too much on your plate.”

“Because you said it was good.”

“It was.”

Ysolde turns another dark slice of pear over on her spoon. “In the mountains, we smoke fruit with juniper and salt. Usually it is served with meat.”

Caelindra looks at the pear, then at Ysolde. “That does not make me more willing to try it.”

“I was not trying to persuade you.”

Rashid's mouth twitches. Ashara laughs under her breath.

“I think the cook
has heard enough
about the pears,”
Seraphel says.

“The cook has heard nothing from me,” Thessaly says. “I ate bread.”

The attendants refill water glasses and clear the first empty dishes. Candlelight moves across the table whenever someone reaches for the serving bowls. Caelindra takes a second helping of roast without comment, which Rashid notices and does not mention.

Instead, he asks, “Has anyone finished the Covenant Law reading?”

“I finished it,” Caelindra says. “It reduced the Veyran arbitration to fourteen pages and never explained why either side accepted the terms. It simply called them mutual concessions.”

“We have not begun the seminar yet,” Aldric says. “The reading is meant to give everyone the same starting point.”

“Then it should begin somewhere useful.”

Ashara lifts her glass. “You have already decided Professor Windwhisper is wrong?”

“I have decided the summary is lazy. I do not know who assigned it.” Caelindra takes a sip of wine. “That is why I have not complained to anyone important.”

Ysolde looks toward the high windows, where the dark garden paths show between the curtains. “Has anyone noticed the north bridge lamp?”

Rashid follows her glance. “The one that flickers?”

“It has done it at the same time each evening since I arrived.”

“I have seen it from the octagon,” Ashara says. “It begins shortly after sunset.”

Rashid gives her a sidelong look. “You can work the octagon into any subject.”

“You asked whether I had seen it.”

“I asked whether anyone had.”

“I am anyone.”

For a moment, Rashid looks as though he may answer. Then he shakes his head and takes another piece of bread. Aldric suggests that the lamp may need repair; Ysolde says she will watch it again tomorrow. No one seems inclined to make more of it than that.

By the time the attendants bring small almond cakes glazed with honey, the plates have been cleared and the discussion has thinned into smaller exchanges. Thessaly asks whether the cakes contain a filling; when told that they do not, she takes one. Caelindra eats hers in two neat bites. Ysolde sets hers beside the last of the pear and tries the pear again.

Ashara puts down her cup and looks at Rashid. “After this, come to the octagon. I want to work through the left-side turn before tomorrow's bout.”

Rashid glances toward the common-room doorway. “I told Dena and the others I would be at the card table after dinner.”

Ashara's expression changes at once, her attention sharpening. “You told me you would drill.”

“I did. I should have said I had already made plans with them.” He rubs his thumb once along the edge of his napkin. “I thought they could start without me, but they ought to know I will be late.”

“They can start,” Ashara says. “Give me the half-hour you agreed to, then go to them.”

Rashid looks at her for a beat. “All right. I will tell them before we leave.”

“You should,” Thessaly says. “They may wait if they think you are coming straight there.”

Caelindra brushes a crumb from her fingertip. “It will take less time to tell them than this discussion has.”

“Thank you, Caelindra,” Rashid says. “That is very helpful.”

“I know.”

Ashara's tail shifts once behind her chair. “Finish your cake. We are leaving when the plates are gone.”

The attendants begin collecting the dessert dishes. Chairs scrape back one by one, and the room loosens after the meal: Seraphel rises with a faint rustle of white wings, Ysolde waits for an attendant to pass before standing, and Rashid folds his napkin beside his untouched pear. Beyond the dining-hall doors, the lamps of Garvey House throw warm light into the common room, where the rest of the evening has begun to gather.
