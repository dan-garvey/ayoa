The lamp on your desk has been turned low enough that its flame makes a small, steady oval among the ink stains. Beyond it, the framed letter catches a line of light across its glass: cramped handwriting, an old date, and the Garvey name in a hand that belonged to someone long dead. The room around it is orderly in the manner of a place prepared for an expected guest. Your mended charcoal coat brings its familiar roughness to the chair when you shift.

Outside the window, the gardens are already silvered by evening. Pale paths run between dark hedges toward the Academy buildings, and farther out the bridges hang above weather that never quite becomes rain here. A lamp-globe wakes near the garden wall, then another. From below, a door opens on a wash of warm air carrying butter, pepper, and the sweetness of fruit roasting somewhere nearby.

The dinner bell is not grand. It gives three clear notes from the ground floor, and the house answers in smaller sounds: a chair drawn back, a cupboard shut, voices gathering where the stairs turn.

A woman in Academy livery appears at the open door with a covered serving dish balanced on one palm. She has evidently made the climb in silence; only the soft clink of a spoon beneath the lid gives her away. She inclines her head without looking past you into the room.

“Mr. Garvey? Supper is ready. The other first-years are in the dining hall.” She indicates the stair rail beyond the doorway. “Straight down, then through the common room.”

From below, a young man's voice says, “Those are the pears? They're nearly black.”

“They've been poached,” a woman answers, low and matter-of-fact. “They're meant to look like that.”

“I know what poached means.”

“Do you?”

“I was checking before I ate one.”

“You were deciding whether to complain, Rashid.”

“I can do both.”

“There is clove,” says a third voice, quieter than the first two. After a moment, she adds, “And smoked sugar.”

“Thank you, Ysolde,” says another woman. Her words are precise, with a faint edge of relief at having the answer. “I knew there was something unpleasantly familiar about it.”

“The smoke is intentional,” the first woman says.

“That does not make it less smoke.”

Someone laughs, briefly and without much restraint. The woman at your door waits with the dish, patient as a piece of furniture, while firelight moves over the common-room ceiling below. The stair rail begins just beyond her shoulder, polished smooth by generations of hands. At its foot, the old house opens toward the long candlelit table and the people already finding reasons to disagree over dinner.
