The server inclines her head at your thanks. By the time you reach the foot of the stairs, she has set the covered dish among the others and taken up a place against the paneled wall with two more attendants.

The dining hall is brighter than the common room, candle flames doubled in the varnish of the long table. Most of its seventeen settings remain untouched, but the occupied stretch near the middle has acquired the disorder of a meal already under discussion: a platter of dark pears, a dish of glazed roots, a basket with one torn roll lying on the cloth beside it.

The young man nearest the pears has a slice speared on his fork as if it has offered testimony against itself. He looks up first. Across from him, a tall woman with swept-back black-red horns has one hand around a wineglass, her gold eyes settling briefly on the empty place you indicate.

“Please,” she says. “If Rashid has another minute alone with the pears, he’ll ask them to apologize.”

“They know what they did,” Rashid says.

The pale woman beside him examines the fruit on her own plate. “They do not.” Her white hair is gathered loosely at her nape; her voice is quiet enough that the statement makes Rashid lower his fork to hear it.

“Thank you, Ysolde,” says the silver-haired woman farther down the table. “I had begun to fear I was the only person here with standards.”

The horned woman’s mouth moves at one corner. “You objected to the smoke before you tasted it.”

“I objected to its presence. Taste is a separate grievance.”

One of the attendants draws back the empty chair. Its place card bears only GARVEY in dark ink. When you are settled, the horned woman sets her glass down.

“Ashara vel Kothren.” She gives her name plainly, as if it belongs in the same category as the saltcellar and the evening’s menu. “That is Rashid vel Amara.”

Rashid inclines his head. “I’ll accept the introduction, though not the characterization.”

“Ysolde Thornmantle,” says the pale woman. She has finally cut into the pear.

“Caelindra Vaeyn,” says the woman with silver hair. Her violet eyes rest for a moment on the Garvey signet before returning to the plate in front of her.

A small pair of white wings shifts behind the next woman’s shoulders as she looks up from a folded napkin. “Seraphel Dawnquill. / You arrive before the plates are overturned.”

“That has happened?” asks a slight woman with blue-black hair that drifts around her shoulders though the room is still.

Rashid glances toward her. “Not tonight.”

“Then I am Thessaly Morrow,” she says, apparently satisfied with the distinction.

At the far end of the occupied places, a broad-shouldered young man in white and gold rises just enough to acknowledge you before sitting again. “Aldric Verantus. Welcome to Garvey House.”

The attendants begin serving the main dishes, moving with the practiced silence of people who have heard arguments over supper before and learned where not to pause. Steam rises from a shallow bowl of saffron rice; the scent of pepper and browned butter displaces the sharper smoke of the pears.

Ysolde tastes hers. She chews for a moment, then says, “It is better warm.”

Caelindra regards her with grave disappointment. “That is an argument for every unpleasant thing ever served.”

“Not every thing,” Ysolde says. “Some things are worse warm.”

Ashara laughs once, open and unguarded, then reaches for a pear herself. Rashid watches her take a bite before he does the same. His expression changes by so little that it might only be the candlelight.

“All right,” he says. “The clove was not a mistake.”

“A historic concession,” Seraphel says. “Mark the evening kindly.”

“Do not,” Caelindra says. “It will encourage him.”

The small burst of talk leaves a quieter space at the table. Rashid sets his fork down and turns toward you, his tone less theatrical now.

“Did the Academy give you a useful account of this place before you arrived, or only rules and a floor plan?”
