The attendant accepts your thanks with a quiet nod and steps aside. The stairs carry you down into the warmer light of the ground floor, where the common room opens onto the dining hall. Candles burn along the long table, their flames reflected in the dark varnish between the place settings. An attendant is setting out a platter of nearly black pears beside bowls of saffron rice, glazed roots, and sliced roast.

Near the middle of the table, a young man with amber eyes has paused with a piece of pear on his fork. Across from him, the tall woman with swept-back black-red horns is watching him with a patient sort of amusement.

“Please, sit,” she says when you indicate the empty chair. “Rashid has been trying to decide whether the pears are edible.”

“I said they were overcooked,” the young man replies. His tone is careful, though he is clearly annoyed with the fruit. “And that the smoke has taken over everything else.”

The pale woman beside him leans over her plate. Her white hair has been gathered at the nape of her neck, and she studies the pear as if the answer might be visible in its surface. “There is smoked sugar in the syrup,” she says. “And clove.”

The silver-haired woman farther down the table gives the platter an offended look. “That explains it. It does not improve it. A pear did not need wine, clove, and smoke.”

“You liked the wine,” the horned woman says.

“I liked the wine before it was put on the pear.”

Rashid lowers his fork. “That is exactly what I meant.”

“You also said it was ruined,” the horned woman says.

“It was a very good pear.”

The pale woman takes a small bite. She chews without hurry. “The pear is still good.”

Rashid looks at her. “You just listed the ingredients like a warning.”

“I was explaining the taste.”

A short laugh escapes the horned woman. At the other side of the table, the woman with small white wings folds the corner of her napkin between two fingers.

“Perhaps the next course
will cause less debate.”

The words come in a low, unhurried verse, plain enough that the rhythm only becomes clear after she has finished.

An attendant pulls out the empty chair. Its place card reads GARVEY in dark, formal lettering. Once you are seated, the horned woman sets down her glass.

“Ashara vel Kothren,” she says. “Welcome. We were expecting you before the pears became the main event.”

“Rashid vel Amara.” He inclines his head. Fine dark fur shows along the back of the hand resting beside his plate. “You have arrived at a useful moment. There are still better things on the table.”

“Ysolde Thornmantle,” says the pale woman. Her voice is quiet, but it carries cleanly over the plates and candles.

“Caelindra Vaeyn.” The silver-haired woman’s violet eyes settle briefly on the Garvey signet, then return to you. “I hope the house staff gave you a better introduction than this.”

The winged woman looks up. “Seraphel Dawnquill.
Welcome to Garvey House.”

A slight woman with blue-black hair, seated beside Seraphel, considers the untouched pear on her own plate. “Thessaly Morrow,” she says. “I have not had one, so I have no opinion.”

At the end of the occupied part of the table, the young man in white and gold rises partway from his chair. His greeting is formal but not cold. “Aldric Verantus. Welcome, Rowan.”

The attendants begin serving the main dishes. Steam curls from the rice, carrying saffron and browned butter; the roast has been rubbed with pepper and herbs. Ashara takes the serving spoon for the roots and adds a bright red pepper from a small dish to her plate without comment. Rashid waits until the attendants have passed before reaching for the bread.

“The rice is good,” Ysolde says after tasting it.

Caelindra glances at her. “You sound surprised.”

“I was uncertain about the smell.”

“It smells like butter.”

“And saffron,” Ysolde says. “The pears smelled like smoke.”

Thessaly accepts a piece of bread from the basket, then examines the pale crust. “Is the bread also intended to smell like something else?”

“No,” Rashid says. “You may eat that without an inquiry.”

“I was making sure.”

Ashara tears off a piece of her own roll. “Eat before he starts another argument.”

Rashid gives her a look, then turns back to you. The annoyance he had aimed at dessert has eased; his attention is direct now, as if he is trying to place what you have been told and what no one has bothered to tell you.

“Did the Academy give you anything useful before they brought you here, or only the rules and directions to your room?”
