“The primer is how they make sure no one can claim surprise later,” Rashid says. He sets his fork down beside the rice. “It tells you everything that will get you killed, and very little that helps you find a decent cup of tea.”

Aldric's expression tightens. “Article Nineteen is not included for administrative convenience.”

“No,” Rashid says. “It isn't.” His courtesy has gone a shade more deliberate. “But a person can know a law is serious without being handed it before anyone tells him where the library is.”

Ashara reaches for the dish of red pepper. “There. That was nearly useful.”

“The library is through the common room and left at the first arch,” Caelindra says, lifting her glass. “The spiral stair leads to the upper stacks. They are less impressive than they believe themselves to be, but the window tables are good.”

“The west terrace is good after dusk,” Ysolde says. “You can see the lightning below the plateau. It is quieter than people expect.”

“The chapel remains open,” Aldric adds. “If you want somewhere still.”

Seraphel folds her napkin once along its existing crease.

“The gardens are also still,
until someone decides
they are not.”

Thessaly takes a bite of bread. “That applies to most rooms.”

The attendants lay a plate of saffron rice, glazed roots, and peppered roast before you, then move on with the practiced quiet of people who know how to pass through a conversation without becoming part of it. You eat while the others settle into the meal. No one makes a ceremony of the silence you leave for them.

Caelindra gives the roast an approving look after the first bite, which seems to surprise her more than anyone else. “At least the cook understands pepper.”

“It needs more,” Ashara says.

“Of course it does.”

Rashid tears a piece of bread in half. “You say that about nearly everything.”

“Nearly everything improves with more heat.”

“This is why you are not permitted to season the shared pot.”

“Who prohibited it?”

“Common sense.”

Ashara's tail gives one brisk movement behind her chair. “Then common sense can say so itself.”

Ysolde has returned to the pear. She studies a dark slice on her spoon before eating it. “In the mountains, we smoke fruit with juniper and salt.”

Caelindra turns toward her. “That makes this sound less adventurous, not more.”

“It is less adventurous. There is less salt.”

Ashara laughs, low and sudden. Rashid's mouth twists despite himself.

“A gentler pear,” Seraphel says,
“is still a pear
with a history.”

“That is a very long history for a pear,” Thessaly says.

“You asked no questions about this one,” Rashid tells her.

“I learned from observation.”

The talk wanders after that. Caelindra complains that the introductory reading list has condensed three centuries of elven treaty disputes into fourteen pages and somehow made the result longer; Aldric says the point is to give everyone the same foundation before seminar, and earns a cool look for his trouble. Ysolde asks whether anyone else has noticed that the Academy's north bridge lamp has begun flickering at the same hour every evening. Ashara says she has noticed it, but only because it is visible from the octagon. Rashid says this is not evidence that the octagon is the center of the Academy.

“It is evidence of good sightlines,” Ashara says.

“From one particular patch of dirt.”

“An excellent patch of dirt.”

By the time the attendants bring out small almond cakes glazed with honey, the pear dispute has become a source of intermittent remarks rather than an argument. Candlelight catches on the dark varnish of the table and the rings at people's hands whenever someone reaches for a dish. The serving platters grow bare.

Ashara sets down her cup and looks at Rashid. “The octagon after this. I want the left-side turn for half an hour before tomorrow.”

Rashid glances toward the common-room doorway, then back at her. “I told Dena I would meet the card table.”

“You told me you would drill.”

“I did. I should have said I had already promised them the evening.”

Ashara's eyes narrow, not with surprise exactly, but with the quick calculation of someone revising a plan. “They can start without you. You are at the octagon for the half-hour you offered. Then go keep the other promise.”

Rashid looks at her for a moment. “All right. I'll tell them I'll be late.”

“You should,” Thessaly says, and takes the last bite of her cake.

Caelindra brushes a crumb from one fingertip. “A startling conclusion, reached before midnight.”

“Thank you for your faith in me,” Rashid says.

“I have none. It makes the occasions when you deserve it more memorable.”

The attendants begin lifting the dessert plates. Ashara pushes her chair back from the table, and Rashid folds his napkin beside his untouched pear. Around them, the welcome dinner loosens into the small sounds of people finishing a meal: chairs moving over the floor, a glass set down, wings rustling once as Seraphel rises. Beyond the dining-hall doors, the house holds the rest of the evening waiting in its lit rooms.
