“A Matter of Life and Death,” you say. “I saw it with a friend on a laptop, and I’ve wanted to see it on a big screen ever since. I’m happy to help with supper, but I’m leaving the cinema work until tomorrow’s induction.”

“Quite right,” Nell says. “Tonight you get a chair and a proper meal. We can make you look at boxes in the morning.”

“A laptop is a cruel way to meet that film,” Bea says. “The first time I saw it, somebody had recorded it off the television and missed the beginning. I kept waiting for it to become ordinary. It never did.”

“It would look good here,” Dev says, setting the receiver case more neatly against the wall with his foot. “Once I’ve stopped worrying about whether the sound is going to vanish halfway through.”

Martin gives a short nod. “Big screen helps. You see the work in it.”

“That is nearly a useful sentence for the exhibition,” Rosa says, reaching for her notebook again.

“Nearly?”

“If you tell me whose work, and which work.”

“I can do that.”

“After you’ve eaten,” Jo says. “Everybody sit down before the pie goes cold.”

The chairs scrape over the foyer floor. You take the place Nell has left at the side of the joined tables, between Bea and Owen. Jo brings the stew in its wide enamel pot and sets it down beside the pie. Steam clouds the lower half of her glasses for a moment.

“There’s enough for seconds,” she says. “That does not mean you have to prove it.”

“That sounds aimed at Martin,” Owen says.

“It’s aimed at anyone with a spoon.”

The stew is thick with chickpeas and tomatoes, brightened by lemon and something smoky. The cheese-and-onion pie breaks into crisp flakes where Jo cuts it. Bread passes down the table, then the salad bowl. When someone needs a dish moved, you pass it on; otherwise the conversation finds its own course around you.

Bea tells Martin that her granddaughter has started sending her discs with notes tucked inside their cases, each one explaining in advance which bits Bea is expected to dislike. “She says it saves time afterwards.”

“Does it?” Rosa asks.

“No. I still dislike the bits.”

“That’s because you’re old-fashioned,” Martin says.

“I’m old enough to know when somebody has put a car chase in because they ran out of story.”

“That’s most films I like,” Owen says.

“You like the bits between the car chases,” Rosa says. “You just pretend you don’t.”

“I like the signs.”

“The signs?” Dev asks.

“Station signs, street signs, anything someone had to make before the camera got there. Half the time you can tell a place is supposed to be busy because somebody’s gone to the trouble of putting up notices nobody reads.”

Martin points his fork at him. “That’s the right way to watch things.”

“You say that because you painted notices nobody read,” Bea says.

“They read them if they wanted to know what was on.”

“They read the film title,” Rosa says. “The rest was your opportunity to make an argument with lettering.”

Martin looks as though he might dispute this, then considers the empty space above the tables instead. “Well. A board ought to look as if somebody cared enough to put it there.”

For a few moments, nobody improves on it. Cutlery taps against bowls. Outside, a car passes on Harbour Street with wet tyres hissing over the road, though no rain has yet reached the windows.

Dev takes a second piece of bread and says, “I’ll put the receiver test in another slot. I can label the cases tonight, but it needs the room properly booked when Hana comes in.”

“I’ll find you one,” Nell says. “A real one, not a gap that disappears when somebody needs a rehearsal.”

“I should have asked before I brought it over.”

“You didn’t know,” Rosa says. “You asked when you did.”

Jo lifts her glass. “A rare and beautiful workplace skill.”

“Don’t encourage him,” Nell says, but she is smiling.

The talk moves on to the opening weekend: not a meeting, exactly, though Nell has to be stopped twice from answering questions with a list. Jo says the cafe will begin with fewer things than people keep suggesting, because a short menu can still be hot when it reaches the counter. Bea argues for a children’s matinee with proper cake rather than packets of biscuits. Rosa says the exhibition labels need to be legible from a step back, and Martin insists that means no tiny grey type.

“That one I agree with,” Dev says. “Anything people have to read in a foyer should survive the lighting.”

“You all make it sound as if I’m trying to hide the words,” Rosa says.

“You use colours nobody can name,” Martin says.

“That is not the same thing.”

Nell glances at the repaired ceiling and then at the room around them: the stacked programmes, the cartons of cups, the table made from two smaller ones. “We’ve got a week and a half,” she says. “We’ll get the useful things done first.”

Jo reaches across to take the empty pie dish from the middle. “And eat while we’re doing it, if I have any say.”

“You do,” Bea says.

The meal thins into the last spoonfuls of stew and a plate of bread crusts. Martin tells a story about a packed Saturday when a boy had tried to buy a ticket with buttons, only to be interrupted by Bea before he can decide whether it happened in 1987 or 1988. Rosa writes neither year down. Owen asks Jo whether there is any more of the salad dressing; Jo tells him to look in the bowl before making it sound like a medical emergency.

By the time the foyer clock nears seven, the supper is finished. You carry a stack of bowls through to the kitchen when Jo begins clearing them, and she gives you a brief, practical nod of thanks as she runs hot water into the sink. Owen takes the plates beside you. From the foyer, Rosa’s notebook closes with a soft thump.

“Right,” she says. “Martin, the blue board first, before you remember three different people painting it.”

“I remember one person painting it.”

“Excellent. We’re improving already.”

Martin picks up his jacket from the back of his chair. Bea gathers her glasses case and follows them towards the auditorium doors, asking Rosa to make sure the boards go back against the wall when they are finished. Dev lifts the receiver case and takes it upstairs again. Nell pauses at the office door, watching the small groups separate into the evening’s work.

The supper has reached its end. With the foyer quieter now and your room upstairs still waiting beyond the landing, what does Rowan do?
