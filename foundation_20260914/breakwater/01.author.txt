The staff staircase brings you out beside the office door, where the foyer is warmer than the landing and smells of pastry, tomatoes and the sharp oniony edge of something just cut. The two cafe tables have been joined beneath the hanging lights. Folded opening programmes have been moved into a neat pile at one end; unopened cartons of cups occupy the other.

At the kitchen doorway, a woman in a striped apron has one hand under a pie dish and the other braced against the frame. A man beside her holds a plate of bread cut into uneven slices.

“Hello, everyone. I’m Rowan—Nell’s new cataloguer. Something smells good.”

“Cheese and onion pie,” says the woman. “And stew, which is behaving itself for once. I’m Jo.” She looks at the bread plate. “Yes, please. Take that through before he decides every slice needs its own planning application.”

“I’m Owen,” the man says as he passes it over. “I maintain that a thin slice is just a small disappointment.”

The bread is still warm through the plate. At the table, the silver-haired man who had been arguing about the blue board shifts a chair clear with his foot.

“Martin. Welcome.”

The woman with the pencil and notebook sets both down. “Rosa. And this is Bea.”

Bea gives a small wave from behind a pair of reading glasses. “Glad to have someone here who can tell a photograph from a receipt without getting sentimental about either.”

Nell steps out of the office, bringing the door nearly shut behind her. “You’ve found everybody except Dev. He’s up in projection. He’ll appear once he’s finished trying to charm the sound system into cooperation.”

You set the bread between the programmes and the empty serving bowls. The question about films travels around the table more readily than the introductions have done.

“Wishes, then,” Nell says. “Nothing anyone is allowed to put on a poster by morning.”

“The Red Shoes,” Rosa says at once. “On a properly dark night. It deserves a screen that makes the colour look ridiculous in the right way.”

Martin nods approval, then says, “The Italian Job. A full house, a bit of noise from the back row, people who know exactly what’s coming and still want it to come.”

“You only want to hear them all tell you the Minis are better than modern cars,” Bea says.

“They are better than modern cars.”

“The Muppet Movie for me,” Bea says, before Martin can take up the argument. “My granddaughter will have complaints about it, which is one of the pleasures of taking her anywhere.”

Jo eases the pie onto the counter, where its crust gives a soft crackle. “Close Encounters. Late enough that people will have settled down, and with enough time beforehand for me to make coffee without running a military operation.”

“The Taking of Pelham One Two Three,” Owen says. “The original.”

Rosa glances at him. “For the trains.”

“For the pace,” he says. “The trains help.”

Footsteps come down the staircase behind you. A man in a dark work shirt reaches the bottom with his phone to his ear and a small receiver case tucked under one arm.

“No, don’t shift the hotel job for it,” he says into the phone. “It was worth asking. I’ll catch you another time.” He listens a moment, thanks the caller, and ends the call before looking to Nell. “Hana can’t get here before seven.”

“Then we leave the test for another evening,” Nell says. “We’ll look at the diary tomorrow.”

“Fine.” He sets the receiver case carefully beside the office wall. “Dev, by the way.” His attention catches on the conversation at the table. “And mine is Stop Making Sense, if I get the room sounding as it should first.”

“That one at least comes with a reason,” Rosa says.

“So does mine,” Nell says. She takes a stack of bowls from Jo and begins laying them out. “The Towering Inferno.”

Bea looks from Nell to the recently repaired ceiling. “Not during the first month.”

“Not during the first month,” Nell agrees. “A quiet November night, perhaps. When all the fire doors have had time to prove themselves.”

“That’s exactly when you’ll want a film about a building coming apart,” Jo says, carrying the stew through. “You can all have your catastrophe after you’ve eaten something.”

The pot sends up steam scented with cumin and tomatoes as she sets it in the middle of the joined tables. The chairs are no longer quite enough to keep everyone standing around the edges. Jo pulls one back with her knee, then looks across at you.

“You’ve made us sound like a very particular sort of committee, Rowan,” she says. “What would you show first?”
