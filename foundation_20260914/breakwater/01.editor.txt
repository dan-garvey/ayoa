The staff staircase brings you out beside the office door. The foyer is warmer than the landing and smells of browned pastry, tomatoes, and the raw onion Jo has been cutting for the salad. Two square café tables have been pushed together under the hanging lights. Folded opening programmes sit in a neat stack at one end; unopened cartons of cups wait at the other.

At the kitchen doorway, a woman in a striped apron balances a pie dish against her hip. Beside her, a man holds a plate of bread, some slices noticeably wider than the rest.

“Hello, everyone. I’m Rowan—Nell’s new cataloguer. Something smells good.”

“Cheese and onion pie,” the woman says. “Tomato and chickpea stew as well. I’m Jo. Would you take the bread through? Owen’s cut three slices twice already.”

“I’m Owen,” the man says, passing over the plate. “The first ones were too thick.”

“Leave these ones alone,” Jo says. “They’re fine.”

The bread is warm through the plate. At the table, the silver-haired man who was discussing the blue board shifts a chair out with his foot.

“Martin. Welcome.”

The woman with the pencil and notebook puts both down. “Rosa. And this is Bea.”

Bea lifts a hand from behind her reading glasses. “Hello, Rowan.”

Nell comes out of the office and pulls its door nearly shut behind her. “You’ve met everyone except Dev. He’s in projection with the new receivers.” She looks towards the bowls Jo has begun setting out. “All right, films. Just favourites, though. We don’t need to decide the programme over supper.”

“The Red Shoes,” Rosa says. “I saw it at college with a projector that kept losing focus, and the colour still got me. I’d like to see it here when the screen’s properly dark.”

Martin nods. “The Italian Job. The old one. We had people come back for it when it was rereleased. They’d spent the whole week quoting bits of it, then they’d still make a noise at the end.”

“You would enjoy that,” Bea says.

“I did.”

“The Muppet Movie,” Bea says. “My granddaughter would say she was only there to point out what hasn’t aged well. She’d know all the songs by the time it finished.”

Jo carries the stew through from the kitchen and sets the pot in the middle of the tables. “Close Encounters. It’s a long one, so I’d want people through the doors early enough to get their drinks before the lights go down.”

“The Taking of Pelham One Two Three,” Owen says. “The original.”

“For the trains,” Rosa says.

“Partly. It moves quickly.”

“You’d be watching the station signs.”

“Probably.”

Footsteps sound on the staircase behind you. A man in a dark work shirt reaches the bottom with his phone to his ear and a small receiver case tucked beneath one arm.

“No, don’t move the hotel job for it,” he says into the phone. “I thought it might fit, that’s all.” He listens, then says, “All right. Good luck with it,” and ends the call.

Nell looks over. “Hana can’t make it before seven?”

“No. I’ll run the test another evening.” He sets the case carefully beside the office wall, then looks around the table. “Dev. I’m here until Sunday trying to get the sound and the assisted-listening kit ready.”

When Nell explains the question, he says, “Stop Making Sense. Once I know I’m not going to spend the first song looking at a fault light.”

“That seems sensible,” Rosa says.

“The Towering Inferno,” Nell says.

Bea looks up at the repaired ceiling. “Here?”

“Later on,” Nell says. “Not while I’m still collecting invoices from roofers.”

Jo pulls a chair back with her knee. “Sit down, all of you. The stew’s ready.” She looks across at you as the bowls begin to fill. “And you, Rowan? You started it. What would you show first?”
