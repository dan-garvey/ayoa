“A Matter of Life and Death,” you say. “I saw it with a friend on a laptop, and I’ve wanted to see it on a big screen ever since. I’m happy to help with supper, but I’m leaving the cinema work until tomorrow’s induction.”

Nell pulls a chair out from the joined tables. “I can see why. No boxes until nine tomorrow.”

Bea takes off her glasses and folds them in one hand. “I first saw it late at night on a television that kept losing the top of the picture. I spent most of the film thinking the staircase was supposed to look like that.”

“The colours will hold here,” Martin says. “On a good projector, the reds don’t go brown at the edges.”

Dev has set the receiver case beside the office wall. “I’d like to hear it after I’ve finished the checks. There are quiet bits under the dialogue that I’ve never caught properly at home.”

Rosa lifts her pencil. “I’m not adding any of that to the exhibition notes. I’ve already got three versions of who painted the blue board.”

“You have one version,” Martin says.

“You gave me Pete, Peter, and ‘the tall one with the cap.’”

“They may all be the same person.”

“Sit down,” Jo says, bringing the pie through from the kitchen. “The pastry’s not waiting for an enquiry into Martin’s memory.”

The chairs scrape across the foyer floor. You take the place Nell has left between Bea and Owen. Jo sets the enamel pot of stew beside the pie, then puts the salad and bread within reach of the people at either end.

“There’s enough for another helping later,” she says. “Start with what’s on your plate.”

“That’s for Dad,” Owen says.

“It’s for anyone who thinks a second piece of bread is a personality trait.”

Martin looks at the bread plate. “I’m having one piece.”

“A sensible start,” Jo says, and begins serving the stew.

It is thick with chickpeas and tomatoes, with lemon and something smoky beneath the sweetness of the onions. The pie flakes apart under the serving knife. You pass the bread when it comes to you and move a bowl along when somebody needs room. Around the table, the talk gathers without waiting for your lead.

Bea tells Rosa that her granddaughter has begun posting films to her with notes tucked into the cases. “She writes which scenes I’m going to complain about. Last week she put a little arrow beside every bit she thought was too loud.”

“Did she get them right?” Rosa asks.

“Most of them. She missed the dog.”

“What was wrong with the dog?” Owen says.

“Nothing was wrong with the dog. It was just in a film that didn’t need a dog.”

Martin smiles into his bowl. “She’s been sending you lists since she was small.”

“She’s more organised than I am,” Bea says. “That’s not difficult.”

Owen takes a piece of pie and nods towards Rosa. “She’s seen The Italian Job with me enough times to know where I start looking at the signs.”

“You paused it at the station entrance last time,” Rosa says. “You wanted to know whether the route numbers had been painted for the film.”

“They looked hand-done.”

“They probably were,” Martin says. “We used to change times on the boards ourselves if a distributor altered something at short notice. You’d be up a ladder with the paint still tacky, trying not to leave a thumbprint beside the last showing.”

Rosa looks across at the boards waiting by the auditorium doors. “That’s why I’m asking whose lettering is where. If Pete changed the time, I want his name in the notes as well as yours.”

Martin’s fork pauses halfway to his mouth. “Pete Hargreaves. He did the late shifts. He had a narrow brush he wouldn’t let anyone borrow.”

“That’s better,” Rosa says, writing it down. “Now I’ve got a name and something I can check.”

Dev reaches for another slice of bread, then glances at the receiver case. “I spoke to Hana. She can’t move the hotel job, so we left the test for another night.”

Nell nods. “After supper I’ll look at the diary for a time when Hana and the room are both free. Don’t arrange anything around a gap until I’ve checked it.”

“I won’t,” Dev says. “I can label the cases and finish the booth work tonight.”

“Good,” Rosa says. “I don’t want to move those boards again before we photograph them.”

Jo points the serving spoon at the case. “Leave it by the office. It’s not going beside the pie.”

Dev moves it a little farther from the table. “Fair.”

The conversation turns to the first opening weekend. Nell starts to mention the rota, then stops when Jo gives her a look over the rim of her glass.

“I only need two availability replies,” Nell says.

“Tomorrow,” Jo says. “I’ve just told the suppliers the opening menu is short. If anybody starts offering me extra dishes while I’m eating, I’ll forget what I agreed to.”

“I’m not offering extra dishes.”

“You were about to ask for a second cake option.”

Bea raises a hand. “That was me. A children’s matinee ought to have cake.”

“It can have cake,” Jo says. “It can’t have six different kinds because your granddaughter sends you opinions in the post.”

“She would choose chocolate sponge,” Bea says. “She’d also say that proves she has taste.”

“She’d be right about the sponge,” Owen says.

Rosa turns her notebook round and sketches a rough block of type in the margin. “The labels will be ready before that. I’m making the headings bigger. Martin says the grey is too faint.”

“It is too faint,” Martin says. “You can see it when you’re holding the paper, but people will be coming in from the street.”

Dev looks over from the far end of the table. “He’s right about that. The foyer lights catch the glossy bits. Make the names darker and I’ll be able to read them from the counter.”

“Fine,” Rosa says. “Darker names. Bigger headings. No more opinions until I’ve finished supper.”

Nell laughs under her breath and reaches for her glass instead of the stack of opening programmes. Outside, a car passes along Harbour Street, its tyres hissing over the damp road. The room settles into the small sounds of a meal: a spoon against the enamel pot, chairs shifting, the front doors giving a faint rattle when the wind catches them.

Martin tells a story about a Saturday afternoon when somebody tried to pay for a ticket with buttons. Bea interrupts before he can decide whether it was 1987 or 1988. Rosa writes down neither year. Owen asks Jo whether there is any salad dressing left; Jo tells him to look in the bowl before treating the question as an emergency. Dev describes a concert venue where every exit sign had been wired to the wrong circuit, and Nell asks just enough questions to work out whether the cinema’s signs need another check.

By the time the foyer clock nears seven, the stew pot is nearly empty and the pie dish holds only crumbs. Jo begins stacking bowls. You carry a small pile through to the kitchen, where she is running hot water into the sink.

“Thank you,” she says, taking the bowls from you. “Leave the cutlery there. Owen’s bringing the plates.”

Owen appears behind you with them balanced against his chest. “I was told I’m allowed to wash up if I don’t make it look like a favour.”

“That’s because you do make it look like a favour,” Jo says.

“I’m carrying plates.”

“Put them down, then.”

From the foyer, Rosa’s notebook closes with a soft thump.

“Martin, blue board first,” she says. “Before you decide it was green.”

“It was blue.”

“Good. We’ve made progress.”

Martin takes his jacket from the back of his chair. Bea gathers her glasses case and follows Rosa towards the auditorium doors.

“Put the boards back against the wall when you finish,” Bea says. “I don’t want anyone catching a foot on them later.”

“We will,” Rosa says.

Dev lifts the receiver case and carries it back upstairs. Nell pauses at the office door, watching the groups break up before she turns to the paper diary on her desk. The foyer is quieter now, with your room still waiting beyond the landing. What does Rowan do?
