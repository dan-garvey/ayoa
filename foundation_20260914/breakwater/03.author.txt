“I can dry for ten minutes, then I’m going upstairs. Where do the clean plates go?”

“You may,” Jo says, sliding open a cupboard above the counter with one damp wrist. “Plates on the middle shelf. Bowls underneath. Mugs live by the kettle. Leave the big pot alone; it needs soaking.”

Owen lowers his stack of plates onto the draining board. “And the cutlery drawer’s sticking again.”

“It isn’t sticking,” Jo says. “You pull it like you’re starting a lawnmower.”

He opens it more carefully. The drawer gives a plain scrape and comes out. “There. Perfectly normal.”

Jo passes you the tea towel. Its blue checks have faded pale at the folds. The first plate is still warm from the stew. You dry it and set it on the middle shelf, then take the next one Owen has rinsed. He keeps the stack manageable, though he tries to add the pie server to it before Jo takes it away and drops it back into the sink.

“Metal on a hot plate,” she says. “Very clever.”

“It wasn’t hot.”

“Don’t begin.”

The kitchen is narrow enough that each cupboard door requires somebody to step aside. Water runs, stops while Jo scrapes crumbs into the bin, then runs again. You put away bowls beneath the plates, mugs beside the kettle, and the clean forks and spoons in the drawer Owen has demonstrated is not sticking.

From the foyer, chairs shift and a notebook closes. Rosa’s voice carries briefly as she and Martin head towards the auditorium, but the words blur once the doors have shut. The rest of the building settles around the work in the kitchen: a footstep on the stairs, the soft clack of something being moved in the office, rain beginning lightly against the back window.

Owen passes over a cup. “How many more cartons of these are there?”

“Three,” Jo says.

“That’s too many cups.”

“It’s a cinema café, not your room above the hardware shop.”

“I’ve got cups.”

“You’ve got two mugs with cracks in them.”

“They’re characterful.”

“They’re leaking.”

He rinses another cup and looks towards the cupboard. “Do you want all of these out before the weekend?”

“Not until I know where people are going to put sugar and tea without asking me every five minutes.” Jo takes a clean serving spoon from you and lays it on a folded cloth. “Tomorrow. That’s tomorrow’s nuisance.”

Owen nods. “Fair.”

The last of the plates comes through the water. Jo tips the pie crumbs from the dish into the bin and leaves the enamel pot in the sink to soak, exactly where she said it would stay. By then the ten minutes have passed. The counter is clear except for the folded tea towel and a few drying drops by the drainer.

“Thank you,” Jo says, looking over the shelves you have filled. “That’s plenty for a first night.”

Owen picks up the empty bowl from the counter. “You’ve made a better start than I did. I got put on onion duty.”

“You were cutting bread,” Jo says.

“I was demoted to bread after onion duty.”

Jo gives him a look and reaches for the tap.

You fold the tea towel over the rail, leave the kitchen to Jo and Owen, and head for the staff staircase upstairs.
