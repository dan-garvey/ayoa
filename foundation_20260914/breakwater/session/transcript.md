**Story**

The room has been made ready for someone staying a little while, and no longer. The narrow bed is freshly made, its striped blanket tucked beneath the mattress. One wardrobe door sits slightly higher than the other. On the desk, the lamp throws a small circle of light over the welcome sheet and the keys Nell handed you: supper at half past six; the staff staircase beside the office; induction at nine tomorrow.

Your luggage takes up most of the clear floor between the bed and desk. Beyond the window, Harbour Street slopes towards the harbour. The evening light catches a parked van's windscreen and the upper panes across the road; farther down, the water has begun to turn the same grey as the sky.

From below comes the clink of plates, then a woman's voice from the kitchen.

“Owen, thinner than that. We need the bread to go round.”

“I am cutting it thinner.”

“The last one was nearly a doorstep.”

“It was a generous slice.”

“Put it on the plate before you start defending it.”

A drawer shuts. Someone laughs softly, close to the kitchen, and the sound of a knife resumes against a board.

At the foyer tables, another woman says, “Martin, when we go in, can you tell me again about the blue board? Slowly this time.”

“I told you. I painted the lettering. Pete changed the showtime.”

“Pete who?”

“Hargreaves. He did late shifts.”

“And you're sure it was him?”

“I didn't say I was sure.”

“You said it as though you were.”

A second woman joins in, amused but firm. “Write down what Martin remembers, Rosa. We can check the programmes before anything goes to print.”

“That's what I'm doing,” the first woman says. “I'm asking before he gets ahead of himself.”

“I don't get ahead of myself,” Martin replies. “I get interrupted.”

Across the landing, a door clicks open. A man calls down the stairwell. “Nell? Can I use the auditorium just after seven?”

The office door opens below. “For what?”

“The hearing receivers arrived early. Hana can help until half seven, and I need somebody moving round the seats while I check the signal.”

“Rosa and Martin have the auditorium from seven to twenty to eight,” Nell says. Her voice is even, but it carries clearly up the stairwell. “It was booked on Friday.”

There is a short pause.

“I didn't know,” the man says. “The cases only came in this afternoon.”

“I need the room quiet,” Rosa calls from the foyer. “The test audio will be on the recordings.”

“I know. I'm not asking you to move.”

“Don't take the receivers down yet,” Nell says. “Call Hana. If she can come earlier, we'll make it work before seven. If she can't, we'll find another evening.”

“All right. I'll ask.”

From the kitchen, Jo calls, “And nobody starts a test while the pie's on the table. It's coming out now.”

The welcome sheet remains beneath the desk lamp. Beyond the room door, the staircase leads down into the warm foyer and the voices of people whose names you have only just begun to place. What does Rowan do?

The staff staircase brings you out beside the office door. The foyer is warmer than the landing and smells of browned pastry, tomatoes, and the raw onion Jo has been cutting for the salad. Two square café tables have been pushed together under the hanging lights. Folded opening programmes sit in a neat stack at one end; unopened cartons of cups wait at the other.

At the kitchen doorway, a woman in a striped apron balances a pie dish against her hip. Beside her, a man holds a plate of bread, some slices noticeably wider than the rest.

“Hello, everyone. I’m Rowan—Nell’s new cataloguer. Something smells good.”

“Martin. Welcome.”

The woman with the pencil and notebook puts both down. “Rosa. And this is Bea.”

Bea lifts a hand from behind her reading glasses. “Hello, Rowan.”

Nell comes out of the office and pulls its door nearly shut behind her. “You’ve met everyone except Dev. He’s in projection with the new receivers.” She looks towards the bowls Jo has begun setting out. “All right, films. Just favourites, though. We don’t need to decide the programme over supper.”

“Partly. It moves quickly.”

Footsteps sound on the staircase behind you. A man in a dark work shirt reaches the bottom with his phone to his ear and a small receiver case tucked beneath one arm.

“No, don’t move the hotel job for it,” he says into the phone. “I thought it might fit, that’s all.” He listens, then says, “All right. Good luck with it,” and ends the call.

Nell looks over. “Hana can’t make it before seven?”

“No. I’ll run the test another evening.” He sets the case carefully beside the office wall, then looks around the table. “Dev. I’m here until Sunday trying to get the sound and the assisted-listening kit ready.”

When Nell explains the question, he says, “Stop Making Sense. Once I know I’m not going to spend the first song looking at a fault light.”

“That seems sensible,” Rosa says.

“The Towering Inferno,” Nell says.

Bea looks up at the repaired ceiling. “Here?”

“Later on,” Nell says. “Not while I’m still collecting invoices from roofers.”

Jo pulls a chair back with her knee. “Sit down, all of you. The stew’s ready.” She looks across at you as the bowls begin to fill. “And you, Rowan? You started it. What would you show first?”

Nell pulls a chair out from the joined tables. “I can see why. No boxes until nine tomorrow.”

Bea takes off her glasses and folds them in one hand. “I first saw it late at night on a television that kept losing the top of the picture. I spent most of the film thinking the staircase was supposed to look like that.”

“The colours will hold here,” Martin says. “On a good projector, the reds don’t go brown at the edges.”

Dev has set the receiver case beside the office wall. “I’d like to hear it after I’ve finished the checks. There are quiet bits under the dialogue that I’ve never caught properly at home.”

Rosa lifts her pencil. “I’m not adding any of that to the exhibition notes. I’ve already got three versions of who painted the blue board.”

“You have one version,” Martin says.

“You gave me Pete, Peter, and ‘the tall one with the cap.’”

“They may all be the same person.”

“Sit down,” Jo says, bringing the pie through from the kitchen. “The pastry’s not waiting for an enquiry into Martin’s memory.”

The chairs scrape across the foyer floor. You take the place Nell has left between Bea and Owen. Jo sets the enamel pot of stew beside the pie, then puts the salad and bread within reach of the people at either end.

“There’s enough for another helping later,” she says. “Start with what’s on your plate.”

“That’s for Dad,” Owen says.

“It’s for anyone who thinks a second piece of bread is a personality trait.”

Martin looks at the bread plate. “I’m having one piece.”

“A sensible start,” Jo says, and begins serving the stew.

It is thick with chickpeas and tomatoes, with lemon and something smoky beneath the sweetness of the onions. The pie flakes apart under the serving knife. You pass the bread when it comes to you and move a bowl along when somebody needs room. Around the table, the talk gathers without waiting for your lead.

Bea tells Rosa that her granddaughter has begun posting films to her with notes tucked into the cases. “She writes which scenes I’m going to complain about. Last week she put a little arrow beside every bit she thought was too loud.”

“Did she get them right?” Rosa asks.

“Most of them. She missed the dog.”

“What was wrong with the dog?” Owen says.

“Nothing was wrong with the dog. It was just in a film that didn’t need a dog.”

Martin smiles into his bowl. “She’s been sending you lists since she was small.”

“She’s more organised than I am,” Bea says. “That’s not difficult.”

Owen takes a piece of pie and nods towards Rosa. “She’s seen The Italian Job with me enough times to know where I start looking at the signs.”

“You paused it at the station entrance last time,” Rosa says. “You wanted to know whether the route numbers had been painted for the film.”

“They looked hand-done.”

“They probably were,” Martin says. “We used to change times on the boards ourselves if a distributor altered something at short notice. You’d be up a ladder with the paint still tacky, trying not to leave a thumbprint beside the last showing.”

Rosa looks across at the boards waiting by the auditorium doors. “That’s why I’m asking whose lettering is where. If Pete changed the time, I want his name in the notes as well as yours.”

Martin’s fork pauses halfway to his mouth. “Pete Hargreaves. He did the late shifts. He had a narrow brush he wouldn’t let anyone borrow.”

“That’s better,” Rosa says, writing it down. “Now I’ve got a name and something I can check.”

Dev reaches for another slice of bread, then glances at the receiver case. “I spoke to Hana. She can’t move the hotel job, so we left the test for another night.”

Nell nods. “After supper I’ll look at the diary for a time when Hana and the room are both free. Don’t arrange anything around a gap until I’ve checked it.”

“I won’t,” Dev says. “I can label the cases and finish the booth work tonight.”

“Good,” Rosa says. “I don’t want to move those boards again before we photograph them.”

Jo points the serving spoon at the case. “Leave it by the office. It’s not going beside the pie.”

Dev moves it a little farther from the table. “Fair.”

The conversation turns to the first opening weekend. Nell starts to mention the rota, then stops when Jo gives her a look over the rim of her glass.

“I only need two availability replies,” Nell says.

“Tomorrow,” Jo says. “I’ve just told the suppliers the opening menu is short. If anybody starts offering me extra dishes while I’m eating, I’ll forget what I agreed to.”

“I’m not offering extra dishes.”

“You were about to ask for a second cake option.”

Bea raises a hand. “That was me. A children’s matinee ought to have cake.”

“It can have cake,” Jo says. “It can’t have six different kinds because your granddaughter sends you opinions in the post.”

“She would choose chocolate sponge,” Bea says. “She’d also say that proves she has taste.”

“She’d be right about the sponge,” Owen says.

Rosa turns her notebook round and sketches a rough block of type in the margin. “The labels will be ready before that. I’m making the headings bigger. Martin says the grey is too faint.”

“It is too faint,” Martin says. “You can see it when you’re holding the paper, but people will be coming in from the street.”

Dev looks over from the far end of the table. “He’s right about that. The foyer lights catch the glossy bits. Make the names darker and I’ll be able to read them from the counter.”

“Fine,” Rosa says. “Darker names. Bigger headings. No more opinions until I’ve finished supper.”

Nell laughs under her breath and reaches for her glass instead of the stack of opening programmes. Outside, a car passes along Harbour Street, its tyres hissing over the damp road. The room settles into the small sounds of a meal: a spoon against the enamel pot, chairs shifting, the front doors giving a faint rattle when the wind catches them.

Martin tells a story about a Saturday afternoon when somebody tried to pay for a ticket with buttons. Bea interrupts before he can decide whether it was 1987 or 1988. Rosa writes down neither year. Owen asks Jo whether there is any salad dressing left; Jo tells him to look in the bowl before treating the question as an emergency. Dev describes a concert venue where every exit sign had been wired to the wrong circuit, and Nell asks just enough questions to work out whether the cinema’s signs need another check.

By the time the foyer clock nears seven, the stew pot is nearly empty and the pie dish holds only crumbs. Jo begins stacking bowls. You carry a small pile through to the kitchen, where she is running hot water into the sink.

“Thank you,” she says, taking the bowls from you. “Leave the cutlery there. Owen’s bringing the plates.”

Owen appears behind you with them balanced against his chest. “I was told I’m allowed to wash up if I don’t make it look like a favour.”

“That’s because you do make it look like a favour,” Jo says.

“I’m carrying plates.”

“Put them down, then.”

From the foyer, Rosa’s notebook closes with a soft thump.

“Martin, blue board first,” she says. “Before you decide it was green.”

“It was blue.”

“Good. We’ve made progress.”

Martin takes his jacket from the back of his chair. Bea gathers her glasses case and follows Rosa towards the auditorium doors.

“Put the boards back against the wall when you finish,” Bea says. “I don’t want anyone catching a foot on them later.”

“We will,” Rosa says.

Dev lifts the receiver case and carries it back upstairs. Nell pauses at the office door, watching the groups break up before she turns to the paper diary on her desk. The foyer is quieter now, with your room still waiting beyond the landing. What does Rowan do?

“I can dry for ten minutes, then I’m going upstairs. Where do the clean plates go?”

“You can,” Jo says. She opens the cupboard above the counter with the side of her wrist, keeping the other hand in the sink. “Dinner plates on the middle shelf. Bowls underneath. Mugs beside the kettle. Leave the big pot where it is; there’s a bit caught on the bottom and it needs to sit in hot water.”

Owen sets the plates beside the drainer and turns on the tap. “I’ll rinse them through.”

“Fine. Just don’t stack them all in the sink at once.”

He picks up the first plate. “The cutlery drawer catches if you pull it too quickly.”

“It catches if you pull it sideways.”

Owen tries the drawer, a little more carefully this time. It opens with a wooden scrape. “All right. Sideways, then.”

Jo takes the blue-checked tea towel from its hook and passes it to you. The first plate is warm beneath the water droplets. You dry it and slide it onto the shelf she indicated; Owen hands over another before the cupboard door has quite swung shut.

The kitchen has room for two people comfortably and three only if nobody turns at the same time. Jo works at the sink while Owen keeps the rinsed dishes coming in a small, orderly line. When he reaches for the pie server, Jo takes it from him and lays it on a folded cloth by the hob.

“That one stays out,” she says. “I’ll wash it with the baking tray.”

“I was only moving it.”

“I know. It can wait.”

From the foyer comes the low drag of a chair, then the auditorium doors closing. The voices beyond them are too muffled to make out. Rain starts lightly against the small back window, and somewhere near the office a sheet of paper is pulled from a printer.

Owen rinses a bowl and glances towards the open kitchen door. “There are still three cartons of cups out there.”

“I saw them arrive,” Jo says.

“I could take two to the store room.”

“Not yet. I want to see what actually fits round the till once I’ve got the tea and sugar out. I ordered them before the counter was finished.” She dries her fingertips on her apron. “They may be too wide for the shelf I had in mind.”

Owen looks at one of the clean mugs in your hand. “They’re decent cups.”

“They’re plain white cups.”

“Plain’s all right.”

“Plain is all right. I just don’t want the first thing people see to be cardboard cartons and a row of cups I’ve put in the wrong place.”

He nods, considering the counter as though it is already in front of him. “If the shelf’s awkward, I could make a narrow rack. There’s enough timber in the store.”

Jo looks over at him. “Not this week. You’ve got your own shifts, and I haven’t measured anything properly. Let me use the counter for a day or two first.”

“All right,” he says. “Tell me when you know.”

“I will.”

You put the bowls beneath the plates and the mugs beside the kettle. The cutlery drawer takes the forks and spoons without complaint once Owen holds it square. Jo empties the last pie crumbs into the bin, then pauses over the remaining wedge.

“Are you taking that home?” she asks Owen.

“If you don’t want it.”

“Fran’s on nights. Take it before it goes dry.”

“I wasn’t planning much for supper,” he says, reaching for the foil.

“There’s a little stew as well, if you want it.”

“I’ll take the pie. The stew can be tomorrow’s lunch.”

Jo gives a short nod and returns to the sink. “Good. I hate throwing food out when people have been here to eat it.”

The last plate comes through the water. You dry it, put it away, then hang the tea towel back over the rail. By the time Jo has left the enamel pot to soak and wiped the counter clear of crumbs, the ten minutes have passed.

“Thank you, Rowan,” she says. “That’s enough for tonight. You’ve got the induction in the morning.”

Owen folds the foil around the pie. “See you then.”

You leave the kitchen to Jo and Owen and head for the staff staircase upstairs.
