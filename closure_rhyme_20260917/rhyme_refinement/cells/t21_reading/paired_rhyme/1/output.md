Seraphel takes the offered chair and sets her slim book beside the lamp.

“Thank you. This quiet will do.”

For a time, it does.

Her book is a collection of travel letters, its pages marked with thin strips of paper. She reads without fidgeting, occasionally making a small note in the margin. Across the library, Professor Stone returns to his brittle papers beneath the magnifying lens.

The Garvey volume proves less ceremonial than its title suggests. Its early pages contain excerpts from old Council records: human seat-holders asking delegations to state a disputed term plainly, recording objections from parties who had not understood a treaty’s consequences, refusing to certify agreements made under obvious pressure. The work sounds less like ruling than insisting that everyone in the room knows what they have agreed to.

Beyond the wall, the card game grows louder. Dena accuses someone of hiding a card in their sleeve; Rashid protests that he has standards; several voices tell him this is not evidence.

Seraphel reaches the end of a letter, glances at the open volume before you, then turns slightly in her chair.

“Would you read one passage aloud?  
I like old records better voiced than browsed.”
