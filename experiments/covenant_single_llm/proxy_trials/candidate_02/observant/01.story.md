The dining hall is warmer than the stairwell, its lamps bright against dark paneling and the long sheen of the varnished table. Seven students have gathered along its nearer half, leaving the upper end and several side places empty. Serving dishes wait beneath silver covers. A cream card stands at one open setting near the middle: GARVEY, in a formal black hand.

At the doorway, you say, “Good evening. I'm Rowan.”

The last speaker—a broad-shouldered young man in a dark, exact-cut coat—finishes his sentence before looking over. Fine fur darkens the backs of his hands where they rest beside his glass. Across from him, the tall woman with swept black-red horns taps the Garvey card once with a blunt nail.

“Your place is marked,” she says. “Ashara vel Kothren.”

“Rashid vel Amara,” says the young man. “We were determining whether assigned seats are protocol or merely an administrative insult.”

“They are both,” says a silver-haired woman farther down the table, without looking up from the place card before her.

Near the hearth, a small-winged woman inclines her head. “Seraphel Dawnquill, / May your first supper here be kind.”

One of the servers lifts a cover at the sideboard; citrus steam unfurls through the room. The chair bearing the Garvey card remains drawn back a hand's breadth from the table.
