Ysolde’s grey eyes come back to you after the cloud has taken the bridge.

“You’re welcome,” she says. “It is easier to understand after you have seen it happen.”

“That is a charitable description,” Rashid says. “The first time, it appears to be a very expensive mistake.”

“You asked whether the south bridge had become ill,” Ashara says.

“I had been here for six hours.”

“It had no fever.”

Ysolde considers this. “It was cold.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is not,” she agrees. “But it was true.”

The answer leaves Rashid with his mouth open for a fraction of a moment before he laughs, the sound low enough not to disturb the other tables of candles and glass. Ashara’s tail gives one quieter tap against the leg of her chair. Across from you, Caelindra lays down her fork.

“I am relieved to learn that the Academy’s bridges are medically sound,” she says. “I had been wondering whether the administration expected us to carry tinctures.”

Seraphel folds her hands around her cup.

“No tonic for a road,
no poultice for the storm.
Only boots, and better sense.”

“The boots are good advice,” Aldric says. “The better sense may take longer.”

“Speak for yourself,” Caelindra says.

“I was.”

That earns him a look from her over the rim of her wineglass: not quite approval, though it has ceased to be boredom. The talk slips on from there, as dinner talk does, catching on small things. Rashid insists that the westward light makes the bridge seem nearer than it is. Ysolde says this is because he judges distance with his feet. Ashara says that is how most people judge it. Ysolde replies that most people are not very good at it.

The next flash of lightning catches the white in Ysolde’s hair and the red-brown polish of the table. Outside, the gardens vanish again beneath the dark. Inside, serving spoons pass from hand to hand. The green sauce thins at the bottom of its dish. Its tarragon bite stays bright against the last pieces of meat and the warm bread.

When the first plates have been mostly cleared, Rashid moves his aside and draws the folded sheet from beneath his napkin. He does not unroll it with the flourish of a speech. He squares it to the edge of the table, uncaps a pen, and reads what he has written with his head bent.

“All right,” he says. “The request asks the faculty to share its findings on the court closure, to give the practice group a date for the inspection, and to consider a supervised replacement hour once the court is cleared for use.”

Ashara extends her hand. “Give it here.”

He passes the paper across. Her fingers are long and strong around it; a little heat lifts from the page into the candlelit air. She reads without hurrying. Aldric watches the sheet rather than her face.

“You have written ‘immediate supervised access,’” Ashara says.

“I thought that had come out.”

“It has not.”

“Then it will.”

“Use ‘a supervised replacement hour.’ You are asking for access when it is safe, not declaring it safe because you have asked.”

Rashid takes the page back and crosses out the word with a single firm line. “Better?”

“Better.”

Aldric leans forward enough to read the line once Rashid has written it. “Ask for the inspection date, not merely an answer. If they say they are reviewing it, they can say that until winter.”

“That is sensible,” Rashid says.

Ashara glances at Aldric. “It is.”

For an instant, Aldric seems almost surprised to have been granted the point. Then he reaches for his water instead of making anything of it.

Thessaly has stopped breaking apart her bread. “Who signs?”

“Anyone in the evening group who wants to,” Rashid says. “No one else needs to put their name beneath it.”

“And the person who was hurt?”

“Doesn’t have to be named. Doesn’t have to sign.”

Thessaly gives a small nod. “That is clearer.”

“It was clear before,” Rashid says, though the annoyance in his voice is aimed more at the paper than at her.

“No,” she says. “It was kinder in your head than it was on the page.”

He looks at the revised sentence for a moment. Then the corner of his mouth draws tight, and he writes another line beneath it.

“All right,” he says. “It is clearer now.”

Caelindra has watched the exchange with an expression of elaborate patience. “An astonishing evening. A petition has survived contact with its own evidence.”

“You may congratulate it once it has survived faculty,” Rashid says.

“I am conserving my enthusiasm.”

“For what?” Ashara asks.

Caelindra turns one palm upward. “A better cause. A tolerable dessert. An instructor who assigns fewer than seven essays on a single treaty.”

“Professor Windwhisper has assigned four,” Aldric says.

“Then she has demonstrated restraint beyond my expectations.”

Seraphel’s smile appears over the edge of her cup.

“Four arguments laid in rows,
seven pages each,
and no one calls it war.”

“They call it education,” Rashid says.

“That is often how war begins,” Caelindra says.

“In a library?” Aldric asks.

“Especially in a library.”

The server returns with a tray of small dishes: dark plums baked until their skins have split, each one set in a pool of pale cream and scattered with crushed nuts. A spoon lands beside each place with a soft, tidy click. For a few minutes, the petition lies facedown again while the room attends to dessert.

Ashara tastes hers and gives the barest inclination of her head toward the kitchen door. “Acceptable.”

“High praise,” Rashid says.

“Do not tell them. They will become careless.”

Ysolde examines a plum as though it has presented a practical problem. “They are from the north garden.”

Caelindra looks up. “How do you know?”

“The skins are thicker.”

“That cannot possibly be enough information.”

“It is enough.”

“For you, perhaps.”

Ysolde eats another bite. “Yes.”

The little exchange turns into an argument over whether the north garden grows anything worth stealing after midnight. Rashid says its pears are too hard. Ashara says this is because he has no patience. Caelindra says the pears have no reason to be patient with him. Thessaly, who has listened to the entire matter with the grave attention of someone considering a contract, says she has never stolen one and will not confirm whether that is because they are hard.

“A wise position,” Aldric says.

“It is an accurate one,” Thessaly replies.

The candles have burned lower by the time the last dishes are taken away. The storm still works below the windows, but the intervals between its flashes have lengthened. Through one of them, the western bridge appears again: a straight white span with its lamps flung eastward in long, pale strokes.

Rashid folds the petition along its new creases and slips it inside his jacket. “I will copy this cleanly before seminar. Ashara, I’ll bring it to you after your bout.”

“Bring it before,” she says. “I will not read it while I am putting on gloves.”

“Before, then.”

“And Aldric,” he adds, looking across the table, “you may decide whether you want your name on it after you have seen the copy.”

“I will,” Aldric says.

No promise is made beyond that. Rashid accepts the answer without trying to improve it.

Thessaly reaches beneath the table and brings up a narrow wooden case, dark with age and rubbed smooth along the corners. She opens it just enough to show a fitted row of blue and ivory tiles.

“The common room has a fire,” she says. “I am taking these there before someone finds an argument that requires a board.”

“What game?” Rashid asks.

“One with rules.”

“That narrows it scarcely at all.”

“It narrows it enough.”

Seraphel rises with her cup in both hands, her small wings shifting behind her shoulders.

“A game, a fire,
and time enough to lose—
I know a good beginning.”

“You say that now,” Caelindra tells her. “Wait until Thessaly explains the scoring.”

“I explain it very well,” Thessaly says.

“That is not the same as making it reasonable.”

Ashara pushes back her chair. “I have an early bout. If anyone wishes to argue about reason, do it quietly enough that the house does not learn the details before breakfast.”

“The house has portraits,” Rashid says. “It already knows everything.”

For the first time that evening, Ashara laughs without seeming to have intended to. It is brief and bright, gone almost as soon as it arrives.

Chairs shift. The long table, which had held everyone in one circle of candlelight, begins to loosen into separate evening purposes: the tiled game in the common room, a folded petition waiting to be copied, an early bout, the weather beyond the glass. The dining hall remains warm after the servers have gone, with the scent of baked plums and tarragon still caught in the air.
