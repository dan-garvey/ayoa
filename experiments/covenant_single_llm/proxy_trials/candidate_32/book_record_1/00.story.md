The cloud takes the western bridge a length at a time. Its pale road disappears first, then the long strokes cast sideways from the lamps, and finally the nearest glow beyond the garden wall. For a moment the window gives back only the dining hall: candle flames doubled in the glass, the dark edges of the table, a server passing behind the chairs with an empty dish balanced on one palm.

“I've never seen anything like that,” you say quietly to Ysolde. “Thank you.”

Ysolde turns from the window. Her white hair has loosened slightly at one temple, though the rest of her remains as composed as the bridge's vanished line. “You're welcome. The first time I saw it from below, I assumed the bridge had shifted.”

Caelindra pauses with her fork midway to her mouth. “And had it?”

“No.”

“How reassuringly brief.”

“It was reassuring,” Ysolde says. “A bridge that moves without notice is a poor bridge.”

Seraphel's wings give a faint rustle against the back of her chair.

“Let bridges stay where bridges are,
or supper takes too long to travel.”

“Supper has already arrived,” Thessaly says. “I think it has won.”

Ashara reaches for the bread basket before Rashid can. “The storm is not yet committed to defeat.” Her tail has curled around one back leg of her chair, the spaded end resting on the dark wood floor.

“Neither are you,” Rashid says.

“Correct.”

He gives a short laugh and tears off a piece of bread. The folded petition is still half hidden beneath his plate, its corner pressed flat by the weight of the china. For a while he leaves it there. The conversation passes through the remaining portions of meat and roots, through a dispute over whether pepper belongs in everything the Academy kitchen prepares, and into the next day's reading.

Caelindra sets down her knife with deliberate care. “The second essay is ninety pages of a person congratulating himself for having located the word *jurisdiction*.”

“It is fifty-six pages,” Aldric says.

“Then it has accomplished ninety pages' worth of self-regard.”

“It explains the voting precedents.”

“A diagram could explain the voting precedents.”

“A diagram could not explain why they were accepted.”

“That is because no one is willing to admit why they were accepted.” Caelindra reaches for the green sauce, then stops with the spoon hovering over it. “And because the author mistakes repetition for rigor.”

“The author has been dead for a hundred and twelve years,” Ysolde says.

“An age at which he ought to have learned restraint.”

Thessaly smiles into her cup. “Can a dead author amend the essay?”

“Not usefully,” Caelindra says.

“Then the assignment has an advantage over Rashid's letter.”

Rashid looks down at the paper. “It has been amended.”

Ashara does not look up from her plate. “No one asked you to begin again.”

“I was not beginning again.”

“You had your hand on it.”

“I was moving it away from the gravy.”

The gravy boat stands nearer to her than to him. A flicker passes along the end of her tail, but she says, “A prudent instinct. Keep it.”

Aldric wipes his fingers on his napkin. “Leave the revised version in the common room after breakfast. I will read it before anyone is asked to sign.”

Rashid studies him for a beat, then nods. “You may mark what you find unsound.”

“I will mark what I find unclear.”

“That is a more diplomatic way to say it.”

“It is also more exact,” Aldric says.

Ashara lifts her gaze at that, not smiling, but not dismissing him either. “Make the request narrow. Ask for the report's conclusion. Ask what conditions would make supervised practice possible. Do not decide the answer before the people responsible have given one.”

“I know.”

“You know it now.”

Rashid's canines show briefly when he presses his mouth closed. Then he unfolds the paper just enough to smooth the crease with one furred thumb. “I know it now,” he says. “If the court itself is unsafe, we ask where the group can meet instead. If it is not, we ask for an hour with someone qualified to watch it. That is the whole of it.”

“That is a request I can consider,” Aldric says.

“Consider it quickly,” Rashid says, though the edge has gone out of the words.

“After I have read it.”

“Of course.”

The paper goes back beneath Rashid's plate. No one reaches for a pen. The matter remains on the table with the salt and the empty gravy boat, less loud than it had been and no more settled.

You take your time with the last of the meal. The herb sauce keeps its bright, grassy bite against the browned meat; the bread is warm enough that steam lifts when it breaks. Around you, names have begun to acquire their own small shapes. Seraphel's short lines arrive when the room has made a place for them. Thessaly listens as if the exact terms of every argument might someday matter, then lets a joke loosen her mouth unexpectedly. Caelindra criticizes the reading list with the concentration of someone who considers bad prose a personal affront. Across from her, Ysolde watches the window whenever the storm thins, as though the sky is providing a lesson with better diagrams.

When a server carries away the last serving dish, another appears with shallow bowls of pears baked in dark syrup and a little white cream melting at the edges. Caelindra examines hers as if suspecting an Academy policy behind it.

“Are you verifying your judgment again?” Ashara asks.

“No. This is an entirely new judgment.”

“How fortunate for the pears,” Seraphel says.

“Pears endure the scholar's gaze.
May we be half so brave.”

Caelindra's mouth twitches despite herself. “You are all determined to make the meal sentimental.”

“It is dessert,” Thessaly says. “It is designed to weaken people.”

Outside, the cloud loosens at its eastern edge. A pale thread of bridge reappears beyond the garden wall, and one by one the lamps make their slanting pools across the storm again. The bowls cool on the table. Rashid's folded page waits beneath his plate for morning, and for a little while the warm room asks nothing more of anyone.
