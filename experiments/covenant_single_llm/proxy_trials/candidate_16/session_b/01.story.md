Under the glass, the ink has browned at its loops and flourishes. The date at the top reads *17 Sunwane, 312 C.R.* The letter beneath it is brief.

> To my great-nephew,
>
> If you are reading this, then the frame has outlived me, which is precisely why I had it hung. I never knew which of you would inherit the room, or whether you would forgive a house with too many stairs.
>
> For the first dinner, take the chair that lets you see the door. Your uncle called that suspicious; he was perpetually surprised by soup.
>
> The fourth stair complains without breaking. The west window opens only if you lift before you pull. The garden gate is worth mending, though I have said that for twenty years.
>
> There should be onion tart. Have some before anyone asks what a Garvey intends.
>
> With love,
>
> Mara Garvey

You put the primer away and leave the room. The fourth stair gives its promised groan beneath you. Downstairs, the common-room fire has sunk to a red bed of coals, while the dining hall beyond it is bright with candles and working lamps. A long table shines under the light; a server in Academy livery is setting down a silver tureen. Seven other young people have gathered around it in loose clusters: white hair against the window's storm-dark glass, silver hair bent over a menu, a pair of small wings catching the candlelight, black eyes turned briefly toward the doorway.

A dark-haired young man with close, fine fur along his wrists folds a sheet of closely written paper and comes around the table. “Rowan Garvey? Rashid vel Amara—you are just in time; we had nearly decided the dinner bell had lost a guest.”

The tall woman beside the nearest chair has ember-red skin and swept black-red horns. “It does not ring for another minute,” she says. “Ashara vel Kothren. Sit before he files a petition about the delay.”

The vacant place beside her has a folded napkin, a shallow bowl, and a view of both the door and the first course waiting under its cover.
