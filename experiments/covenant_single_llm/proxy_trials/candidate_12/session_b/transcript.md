## Opening

**Player**

My character: Rowan Garvey. A twenty-three-year-old human man with brown skin, unruly dark hair, and ink-stained fingers, wearing a carefully mended charcoal coat and the inherited Garvey signet. Newly arrived and unsure of Conclave customs. He grew up unaware of his heritage, lost his parents young, and does not know whether he has any magic.

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

**Story**

On the desk, the Academy primer lies open beside a brass place card: **ROWAN GARVEY**. The lawyers' clothes have been folded into a precise stack on the bed, though the framed letter above the desk has been hung slightly crooked. Its faded hand remains legible behind the glass, addressed to a great-nephew who never came.

Beyond the window, slow lightning walks the storm below the gardens without making a sound in the room. The house itself is less quiet. Plates touch down somewhere on the ground floor. A man's voice, carrying up the stairwell, says, “I said I would ask after dinner.”

“Then ask after dinner,” a woman answers.

“Dinner has begun. This is close enough.”

“Your sense of time is opportunistic.”

A knock comes at your door.

“Rowan Garvey?” The voice on the other side is clear and young. “Seraphel Dawnquill.”

She continues in short, plain lines.

“Your place is set downstairs.
Rashid has brought a petition,
and Ashara has brought objections.
Neither will wait forever.

Would you like a guide
through the first descent?”

The corridor beyond the door smells of candlewax and browned butter. The stairs are to the left, down toward the dining hall and its long table.

## Turn 1

**Player**

"Yes, please." I open the door and go downstairs with Seraphel, greet the table, and take a seat. While I help myself to dinner, I ask her, "There's a letter from a Garvey aunt in my room. Did you ever meet any of my family?"

**Story**

Seraphel steps aside when you open the door. Her white wings are tucked close behind her formal coat, leaving room in the narrow corridor as she leads you toward the stairs.

The dining hall is warmer than the rest of the house. Candles burn between working lamps, and the long table holds more places than people. At the near end, a young man with dark fur along his hands rises and shifts a leather folio away from the chair beside Seraphel's.

“Rashid vel Amara,” he says. “Welcome.”

Across from him, Ashara vel Kothren's horns catch the candlelight. “Sit before he asks you to sign something.”

“After soup,” Rashid says.

You greet the table and take the offered place. Names travel around the candles: Ysolde Thornmantle, very still beside a glass of water; Caelindra Vaeyn, closing the book she had brought to dinner; Thessaly Morrow, who gives a small wave; Aldric Verantus, upright in white and gold. The serving platters pass from hand to hand: roast vegetables, dark bread, bowls of thick soup bright with herbs.

When you help yourself and ask Seraphel whether she ever met anyone in your family, she sets down her spoon.

“No, not in person.
Only from old lessons,
and from watching people
say the name carefully tonight.

What does her letter say?”

**Story**

“No teachers tonight.
But Professor Gareth Stone may.
He teaches human Covenant history,
and he is often in the library

“Stone has been here forever,” Rashid says, tearing a piece of bread in half. “Long enough that every new student assumes he knows where the building keeps its secrets.”

“The building does not keep secrets,” Aldric says. “People do.”

Thessaly tips her head toward him. “Those are the same thing when a building has locks.”

For a moment, Aldric appears to consider objecting. Then he reaches for the soup instead.

Caelindra folds her napkin across her lap. “Professor Stone will either tell you precisely what he knows or decide that your question is tiresome. He is very consistent.”

“That sounds almost reassuring,” Ashara says.

Rashid's folio remains closed by his elbow. “The library stays open after dinner. If he's there, you can find out whether the Garveys were more interesting than their portrait gallery suggests.”

From the hallway beyond the dining room, the house's old clock begins to strike the hour.

## Turn 3

**Player**

After dinner I go upstairs and read the whole letter. Then I go to the library to look for Professor Stone.

**Story**

After dinner, you climb back to your room and take the letter from its frame. The paper is thinner than it looked behind the glass, creased once down the middle and written in a narrow hand.

> To the great-nephew I will not meet,
>
> If you have found your way to Garvey House, eat at the long table before you try to understand the portrait gallery. The dead have had their say already. Ask the living what they want of you, then decide what you can give.
>
> A name may get you invited. It cannot do the rest on your behalf.
>
> With love and every hope,
> Edda Garvey

There is no date, no mention of your parents, and no explanation for why the letter was waiting in the room.

The library is quiet by the time you reach it. Lamps throw pooled light across the open shelves and the iron spiral stair. A man sits alone at a reading table beneath the stair, his papers arranged in careful stacks beside a heavy volume whose leather corners have been repaired more than once.

At the sound of the door, he looks up. His gaze catches on the Garvey signet before returning to your face.

“Professor Gareth Stone,” he says. His voice is low, worn smooth by use. “If you are looking for the restricted cabinet, it will disappoint you. If you are looking for me, that is less difficult.”

## Turn 4

**Player**

"I was looking for you. There's a letter from Edda Garvey in my room. Did you know her?"

**Story**

Stone's hand stills on the edge of his book.

“Edda?” He slides a thin marker between the pages before he looks at you again. “Yes. I knew her.”

He leans back in his chair. “One night she arrived with a stack of Council circulars and announced that they were unreadable. She assigned me the angel parts and performed everyone else herself. Ashworth came in halfway through, listened to three lines, and walked straight out again.”

The corner of Stone's mouth lifts, briefly. “Edda called that a successful reading.”

He looks toward the dark windows, then back to the papers in front of him.

“She was older than she looked, and determined to make herself inconvenient whenever someone told her a thing had always been done a certain way. Your letter is yours. But if you decide you want another pair of eyes on it, bring it here. I would recognize her hand.”

The repaired volume remains open beside him, its pages dense with names and dates. Stone rests one finger on the margin, waiting.
