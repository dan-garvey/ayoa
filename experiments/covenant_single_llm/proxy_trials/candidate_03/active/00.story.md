Three measured knocks sound at the door as the evening lamps along the garden paths begin to glow.

Your room holds the practical order arranged before your arrival: a made bed, a desk beneath a shaded lamp, and a wardrobe whose cedar scent has not yet yielded to the rest of the house. The framed letter over the desk bears the hand of a Garvey aunt who wrote for a great-nephew she never met. Beyond the window, pale paths run between clipped hedges toward the bridges, where slow lightning turns the clouds beneath the plateau a bruised white.

Your charcoal coat sits neatly at the shoulders, its careful mending visible at one cuff when the light catches it. Ink has settled in the creases of your fingers around the old Garvey signet.

From the corridor, a young voice says, "Mr. Garvey? Dinner is being laid. The other first-years are downstairs."

The speaker waits only long enough for the words to carry, then their steps retreat toward the stairwell. Below, conversation rises through the house in separate currents: a low voice, a brighter one, the scrape of a chair across the dining-hall floor. The smell of roasted pears and peppered broth follows it upstairs.
