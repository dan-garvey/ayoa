The framed letter above the desk hangs crooked by the width of a fingernail. Beneath its glass, the opening address—*To my great-nephew, whom I never expected to know*—has browned more deeply than the rest of the page. Clothes sent ahead by people who know your measurements only from paperwork sit in careful stacks beside it. Ink darkens your fingers against the cuff of your carefully mended charcoal coat, and the Garvey signet catches the lamplight whenever your hand shifts.

The window looks over gardens already going blue at the edges. Pale paths wind between flowering walls; beyond them, a hard-light bridge holds a steady white line over the roiling cloudbank far below. The storm gives no thunder here. It only flashes now and then under the horizon, as if the world has remembered something it means to say later.

From downstairs comes the useful, ordinary noise of a house preparing to feed people: chair legs eased across old boards, a server calling for more bread, a burst of laughter cut off by a voice saying something too low to make out. Someone has opened the doors between the common room and the dining hall. Butter, pepper, and roast onions climb the stairwell with the warmth from the lamps.

A light knock sounds against the doorframe. A server in blue-and-silver Academy livery stands at the threshold, empty-handed for the moment.

“Mr. Garvey,” he says, “we are seating in a few minutes. The dining hall is down the main stair, through the common room. If you would like, I can show you the way.”
