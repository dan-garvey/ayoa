Rashid does not answer immediately. The next few days pass through the familiar rooms of the Academy—seminar tables, library lamps, the gardens’ sheltered paths—until he finds you on the stair outside the common room with a folded attendance slate in one hand.

“Everyone I asked is happy for you to watch. The first supervised session is tomorrow after the last bell.”

The east court is a narrow practice yard behind the Military halls, open to the wind on one side and marked in fresh white chalk. A faculty supervisor checks the padded blades, staffs, and floor anchors before anyone begins. You stay beyond the line with the few other observers while Rashid joins eight students inside it.

The session starts with balance work and measured footwork instead of sparring. Dena Arl is among the group, her splinted wrist held close beneath the cuff of her Commerce jacket as she works the turns one-handed with a shorter student. Rashid moves between pairs when asked, then takes a staff himself when the supervisor calls for the first controlled exchanges.

Nothing in the practice is dramatic. Wooden weapons meet and stop on clear touches; partners reset their footing; the supervisor interrupts twice to correct distance and once to replace a worn grip. By the final quarter-hour, the careful rhythm has become a roomful of people breathing hard, laughing at misses, and asking for another attempt before the bell ends the session.

The equipment goes back onto the rack, attendance is recorded, and the students leave the east court in small knots of conversation. The first trial session is over.
