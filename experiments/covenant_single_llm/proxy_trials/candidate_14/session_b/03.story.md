Dinner runs on after the first disagreement has lost its sharpest edge. Rashid keeps his petition beside his plate, speaking with Thessaly in low tones when she returns it; Aldric eventually excuses himself toward the chapel. Ashara remains at the table long enough to finish her wine, while Caelindra's book reappears at her elbow between courses. By the time the last plates are cleared, the garden beyond the windows has gone dark except for the lamps along its paths.

Upstairs, the frame comes down from the wall with a faint dry rasp of brass hook against plaster. Its backing is held by small bent tabs. When they are eased aside, the painted board lifts free to reveal a sheet of cream paper tucked flat behind it, folded twice and softened at the creases.

The handwriting is the same brown, sloping hand visible through the frame.

*My dear great-nephew,*

*If you have found this, then our long line of improbabilities has produced you at last. I had it framed because a letter in a drawer is treated as property; a letter on a wall becomes decoration, which is how important things are often kept safe.*

*The house is yours in the precise sense that its roof and debts are yours. The people who eat beneath it are not. Give them room, food if it is wanted, and the right to decline you. The Council has suffered enough from people mistaking obligation for affection.*

*You will hear the Garveys praised for being useful. Be useful only when you mean to be; usefulness becomes a leash when someone else fastens it for you.*

*Keep notes in your own hand: dates, names, exact words. Ask who has seen the original. But do not let every kindness become a clue. It is possible for a person to want nothing from you except your company, and I hope you find such people.*

*If a day at the Academy feels too large, come home to dinner. The house should be good for that much.*

*Your affectionate great-aunt,*

*Miriam Garvey*

The paper settles back into its hiding place without resistance. Once the frame is restored, Miriam's first visible line returns to its old place above the bed, giving nothing away.

The library is quieter than the dining hall, with lamp-oil air and the occasional soft tick of cooling metal from the spiral stair. Most of its tables are empty. At one near the ground-floor windows, a human man with iron-grey hair sits beneath a green-shaded lamp, reading a wide volume whose margins are crowded with smaller, neater handwriting. A plain cup of tea has gone cold at his elbow.

He looks up as you approach. His eyes find the signet, then your face. After a moment, he closes the book over one marked page.

“Mr. Garvey,” Professor Gareth Stone says. “What are you looking for?”
