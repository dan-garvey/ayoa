Stone's expression changes at the mention of your parents, the almost-smile leaving it without quite becoming solemnity. He draws his cold cup closer, more for something to do with his hands than for the tea.

“She did, though she wrote about them more freely than she spoke,” he says. “I met them once, here, when you were still small enough to sleep through a library. Miriam said they wanted you to have an ordinary childhood before the Conclave was allowed to claim any part of you.”

It had been a wet afternoon, Stone explains, and the two of them arrived with a small leather box of water-damaged household papers. One spent most of the visit asking whether the maps inside could be dried without losing the pencil marks; the other found a children's history left on a nearby chair and began correcting its captions in the margin with an ordinary graphite pencil. Miriam had been exasperated by both of them and plainly pleased to have them near.

From her letters, he knew more of their daily life than of their plans. She wrote that one of them repaired things long after anyone sensible would have replaced them: hinges, clocks, a kettle that whistled at the wrong pitch. The other kept names in a pocket notebook—neighbors, teachers, the children who came to the door for lessons—because forgetting a name was, in Miriam's view, a small failure of manners. When she described the two together, she tended to make them sound busy, untidy, and capable of turning a modest supper into an occasion.

Stone rests a finger on the book's worn cover. “That is not the whole of them. It is only what I saw, and what Miriam chose to tell me.”

Beyond the green-shaded lamp, the library shelves rise into shadow. The book he had been reading remains closed between you, its marker ribbon caught at the edge of the pages.
