You mark *Magical Literacy and Safe Practice*, then *Treaty Languages and Records*. The choices are not due yet, so the sheet goes back into your primer instead of into an official box.

The afternoon library introduction starts beneath the iron stair. The Academy librarian has grey hair pinned in a coil and a brisk way of moving that keeps the first-years close behind her. She points out the lending desk and the open stacks on the ground floor, then the journals and maps above. A lit lamp reserves a study table until midnight.

At the locked cabinet of Covenant histories, she gives the brass handle a glance. “Chancellor's signature.” Then she leads the group on.

A shelf of ward manuals carries a card: *No casting required for consultation*. The librarian opens one book and shows where its catalogue entry says whether it assumes a reader can work magic. For a damaged or incomplete source, she points out the hazard notice at the front.

At the records desks, she lays out three versions of the same trade compact: the formal treaty script, a merchant's working translation, and a copy thick with decades of marginal corrections. The last looks like a nest of faded ink until she directs everyone to the names crossed through at the edge of a page.

Caelindra stays over the documents when the rest of the group starts along the next aisle. “The merchant copy leaves out the amendments,” she says, one finger hovering over a struck-through name. “And the people who pressed for them.”

“That is why the copies are kept together,” the librarian says. “Use the working translation if you need it, but compare it before you rely on it.”

Ysolde has stopped before a weather map of the Nexus, following old storm lines with one pale finger. By the time the introduction ends, the way back to Garvey House has gathered landmarks: the garden gate with its chipped stone bird, the low arch with a dragon's jaw worn smooth by passing hands, and the narrow turn where the house's dark roof comes into view.

Dinner is already under way when you return. It is less formal than the welcome meal: books and folded course sheets lie beside the water glasses, and lentils, roasted squash, and flatbread fill the middle of the table. Ashara and Aldric sit at one end; Caelindra has a book propped beside her plate, while Ysolde watches the lamp-light catch in her water. Seraphel and Thessaly are sharing the bread basket. Rashid has a narrow receipt tucked beneath his plate.

After the first course, he draws it out. “The petition has been logged. The Dean of Student Safety's office says there will be a written response by tomorrow evening.”

“To all three requests?” Ashara asks.

“Access periods, the incident findings, and the date and criteria for review. That is how the clerk entered it.”

Ashara holds out her hand. Rashid passes her the receipt; she reads it, then gives it back. “Keep this. If their reply leaves out a piece, take both pages to the same clerk.”

“A written response will show whether they have addressed the petition as filed,” Aldric says.

“That is the hope,” Rashid says. He folds the receipt once and puts it away.

Seraphel traces the rim of her glass.

“Let them write the rule.
The door is already closed.”

“And the review date,” Thessaly says, reaching for more flatbread. “Otherwise it is a closure with a seal on it.”

No one carries the court further that night. The course sheets take its place. Ashara reads one of the rhetoric descriptions twice.

“This studio promises a mutually useful outcome to every disagreement,” she says. “I would like to see what it calls useful.”

Caelindra glances toward the library. “Give me Records. That compact had amendments missing from its merchant copy. At least there is something there to find.”

The lamps in the common room are lit by the time dinner ends, and the library door stands ajar beyond them.

Professor Stone has cleared the chair beside his desk. The brass frame rests on the blotter, its glass freshly wiped, beside an open household index and a copied page in his compact hand. At your greeting, he looks up.

“Good evening, Rowan. Sit. I found names, and dates that hold together better than I expected.”

He turns the copied page toward you. It is a narrow sequence of entries joined by lines.

“Lenora's younger brother was Tomas Garvey. Tomas's eldest son, Jude, was your grandfather. That is the branch the inheritance follows.” He taps the next entry. “Willan was the son of Elspeth, Lenora's niece, from another branch. A distant cousin.”

The index gives Willan a city address and, in smaller writing, an occupation: junior clerk in a municipal records office. A forwarding address follows it. Beneath that, the entry stops.

“The household papers do not tell us whether he missed the visit at all, why he might have done so, or what became of the blue folio,” Stone says. “If you decide that branch is worth tracing, I can request the municipal ledger through ordinary channels. It will take time. It may only give us another address.”

He closes the index and adjusts the frame square with the desk.

“As for the house, the Garvey trust placed it in Academy use long before you arrived. Furniture and papers have come from several households over the years. This letter was in your room last term. It is a family thing, but it was not left there for you in particular.”

Stone puts the copied page beside the frame. “Keep both. Lenora appears to have worried about pears, rain, and missing papers. The letter is clear on that much.”

Beyond the tall windows, the storm leaves the distant bridges as pale strokes against the dark. Stone's lamp keeps its small circle over the index and the family names.
