You make two small marks on the course sheet: one beside *Magical Literacy and Safe Practice*, one beside *Treaty Languages and Records*. The choices are not due yet, and the paper goes back into the primer with the others rather than into any official box.

The afternoon introduction begins beneath the library's iron stair. An Academy librarian with grey hair pinned in a coil takes the first-years through the open stacks in a brisk circuit: lending desks on the ground floor, journals and maps above, study tables that may be claimed until midnight if their lamps are lit. She pauses at the locked cabinet of Covenant histories only long enough to explain the chancellor's signature requirement, then moves on before anyone can turn a rule into a conversation.

The useful parts are plainer. A shelf of ward manuals bears a card reading *No casting required for consultation*. The librarian demonstrates how to check whether a text assumes a reader can work magic, and where to find the hazard notices that accompany damaged or incomplete sources. At the records desks, she lays out three copies of the same trade compact: one in formal treaty script, one in a merchant's working translation, and one with decades of marginal corrections. The last is nearly illegible at first glance and much more revealing once she points out whose names have been crossed through.

Caelindra lingers over it after the group has begun to move on. “A translation that conceals every amendment is not a translation,” she says, with the faint satisfaction of having found something to criticize accurately.

“It is often the version people prefer to circulate,” the librarian replies.

Ysolde spends several quiet minutes in front of a weather map of the Nexus, following old storm lines with one pale finger. By the time the introduction ends, the route back to Garvey House has acquired landmarks of its own: the garden gate with its chipped stone bird, the low arch where the carved dragon's jaw has been rubbed smooth by passing hands, the narrow turn that brings the house's dark roof into view.

Dinner is already under way when you return. The long table is less ceremonial than it was last night; books and folded course sheets have appeared beside the water glasses, and a serving dish of lentils, roasted squash, and flatbread occupies the middle of the table. Rashid has a narrow receipt tucked beneath his plate.

After the first course, he draws it out. “The petition has been logged. The Dean of Student Safety's office says there will be a written response by tomorrow evening.”

Ashara breaks a piece of bread between her hands. “A response to what, exactly? The access request, the incident report, or the request for a date?”

“All three,” Rashid says. “That is what the clerk entered.”

“Then keep the receipt,” Ashara tells him. “Clerks forget less readily when paper remembers for them.”

Aldric glances from the receipt to Rashid. “A written reply is reasonable. It means the Academy will have to give a reason for whatever it decides.”

“That is the hope,” Rashid says. He folds the receipt once and puts it away.

Seraphel traces a little circle in the condensation on her glass.

“Let the answer name the rule,
and not merely the locked door.”

“If it names neither,” Thessaly says, reaching for another piece of flatbread, “then it is not an answer.”

The discussion leaves the court there. Later, course sheets draw the table into smaller, less settled opinions: Ashara objects to a rhetoric seminar's claim that every disagreement has a mutually useful outcome; Caelindra maintains that most records are made difficult by people who benefit from being misunderstood. The meal ends without anyone settling either proposition. By then the lamps in the common room are on, and the library door stands ajar beyond them.

Professor Stone has cleared one of the chairs beside his desk. The brass frame waits on the blotter, its glass freshly wiped, next to an open household index and a single copied page in his compact hand. At your greeting, he looks up from the book.

“Good evening, Rowan. I have names and better dates. That is more than I expected to have by supper.”

He indicates the chair, then turns the copied page so it faces you. It is not a grand family tree, only a narrow sequence of entries with lines drawn between them.

“Lenora's younger brother was Tomas Garvey,” he says. “Tomas's eldest son, Jude, was your grandfather. That is the branch the inheritance follows. Willan was the son of Lenora's niece, Elspeth, from another branch of the family. Distant cousins, then, but close enough that Lenora expected him to bring papers when he visited.”

The index gives Willan a city address and an occupation noted in smaller writing: junior clerk in a municipal records office. Beside it is a last forwarding address, then nothing. Stone taps the blank space beneath the entry with one finger.

“The household papers stop there. They do not say why he missed the visit, whether he missed it at all, or what became of the blue folio. I can ask for the municipal ledger through ordinary channels if you later decide the distant branch matters to you. It will take time, and it may only produce another address.”

He closes the index gently. “As for the house: it bears your family name because the Garvey trust placed it in Academy use long before you arrived. The furniture and papers came from several households over the years. That letter was in your room last term as well. It is a family thing, but it was not left there for you in particular.”

Stone lifts the frame and holds it out by its sides. “You may keep this, and the copy. Lenora appears to have been exactly the sort of aunt who worried about fruit, weather, and neglected documents. There are worse people to find in a line.”

Beyond the tall library windows, the storm turns the distant bridges into pale strokes against the dark. Stone's lamp holds a small, steady circle over the index, the returned letter, and the names that now have places beside one another.
