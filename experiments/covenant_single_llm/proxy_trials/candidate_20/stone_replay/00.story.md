At the end of Ashworth's address, you mark *Magical Literacy and Safe Practice* for consideration, then *Treaty Languages and Records*. The course sheet joins the others at the center of the table while the chancellor moves on to questions about timetables and meal hours.

The afternoon introduction begins at the library's ground-floor desk. A librarian in Academy blue slides open a long cabinet of catalog drawers and has the first-years pull sample slips, then follows them through the public shelves and up the iron stair. When Thessaly pauses before the locked history cabinet, the librarian says, “That collection needs the chancellor's signature. A request is not a signature.”

“Then I will not mistake one for the other,” Thessaly says, and moves on to a shelf of game manuals.

There is enough time afterward to trace the route back at a walk: past the oval seminar door, beneath the low carved arch, and through gardens smelling of wet stone and late flowers. By dinner, the way no longer requires the primer held open in front of you.

The meal is quieter than the welcome supper. Rashid arrives with the petition folded in his pocket and a fresh crease of fatigue at one cuff. Before the servers clear the last plates, he tells Ashara and Aldric, “I have twelve names. I am taking it to Student Affairs in the morning.”

“Then make them answer the request you made,” Ashara says. “Not the one they would prefer to dismiss.”

Aldric nods once. “And if their report gives a reason for the closure, read it before you decide they have none.”

Rashid looks from one to the other. “That is the plan.” He does not reopen the argument.

By the time you reach the library, the lamps along its upper gallery have been lowered. Stone's desk is clear except for the brass frame, his leather notebook, and a slim grey folder held shut with a faded blue ribbon. He looks up when you enter.

“Good evening, Rowan. Sit down.” He indicates the chair beside the desk. “I have been looking forward to giving you an answer with fewer shadows than yesterday's.”

The notebook lies open to a page of careful names and dates. Stone turns it so you can read the branching lines without having to lean across his papers.

“Lenora's younger brother was your grandfather's father. That is the connection. Willan was the son of one of Lenora's nieces, from a branch that had settled in the city.” His finger rests beside a smaller notation. “The household index led me to an account book. It records that Willan missed the visit, Lenora sent for the blue folio the following week, and he wrote an apology after. There is no buried calamity in it. He was late, she was annoyed, and apparently the pears went to a neighbor.”

Stone's mouth tightens briefly, as though he is deciding whether the story deserves a smile. “That may be an ordinary answer, but it is a true one.” He loosens the blue ribbon and sets the folder within reach. Its label reads: *Lenora Garvey, household book and correspondence, selected copies.*

“There are a few more letters here,” he says. “Enough, perhaps, to let her be a person rather than a name in your inheritance papers.”
