The tile with the shallow curve gives the nearest gate a clean approach. You fit it against the center square, set a blue lantern on the left entry circle, and move it two marks along the new road.

Thessaly studies the join where the paint meets. “Legal,” she says. “And annoyingly direct.”

Her next tile sends her amber lantern away from the moon gate and toward the opposite side of the board. It looks like a waste until the road branches around the space you have just claimed. She moves two marks, then takes an apricot.

“Most people see the gates first,” she says. “The roads decide which ones remain useful.”

The first blue lantern reaches its gate on your next turn. Thessaly lifts it from the board and places it back in your hand. “Returned. It may enter again later, but it counts as one finished.”

The map spreads over the tablecloth. Roads curl around each other, meet at sharp little junctions, and leave two gates stranded behind lanes neither of you wants. Thessaly plays quickly, though she pauses to make sure the edges meet before she lets go of a tile. When you start a second blue lantern and try to take it through a crowded crossing, she puts a fingertip beside the amber piece in its way.

“Not through that one. You may stop before it, or choose the other entry circle.”

You use the other circle. A few turns later, she catches a different mistake when a blue lantern would take a third marked space.

“Two,” she says. “You have already spent both.”

“That explains why the game has roads.”

“It explains why the rules card is inadequate.” Her black eyes narrow at the card as if it has personally disappointed her. “The person who wrote it assumed that everyone arrives knowing what counts as a space.”

By then one of her amber lanterns has slipped around the far side of the board and reached the sun gate. She returns it to her hand, starts another at the center, and lays a tile that closes the shortest path to the gate you had been eyeing. There is still a route around it, only longer.

“That was deliberate,” Thessaly says, before you can mistake the placement for bad luck.

The game goes on under the lamp while the apricots slowly disappear. Your blue lanterns take the open routes as they appear; hers make better use of the turns when the map forces a choice. Near the end, you return a third blue lantern at the same moment her last amber piece is two spaces from the moon gate.

She draws a straight road, checks its edges, and sets it down. The amber lantern has an unobstructed path. Two careful marks later, it reaches the painted moon.

Thessaly gathers the four amber lanterns into her palm. “That is the game.” She looks over the crowded board, then begins lifting the road tiles one at a time. “You learned it quickly. Next time, do not let me have both long sides of the map.”
