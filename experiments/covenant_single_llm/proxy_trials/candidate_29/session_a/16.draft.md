The second bell is still sounding across the courts when you reach the octagon. Morning practice has left a pale scuff of dust over the stone. Ashara stands beside the south weapons rack with two blunted ash blades resting against her boot, her sleeves pushed above her wrists.

She looks at the card disappearing into your coat pocket, then at you.

“Flat both times. I still want to learn the blade.”

Ashara gives one short nod. “You made it here before the bell finished. Take the shorter one.”

The practice blade is smooth with use, its rounded edge darkened where many hands have held it. Ashara takes the other and steps out onto the marked stone.

“Use the hand you write with below the guard. The other hand stays at the end. Don’t strangle it.” She waits while you settle your grip, then comes close enough to turn the blade a fraction between your hands. “There. If your knuckles go white, I’ll make you start again.”

She sets her own blade low, its point aimed past your hip rather than at you. “One foot forward. Not a lunge. Just far enough that you could leave it without crossing your legs.”

For the first part of the lesson, she has you move along one of the pale octagonal lines: forward, back, then off it to either side. When your shoulders rise, she catches the flat of her blade against them with a quiet tap.

“You’re carrying the weapon up here,” she says. “It’s down there.” She points at your feet with her chin. “Move those first.”

After a few passes, she takes a square leather target from the rack and holds it at chest height.

“Cut from above. Stop when the edge would meet it. I don’t care how hard you can hit leather.”

The blade makes a dull wooden sound against the target. Ashara shifts it an inch to one side for the next stroke, then lower. Each time, she corrects the line with two fingers against your wrist or a word before you begin: “Shorter. Again. Don’t pull the point behind you.”

When she puts the target away, the work changes. She approaches in guard and lets you see the distance between your blades.

“Watch my shoulders,” she says. “The blade will lie to you if it can.”

Her first motion is slow enough to follow: a step, a turn of the hips, the ash blade traveling toward your upper arm. She stops a handspan away. On the next pass she asks less of the movement, and her blade reaches your sleeve before she draws it back.

“That would be a mark. You gave me your arm when you tried to meet the blade.”

She shows the same attack again, this time leaving the opening for your practice blade to turn hers aside. The contact jars lightly through the handle. She does it once more at a quicker pace, then steps clear.

“Better. You don’t need to win the blade. You need it out of the way.”

Half an hour passes in the repetition: the marked line beneath your feet, the dry click of ash meeting ash, Ashara’s voice cutting through the open air whenever your stance loosens. At last she lowers her weapon and reaches for the practice blade.

“That’s enough for today,” she says, taking it by the guard. “You reset your feet before you chase the point. Keep that part.”

She returns both blades to the rack, their wooden edges knocking softly together.
