Published fiction after two sequential requests to the same author. Each NN.draft.md and NN.editor_request.txt is preserved separately; drafts are not player-visible turns.

## Opening

**Player**

My character: Rowan Garvey. A twenty-three-year-old human man with brown skin, unruly dark hair, and ink-stained fingers, wearing a carefully mended charcoal coat and the inherited Garvey signet. Newly arrived and unsure of Conclave customs. He grew up unaware of his heritage, lost his parents young, and does not know whether he has any magic.

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

**Story**

The window's latch is cold, though the storm beneath the Nexus never makes a sound.

Three hundred feet below Garvey House, lightning opens and closes inside a violet-black country of cloud. The gardens lie between the house and that drop: pale paths, flowering hedges held close by low walls, lamps beginning to pearl in the dusk. Beyond them, one hard-light bridge runs straight into the weather, white and unwavering until the storm takes it.

Your room occupies a quiet corner of the second floor. The bed is tightly made. The clothes sent ahead by the lawyers wait in the cedar wardrobe in careful folded ranks. A washbasin gleams on its stand. Your charcoal coat carries its neat repairs at cuff and elbow; the Garvey signet at your hand catches a pulse of distant lightning. Ink darkens the creases of your fingers.

An Academy primer rests beside the green-shaded desk lamp. Its contents promise introductions to the Conclave, dinner customs, bridge permissions, and Article Nineteen in the same dry typeface. Beside it stands a framed handwritten letter. The visible lines are addressed to a great-nephew whose name is not yours and concern an upstairs room, fresh sheets, and a visit expected before winter. Whatever became of that visit, the letter has been kept behind glass.

The door is ajar onto a corridor of dark floorboards. At its far end, warm light from the stairwell falls across the runner. From below come the sounds of dinner being arranged: a serving spoon against china, the low rush of a fire in the common room, a chair dragged an inch and set straight again.

Then voices rise through the house.

“Rashid, if you want to ask for access, ask for access,” says a young man with a clear, measured voice. “Do not turn an injury into a referendum before anyone knows what caused it.”

“The gate has been shut since this afternoon,” Rashid answers. His restraint gives each word a hard edge. “No report has to be complete for that to be true.”

“You have a point about the court,” a woman says. “You do not therefore have a petition.”

“That is an arbitrary distinction.”

“No.” The answer arrives without heat. “I can tell when a blade will take off a thumb. I cannot tell why someone was hurt when I was not there.”

“Ashara is right,” the first man says. “We should know whether the restriction is sensible before we demand it be lifted.”

“I did not say lifted.” Rashid lets out a breath through his nose. “I said supervised access for the group. A time. An instructor. Something other than telling people to acquire private tutors.”

Another woman joins them, her voice light and unhurried. “I will sign a specific request. I will not sign a mood.”

“Fine,” Rashid says after a beat. “Supervised access. Tomorrow, after the report.”

“Bring the wording,” Ashara says. “And leave my dawn booking out of it.”

“I was not going to steal your booking.”

“You have asked me three times.”

“Twice.”

“Then I was charitable.”

The chair legs downstairs scrape again. A different woman speaks in brief, plain lines.

“Arguments improve in daylight.
Stew improves before it dies.”

The silence that follows is interrupted by a short laugh from Rashid.

“Tomorrow,” he says.

“Tomorrow,” says Ashara.

The conversation loosens. Someone asks whether the bread has arrived. Someone else replies that it has, and that it is being guarded from desperate hands. The scent reaching the stairwell is roasted herbs, warm bread, and something sharp with citrus.

Footsteps climb to the landing. An Academy server in blue-grey livery pauses at your open door with a covered dish balanced on one hand. She knocks once against the jamb and inclines her head.

“Mr. Garvey,” she says, “dinner is served.”

**Story**

The primer closes beneath your hand with a soft papery clap. The server accepts your thanks with a practiced nod, then leads the way down the stairwell, where firelight from the common room warms the old wood underfoot.

The formal dining hall opens off it in a spill of candlelight. Seventeen places run the length of the varnished table, most of them empty tonight; the occupied settings cluster near the middle among water carafes, bread baskets, and low bowls of citrus. Paneled walls hold the lamplight in amber squares. Servers move quietly between the sideboard and the table with covered dishes.

The conversation at the near end stops when you reach the doorway.

“Rowan Garvey,” you say. “Thank you for having me.”

The tall woman nearest the open place shifts a water carafe clear of it. Her swept-back horns are black at the base and dark red at their tips; a long tail rests in a deliberate curve beside her chair. “Ashara vel Kothren,” she says. “Welcome.”

Across from her, the well-dressed young man with dark fur along his hands lifts his cup. “Rashid vel Amara. We were meant to wait until you sat down before disagreeing.”

“Aldric Verantus,” says the golden-haired man beside him.

At the far side of the table, a pale woman with white hair and grey eyes gives you an exact nod. “Ysolde Thornmantle.”

“Caelindra Vaeyn.” The silver-haired woman says it without rising, violet eyes steady on you.

The smallest person at the table has blue-black hair drifting slowly around her shoulders and eyes so black the candlelight finds no color in them. “Thessaly Morrow,” she says. “The stew has no terms.”

Beside her, the warm-gold angel's small white wings are folded close against the back of her chair. She smiles.

“Seraphel Dawnquill.
Welcome to dinner.”

The server indicates the vacant setting between Ashara and Ysolde. You take it and settle your napkin across your lap. A bowl is placed before you, steam carrying rosemary, pepper, and the sharp citrus scent from the stairwell.

“I've heard enough about the court to know I shouldn't sign anything on my first evening,” you say. “I'd be glad of some stew, though.”

Rashid looks down at the rim of his bowl for a moment. “I did not intend to ask you. I asked the people who train with the group.” He reaches for the water, filling his own cup before returning the carafe to its place. “The request can wait until tomorrow.”

Ashara tears off a piece of bread. “Good.”

The first spoonful of stew is set down for each of you. Ysolde holds her hand over the rising steam, then lets it fall to the tablecloth.

“The fennel is stronger tonight,” she says.

“Not as strong as last week,” Caelindra replies. “The kitchen has relented.”

Seraphel lowers her eyes to her bowl.

“Last week the cook called it medicine.”

“It was,” says Caelindra.

Ysolde tastes the broth. “It was not unpleasant.”

Ashara slides the bread basket toward the space beside your bowl. “Take some before Rashid decides it belongs to his campaign.”

Rashid cuts a slice from the loaf without looking up. Around the table, spoons begin to move, and the argument remains where it was left: in the morning, with the report.

**Story**

You take a piece of bread from the basket. Its crust gives with a quiet crack beneath your fingers.

“Fair enough, Rashid. I mistook a loud conversation for an invitation.”

“Fair,” Rashid says. “I asked the people who train with the group. You are not one of them.” He returns to his stew.

The broth is thick with lentils, onion, and fennel, the rosemary arriving after the pepper. You try it, then turn to Ashara.

“Do you compete for the Academy, or is the dawn booking for your own training?”

Ashara has folded one sleeve above her wrist to keep it clear of the bowl. She looks at you across the candle flame.

“The Academy keeps rankings, not teams. There are officials, rules, and a board outside the dueling ground. I do not fight in its colors.” She tears off a piece of bread. “Dawn is mine. I have a ranked bout coming, and I need the covered court for footwork. If I miss a turn there, I know it was my fault.”

“First,” Rashid says, reaching for the bread, “this week.”

Ashara glances at him. “Rankings move.”

“They do.”

Ysolde lifts the lemon from her bowl with her spoon and sets it aside. Caelindra waves away the same garnish when a server offers it from a silver dish.

Ashara takes another piece of bread. “Have you ever watched a ranked bout?”

**Story**

“Never,” you tell Ashara. “I'd like to see one.” You set your spoon down. “If the covered court is closed, how does your dawn booking work?”

“The covered court is not closed to everything.” Ashara rests her forearms on the edge of the table. “The group hour was cancelled. My individual slot was entered separately, before the injury, and it remains.”

Rashid breaks his bread in two. “That is the distinction.”

“Yes,” Ashara says. “It is a bad one. It is still my session.”

She reaches for her spoon, then looks back to you.

“Ranked bouts are outside, in the octagon. The board posts the time and the names. Come to one of those if you want to watch. Do not come to dawn; practice is work, not a performance.”

“The south rail is better,” Ysolde says from beside you. “At noon, the north side catches the light. You will miss the footwork.”

Ashara gives her a brief nod. “She is right about that.”

Seraphel folds a scrap of bread between her fingers.

“Then the south rail,
and clear light.”

Rashid takes the smaller half of his bread for himself. The court is left for morning, and the stew begins to cool.
