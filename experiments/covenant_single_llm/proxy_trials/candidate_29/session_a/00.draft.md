The window's latch is cold, though the storm beneath the Nexus never makes a sound.

Three hundred feet below Garvey House, lightning opens and closes inside a violet-black country of cloud. The gardens lie between the house and that drop: pale paths, flowering hedges held close by low walls, lamps beginning to pearl in the dusk. Beyond them, one hard-light bridge runs straight into the weather, white and unwavering until the storm takes it.

Your room occupies a quiet corner of the second floor. It has been made ready with the thoroughness of people who know how to prepare a room and nothing at all about the person arriving in it. The bed is tightly made. The clothes sent ahead by the lawyers wait in the cedar wardrobe in careful folded ranks. A washbasin gleams on its stand. Your own charcoal coat carries its neat repairs at cuff and elbow; the Garvey signet at your hand catches a pulse of distant lightning. Ink remains in the creases of your fingers despite the basin.

On the desk, an Academy primer rests beside a lamp with a green glass shade. Its table of contents gives equal weight to the welcome dinner, bridge permissions, and Article Nineteen, as if all three are matters of sensible household order. The framed letter propped beside it belongs to a different kind of order. The handwriting is narrow and brown with age. It is addressed to a named great-nephew, not to you, and speaks in the visible lines of rooms to be aired and a visit expected before winter. The visit, apparently, never occurred. A dead Garvey aunt has been keeping that small disappointment behind glass ever since.

The door stands open onto a corridor of dark floorboards. At its far end, warm light from the stairwell lays a long bar across the runner. From below come the practical sounds of dinner being arranged: a serving spoon against china, the low rush of a fire in the common-room hearth, a chair dragged an inch and set straight again.

Then voices rise through the house.

“The restriction is broad because broad is easy,” a young man says. He speaks with the controlled force of someone who has made the same point more than once. “That does not make it wise.”

“It is temporary,” another answers. His voice is clear and measured, built for rooms where people are expected to listen. “Until the instructor's report is reviewed, inconvenience is preferable to another injury.”

“Only if the restriction produces safety.” A woman's reply is low and exact. “At present it produces a closed court and a great deal of satisfaction among people who have never trained there.”

There is a brief pause below.

“Thank you,” the first man says.

“Do not thank me. I have not offered you my booking tomorrow.”

“I know.” His answer comes a shade too quickly. “I did not ask for it.”

“You asked everyone else to sign a request before anyone has seen the report,” says a third voice, lighter than the others. “That is not the same thing.”

“The people who use the court have said what they need.”

“People often say what they need while leaving out what they do not wish made public,” the clear-voiced man says. “You know that.”

For a moment, only the fire answers. Then the woman with the clipped voice says, “We can obtain the report tomorrow. We can decide what to sign after that. Tonight we can eat.”

Another woman speaks, her words spaced into small, plain lines.

“Let the newcomer eat first.
Let no one make him proof
of an argument.”

A laugh breaks from someone downstairs, short and unwilling to grow into a larger one.

“Fair,” says the first man. “On both counts.”

The conversation loosens after that. A voice asks whether the soup has gone cold. Someone else says it was never permitted the chance. Crockery shifts. The dinner hall's open doorway throws a brighter rectangle of candlelight across the common room below, and the scent that reaches the stairwell is roasted herbs, warm bread, and something sharp with citrus.

At the foot of the stairs, an Academy server in blue-grey livery appears with a covered dish balanced on one hand. She looks up, finds you at the open doorway, and inclines her head.

“Mr. Garvey,” she says, “dinner is served.”
