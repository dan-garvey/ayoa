Ysolde looks from the window to you. For the first time since dinner began, she turns fully in her chair rather than answering over her shoulder.

“The light itself,” she says. “There is no flame in a bridge globe. The glass is fixed to the rail, but the radiance inside it is drawn outward in a particular shape.”

She lifts one pale hand over the table, fingers together, then opens them slowly toward the window.

“On a calm night, each lamp makes a round pool on the bridge. When the storm current runs west beneath the plateau, the far edge of each pool lengthens east. The posts do not move. The bridge does not move. From the gardens, the whole row looks tilted.”

The green sauce is tarragon after all, sharp and grassy against the browned meat. Ysolde watches the window again as another thin flash lights the stormbank.

“From above, it is clearer,” she adds. “The lamps make a line like pale feathers pressed in one direction.”

Caelindra turns her wineglass by its stem. “From the ground, it looks like the Academy has built a very expensive street of lamps and allowed them all to become drunk.”

“That is not what feathers look like,” Ysolde says.

“No, but it is what bad civic design looks like.”

“The design is sound.”

“The design has weather.”

Ysolde considers this with the grave attention she gave the gravy boat. “Yes.”

Thessaly’s mouth curves around the edge of her bread. “Then both statements may be true.”

“They often are,” Aldric says.

Ashara reaches across for the dish of roasted roots. “That one will enjoy itself for the rest of the evening.”

“I am not obliged to stop because you have grown bored,” Caelindra says.

“You are not. But the roots are cooling.”

Caelindra takes another helping without conceding the point.

Seraphel looks toward the storm. Her wings settle close against the chair.

“No wick, no smoke,
only light persuaded
to keep a road.”

“That is closer,” Ysolde says.

Seraphel smiles into her cup.

Rashid has unfolded his petition again, but he does not begin writing. “The lamps on the south bridge do it most strongly,” he says. “The first time I saw it, I thought the bridge had been damaged.”

“It was your first week here,” Ashara says.

“It was raining sideways.”

“That is still not damage.”

“It is not now.”

“You can walk beneath them safely,” Ysolde says. “They are only strange if you expect a lamp to behave like an oil lamp.”

The server returns to collect the empty serving dish, and Ysolde moves her plate aside without looking away from the window. Beyond the glass, the storm sends another wash of white across the gardens. For a breath, the far line of the western bridge is visible through the cloud: a pale thread from the Academy’s edge, its lamps spilling thinly sideways into the dark.

“There,” Ysolde says.

The light does not move like a flame. It lies across the bridge in long, slanting strokes, each bright pool reaching after the next.

No one speaks over the view. Then Rashid folds his petition once more and tucks it under his plate, while the dinner talk resumes in lower voices around the table.
