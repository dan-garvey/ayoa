The lamp on the walnut desk makes a bright island of the room and leaves the upper corners to their old shadows. An uncapped bottle of ink stands beside the blotter; its black rim answers the stains at your fingertips. The Garvey signet has been polished hard enough to catch the light, though the charcoal wool at your wrist still shows every careful mend.

The framed letter beside the lamp is addressed to a great-nephew with another name. Its writer expects him in summer and has made room for him in the house between a recipe for plum cake and a complaint about the garden wall. The paper has yellowed evenly inside the glass. Nothing in it asks anything of you.

Beyond the window, the storm turns soundlessly below the plateau. Lightning opens and closes in the cloudbank, whitening the distant garden paths for an instant at a time.

Downstairs, somebody drags a chair back from the dining table. A covered dish gives off a brief, savory breath when its lid is lifted. Then Rashid says, with the measured patience of a man already repeating himself, “I’m not asking the Academy to reopen the court tonight. I’m asking them to say what failed and to give the practice group a supervised hour once they know.”

“And if they do not know by tomorrow?” Aldric asks.

“Then they should say that, too.”

Ashara’s reply comes a moment later. Heat seems to travel with it. “You have accounts from the people there. You do not have the instructor’s report.”

“I know.”

“Good. Then don’t write the report for them because you dislike the delay.”

Rashid exhales through his nose. “I dislike them closing the only court that doesn’t require a private tutor.”

“Those are separate objections.”

“They are connected objections.”

“Only if you make them so.”

A spoon taps porcelain. The argument does not seem to have spoiled anyone’s appetite; the small sounds of serving continue around it.

“The report may include something the students did not see,” Aldric says. His usual formal cadence has softened, though not by much. “A damaged ward, perhaps. If another person is hurt because everyone wanted the doors open quickly, that will not make the original restriction seem unfair.”

“No,” Rashid says. “It will make it worse.”

For several seconds, the room below is quiet enough that someone can be heard pouring water.

Thessaly says, “The request can ask for the report’s conclusion without claiming to know it.”

“It can,” Rashid says.

“That is less satisfying than a campaign,” she adds, “but I did not say it was impossible.”

The chair legs scrape again. Ashara’s tail gives a single impatient thump against wood. “Write that version, then. Leave the part about my training slot out of it.”

“I had not put that in.”

“You were about to.”

“I was considering it.”

“No distinction worth billing for.”

This earns a reluctant laugh from Rashid, and then a quieter one from someone farther down the table.

“If you have finished conducting civic repair over the carrots,” Caelindra says, “would anyone like them? They have been boiled past the point of political relevance.”

“They are good,” Seraphel says.

Her voice makes three plain lines of it.

“Soft from the pot,
bright with butter,
and still a carrot.”

“A devastating defense,” Caelindra says, but there is no bite in it.

“The carrots are not the problem,” Ysolde says. “The gravy boat is empty.”

“You noticed that before the argument?” Thessaly asks.

“Yes.”

“Why did you wait?”

“It was not yet empty.”

A laugh moves around the table, easier this time. Someone crosses the dining hall; the rhythmic click of shoes reaches the staircase, followed by the low rustle of Academy livery. A server appears on the landing with a small covered dish balanced in one hand. She looks toward your door and inclines her head.

“Mr. Garvey,” she says, keeping her voice tactfully low. “Dinner is served. The others have begun.”

At the foot of the stairs, the dining hall is warm with candlelight and conversation. The long table lies just beyond the doorway, its occupied places gathering the evening’s voices around it.
