The bench is cold through the wool of your coat. With your hands tucked into its pockets, you watch the western bridge hold its straight line over the storm.

The lamps never quite settle. Their light reaches east in the wind’s pull, then narrows, then reaches again. Beneath them, silver opens within the clouds whenever lightning moves through it—not a flash from above, but a pale stirring inside the weather itself. The garden remains quiet behind you: cedar branches shifting, a globe lamp making its small circle on the path, water ticking somewhere from a stone lip after the damp evening.

By the time the cold has found its way through the coat sleeves, the storm has taken the bridge twice and given it back twice more. The last view stays clear long enough to hold: the fixed span, the slanted lamps, the bright silver gathered in the cloud below.

The south path is darker on the return. The garden door opens easily at your touch, and the warmth of Garvey House comes out in a low breath of wax, old wood, and dinner’s fading spices. Voices still carry from the dining hall, softened by the door and too indistinct to separate. Upstairs, the corridor is empty.

Your room has kept the lamp burning low. At the washstand, a blue-and-white basin waits beside a folded towel and a square of harsh soap. The water turns gray at once. Ink loosens from the pads of your fingers and gathers in faint shadows at the drain, though a little remains at the creases around your nails.

The coat comes off with the garden chill still caught in it. You hang it carefully over the chair by the desk, beneath the framed letter and its faded handwriting. The room’s quiet is different from the garden’s: enclosed, familiar already in small things—the turned-down bed, the weak gold of the lamp, the distant settling sounds of the house.

When the lamp is lowered and the covers drawn up, the storm is only a muted light behind the window. The bridge and its leaning lamps can wait until morning.
