Ysolde turns from the window to you. Her attention settles with the same stillness she brought to the gravy boat.

“The light itself,” she says. “The globe has no flame. Its glass and metal are fixed to the bridge rail, but the radiance inside is held in a shape.”

She sets two fingers together above the table, then draws them slowly sideways.

“On a calm night, each one makes a round pool beneath it. When the western current runs under the plateau, the storm pulls the outer edge of that pool east. The post remains upright. The bridge remains where it is. Only the bright part stretches.”

The tarragon is sharp and grassy against the meat, its heat lingering after the first bite. Ysolde glances back to the dark beyond the windows.

“From the gardens, it looks as if the whole row has tipped. From above, the lamps look like pale feathers pressed in one direction.”

“From the ground,” Caelindra says, turning her wineglass by the stem, “it looks as if someone paid dearly for a street of lamps and then neglected to screw them in.”

“They are screwed in,” Ysolde says.

Caelindra’s mouth twitches. “Then the design is only visually irresponsible.”

“It is useful from the air.”

That gives Ysolde more to say. “The lamps make the safe approach visible in bad weather. The bridge itself is difficult to mistake for anything else, but the line of light tells you which end is the Academy. In fog, it is more important.”

Rashid looks toward the storm. “I asked a groundskeeper whether the south bridge was failing the first time I saw it.”

“You asked if it was supposed to do that,” Ashara says.

“Which was more diplomatic.”

“You were pointing at it as though it had insulted your family.”

“It was raining sideways.”

“That is weather,” Ysolde says.

“Yes,” Rashid says. “I know that now.”

Seraphel’s smile moves faintly at the edge of her cup.

“No wick, no smoke,
only light persuaded
to keep a road.”

“That is close,” Ysolde says.

Thessaly tears a piece of bread from the loaf. “Does it ever stop keeping the road?”

“Not in weather like this.”

“Then I prefer it to a flame.”

“Most people do,” Aldric says.

Caelindra takes another spoonful of the tarragon sauce and looks faintly displeased to find that she still dislikes it. “The lamps may be dependable. The kitchen is less so.”

“You have had three helpings,” Ashara says.

“I am verifying my judgment.”

No one asks for further proof. The server returns for the emptied serving dish, and Rashid slides his folded petition beneath his plate rather than opening it again. Dinner resumes around smaller matters: whether the first seminar’s reading list was intended as instruction or punishment, whether pepper belongs in every Academy sauce, whether the north garden’s roses have always grown in winter.

Another wash of lightning opens the cloud below the plateau. Between one blink and the next, the western bridge shows itself beyond the garden wall: a pale line held perfectly straight over the storm, its lamps casting long bright strokes sideways into the dark.

Ysolde watches until the cloud takes it again. Then she returns to her plate.
