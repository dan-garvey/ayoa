Thessaly leaves her hand above the box of black stones while she considers the question.

"Then it is a draw," she says. "The board has more crossings than we have stones. After the seventh white and seventh black, an unjoined path is still unjoined. We do not move anything afterward to make it prettier."

She taps one of the carved lines with a narrow fingernail. "A stone may block a route, but it cannot remove one already laid. That is all."

The fire gives a soft pop behind her. At the study table, Rashid turns a sheet over and continues writing beneath the lamp. Ysolde's grey-covered book lies open in her lap; rain makes wavering silver threads on the window beyond her shoulder.

You take the first white stone and set it near the middle of the board, where two paleward runs branch away from it. Thessaly answers at once, placing black nearer one of the dark notches. Her face does not change, but she sits a little farther forward over the board.

Your next white stone makes a clean join toward the left-hand pale edge. Thessaly places hers across the board instead of cutting that line, beginning a black run that angles down toward the far dark notch. The pieces are smooth and cool beneath your fingers; the carved crossings make their small map clearer with every stone.

On your third turn, you put white into the open crossing she needs to lengthen that black run. Thessaly looks at it for a moment.

"That does stop the direct route," she says.

Her next stone goes one line lower, where its connection is less obvious. You carry your white path toward the opposite pale notch. By the fourth pair of stones, the board holds two interrupted roads between its edges, each one close enough to make the other matter.

Thessaly's fifth black stone lands beside your advancing white line. It does not break it, but it takes the one crossing that would have joined your last two stones in a straight run. You turn your next white stone toward the remaining branch and place it over the gap in her lower route.

Rashid's pen stops. He glances over from his papers, first at the board and then at Thessaly.

"White has made that inconvenient," he says.

"For the moment," Thessaly says.

She sets her sixth black stone away from both blocked routes, linking it to a small black cluster near the board's center. It is a quiet move. Only when you lay your seventh white stone across one of the two darkward openings does the shape of it settle into view: the black stones now reach the opposite notch by a second line.

Thessaly takes the last black stone between finger and thumb. "This one joins," she says, and places it in the open crossing you cannot cover.

The black path runs from one dark notch to the other in an uneven, continuous chain. She traces it once without touching the stones, then leans back.

"Black," she says.

Rain ticks steadily against the window. You have seven white stones spread between the pale notches, close in two places but not joined in either. Thessaly studies them with the same attention she gave the winning line.

"You saw the first block," she says. "Most people spend their early games building only one route and notice mine when it is already finished. You did not."

Rashid gathers his papers into a neat stack. "You also made her use both branches. That's more than a first game usually gets."

Thessaly begins returning the stones to their separate piles. "He made me use both branches because he had stones left to put in the way. That is the game."

She fits the board back into its shallow box, white stones on one side and black on the other, and sets the lid in place. The firelight catches briefly on the carved paths before they disappear.
