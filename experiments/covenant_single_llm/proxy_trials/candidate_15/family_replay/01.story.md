Stone's hand stills over his notes. At Iona's name, some of the formality goes out of his face.

"Your great-aunt Iona," he says. "I did. She was a Conclave student when I was new enough to think a neatly copied map could not possibly be wrong."

He draws the cold cup a little nearer, looking at it rather than at the books. The map had been a survey of the garden paths, he says: an old, handsome thing with its hedges done in green wash and every gate numbered in a careful hand. He had set it for an optional first-year exercise and insisted its scale was sound. Iona stayed after the seminar to point out that the gate by the glasshouses stood three paces farther east than the map allowed.

He had told her that an archive did not alter because she disagreed with it. She asked whether he had walked the path himself. He had not.

"She offered six silver that three first-years could prove her right before supper," Stone says. "I accepted, because I was a young lecturer and had confused confidence with judgment."

The first-years in question had been avoiding a policy lecture and readily agreed to the wager once Iona explained it. Near dusk they came back muddy to the hem, carrying the map between them and arguing over which of them had noticed the gate first. The gardener had moved it years before to spare an old quince tree. Iona had known only that the tree was there; the three of them had known enough to look for it.

Stone's mouth almost bends into a smile. He says Iona made him pay the six silver in front of the class the next morning, then spent it on tea and bread for the three first-years. When he protested that she was using his loss to buy their loyalty, she wrote *map correction fee* on the receipt and told him that if he wanted a better defense, he ought to bring one.

"She could be exhausting," he says. "If she thought a person had made a lazy claim, she would not let it pass merely because they had said it pleasantly. But she remembered who had missed a meal, who was late because a bridge permit had failed to arrive, who had no one to sit with at dinner. She was forever finding some practical excuse to put food in front of people."

He looks toward the darkened upper shelves, where the lamps leave the highest bindings in shadow.

"Iona treated Garvey House as a house before she treated it as a name. She would have been pleased that you found her letter. She would also have pretended not to be."

For a moment, the only sound is the small, steady tick of the library clock.
