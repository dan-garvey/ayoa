By the time the fruit course arrives, the court dispute has settled into the practical shape of a request and its unanswered conditions. Rashid passes the sheet along once more. Ashara adds her sharp signature near the bottom; Aldric leaves the pen where it lies.

"Who would supervise?" Thessaly asks, having read the first paragraph twice.

"Whomever the faculty approves," Rashid says. "That is what I am asking them to decide."

Thessaly considers the answer, then returns the paper without marking it. Caelindra turns a page in her little book. The talk gradually shifts to seminar timetables and a garden path washed slick by last week's rain, while servers bring out pears baked with honey and cloves. You finish dinner without adding your name or judgment to the paper.

When you leave the dining hall, voices still travel through its partly closed doors behind you. Upstairs, the brass catches on the back of the frame resist for a moment, then give beneath your careful fingers. The letter comes free on a single folded sheet, its old ink browned but still clear.

*To my great-nephew, should one ever come to Garvey House—*

*The lawyers will call this place an inheritance. That is their word for every room that needs work. The third-floor landing leaks in hard rain, the blue cupboard in the pantry sticks, and the dining table is at its best when it is loud.*

*Let the others use the house if they want it. I have seen too many grand rooms become monuments because everyone was too polite to put a wet coat over a chair.*

*Professor Gareth Stone was a junior lecturer when I wrote this. He owes me six silver from a wager involving an incorrectly drawn map and three first-years who knew better than both of us. If he is still here, do not tell him I remembered. He will make too much of it.*

*Your affectionate great-aunt,*

*Iona Garvey*

The back of the frame closes again with a faint click. By the time you reach the ground floor, the library is quieter than the rest of the house, its lamp-oil air and old bindings holding the evening at a distance.

Professor Stone sits at a long table beneath the upper gallery, a cold clay cup beside a spread of books and loose notes. He is a narrow older man in a coat worn shiny at the elbows. At the sound of the door, he looks up; his eyes pause on the Garvey signet before lifting to your face.

"Mr. Garvey," he says. "Professor Stone. I was told you had arrived. What can I do for you?"
