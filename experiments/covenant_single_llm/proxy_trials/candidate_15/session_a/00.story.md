At the desk, the brass lamp turns the paper-bare center of the blotter honey-colored and leaves the corners of the room in dusk. Your primer lies open where its final section begins—Article Nineteen, its clipped legal language giving no warmth to the word *execution*. The inherited signet sits heavy against the mended cuff of your charcoal coat. Beside it, a framed letter in a narrow, impatient hand begins *To my great-nephew,* and continues beneath the glass toward a life that never arrived.

Through the window, the gardens have begun to light themselves: pale path stones, white walls, lamp-globes floating above flowerbeds that somehow continue to bloom in the wind off the storm. One floor below, a dining chair scrapes hard across the floorboards. Crockery answers it; the house has the busy, constrained sound of people making space around a table that was set before any of them were born.

A woman says something too low to distinguish. A young man replies, more clearly, “You know I’m not asking for your court.”

The next sound is three knocks on your door.

“Rowan Garvey?” The voice from the landing is controlled and warm. “Rashid vel Amara. The bell has not rung yet. I thought I could save you from having to make your entrance alone.”

At the far end of the hall, the stair descends toward candlelight and the faint smell of browned butter. A shadow waits on the other side of the door.
