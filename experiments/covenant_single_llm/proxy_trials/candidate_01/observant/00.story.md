Dusk reaches Garvey House by degrees. Blue light lies over the gardens outside your second-floor window, caught in the pale paths and the waxy leaves of flowers that ought not all be blooming together. Beyond the garden wall, one of the hard-light bridges runs out over the storm: a white line thinning into cloud, with slow lightning moving far below it and no thunder at all.

The room set aside for you is orderly in the way a room prepared by strangers is orderly. The bed has been made tightly enough to show every fold. A narrow desk stands beneath the window with an oil lamp, a new primer on the Covenant and the Academy, and a framed letter whose browned first page is addressed to a great-nephew who never arrived. The clothes sent ahead by the lawyers have been hung in the wardrobe with their hems level and their sleeves turned neatly inward. Your carefully mended charcoal coat makes its own, plainer line among them. The Garvey signet catches the lamplight in the fading room; ink has settled dark in the small creases of your fingers.

The primer gives the welcome dinner a page of ceremony. First-years of the Conclave eat together every evening in the formal hall. Tonight, the empty human place is no longer empty, and the long table has been set for seventeen.

Below, the house is beginning to fill itself. A chair leg gives a brief scrape across varnished floorboards. Doors open and close along the ground floor; the smell rising through the stairwell is warm bread, pepper, roasted roots, and something sweetly citrus. A server in Academy livery passes the foot of the stairs with a stack of white plates, the blue-and-gold cuff of the uniform flickering between the banisters.

Then a woman's voice carries from the dining hall, low and unmistakably close to the edge of impatience.

“The table was laid at seven.”

Another voice answers from somewhere farther in, measured and dry. “It is still seven. The meal has survived the first forty seconds.”

There is the faintest pause before the woman replies, “An argument of remarkable generosity toward the meal.”

Someone else laughs, not loudly. Cutlery settles. The room below continues without waiting for an audience to make it real.

Three crisp knocks sound at your door.

“Rowan Garvey.” The voice on the other side is the first woman's. A dry, contained warmth gathers in the hallway. “Ashara vel Kothren. The dining hall is downstairs, past the portraits and through the blue doors. We have not begun.”

From below, the measured voice calls, “Ashara, if you are drafting a map, include the stairs.”

“I have,” Ashara says, without raising her voice.

Her next words come to the door more quietly. “There are enough people at the table to make an entrance difficult if you arrive after the soup. I thought you should know the route.”

The hall beyond the door remains still except for the small tick of cooling wood and the distant work of the dining room. Ashara has not yet gone back downstairs.
