The stair turns twice through the portrait gallery. Faces in tarnished gilt look down from the walls: men and women in old human cuts of coat and dress, some painted against city windows, some against dark fields or formal chambers. Their brass plaques carry the same surname as the signet on your hand. At the lower landing, Ashara passes beneath them with the contained heat of a banked forge. Her horns nearly meet the lowest frame before she ducks her head by a fraction and continues toward the blue doors.

The dining hall opens brightly beyond them. Candles run the length of the varnished table, their flames steady despite the draft from the old house. Working lamps throw warmer light across paneled walls and silver serving dishes. Seventeen places have been laid, though several still hold no one; soup waits in a broad covered tureen at the center, and the bread basket has already been raided from one side.

Conversation loosens when you appear in the doorway. “Good evening. I’m Rowan,” you say. Ashara takes the chair facing the fireplace without ceremony, the point of her tail settling along the floor beside it.

“Good evening, Rowan,” says the man whose voice carried upstairs. He rises halfway from his chair before seeming to decide the gesture is excessive and rests one furred hand on its back instead. His coat is dark blue, precisely fitted, and his amber eyes remain on you without softening into a performance. “Rashid vel Amara.”

Across from him, an angel in white and gold inclines his head. He is broad-shouldered and composed enough to make the small movement look practiced. “Aldric Verantus. Welcome.”

The young woman at Aldric’s right has pale skin with a green cast in the candlelight and black eyes that take the flame without reflecting it. Her blue-black hair drifts slowly over one shoulder, though the air in the hall is still. “Thessaly Morrow,” she says. “That is an introduction. I do not know what else is required of one.”

“You have fulfilled the difficult part,” Rashid tells her.

At the next place, a small-winged woman with honey-blonde hair smiles over the rim of her cup. Her voice enters the room in two neat lines.

“Seraphel Dawnquill, if you please.
Welcome, before the soup congeals.”

The woman beside her bends close to her plate, as if the contents have suddenly become absorbing. Silver hair has been arranged in a complicated fall over one shoulder; a pointed ear moves once under it. When she looks up, violet eyes travel briefly from your signet to the repaired seam at your cuff and then away again.

“Caelindra Vaeyn,” she says. “The soup is in no danger. The kitchen here overestimates its own importance.”

“The kitchen has kept us alive through three terms,” Rashid says.

“An ambitious claim for vegetables and salt.”

At the far side of the table, a slender woman with white hair and grey eyes has been cutting a pear into exact slices. She sets the knife down before speaking. “Ysolde Thornmantle.” Her voice is quiet enough that the clink of silver nearly covers it. “The bread is better than last week’s.”

“There,” Rashid says, returning to his seat. “A useful measure.”

Ashara looks from the bread basket to him. “You argued for its survival forty seconds ago. Your standards have advanced quickly.”

“I adapt to evidence.”

“Conveniently.”

One of the servers moves past with a steaming pot, setting it down near the tureen before withdrawing toward the wall. At the middle of the table, three places remain clear: one beside Rashid, with the bread basket within reach; one opposite Ashara beneath the gaze of a severe Garvey portrait; and one near Ysolde, where the lamp light falls coolly across the white linen. Around them, the others resume their small disputes about salt, soup, and whether the kitchen deserves praise.
