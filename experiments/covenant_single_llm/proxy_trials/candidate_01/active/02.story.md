The chair gives a small sound against the floor as you take the place marked for Garvey. Thessaly sits to one side with her hands folded at the edge of her napkin; Ysolde occupies the other, her untouched water beading on the outside of its glass. Across the table, Ashara takes the hottest bowl from the server's tray without waiting for it to be set down.

At the mention of handbills, notices, and stories, Caelindra's eyes lift from the spoon she has just selected.

“Stories,” she says. The word is neither praise nor dismissal.

“Personal work is permitted at the Commerce press,” Rashid says. “Course material and official notices have precedence. The rest is a matter of the ledger.”

“It must carry a printer's name,” Thessaly adds. “The workshop steward removes unsigned sheets.”

“Because anonymous accusations are usually less brave than their authors,” Aldric says.

“And because someone once printed two hundred invitations to a party that did not exist,” Ashara says. “The kitchen prepared for it.”

Rashid's mouth shifts at one corner. “That was not an accusation.”

“It was an excellent waste of sugar.”

Ysolde looks from your ink-stained fingers to the long table, where every name card has been written in the same dark hand. “The letters are separate, and then made to appear continuous.”

“A little art in iron dress,” Seraphel says,
“Makes many quiet voices cross a press.”

The first course is poured: a clear broth fragrant with pepperroot and herbs, followed by roasted squash glazed to a dark sheen and bread still warm in its basket. Conversation loosens around the work of passing dishes. Caelindra asks the server for the smaller spoon; the server returns with it before the next plate reaches the table. Aldric thanks her by name. Thessaly examines a sliced pear as though it has offered a condition she has not yet agreed to.

Rashid turns to Ashara when the bread comes around again. “Kess put the rematch on the dueling board. Sixth bell tomorrow.”

“Then Kess will be there at sixth bell,” Ashara says.

“You have Rhetoric before that.”

“Rhetoric occurs indoors. The ground will still be empty afterward.”

Caelindra sets down her spoon. “A comprehensive argument for the value of education.”

“I did not say it lacked value.”

“You did make it sound furnished.”

The small motion of Ashara's tail crosses the back of her chair. Rashid reaches for his cup. Ysolde's attention remains on the exchange for a beat longer than it does on the food.

At the far end of the table, Aldric has asked Seraphel whether she will attend dusk prayer after classes. Her answer arrives with the same clear cadence as before.

“At dawn the chapel claims its due;
At dusk I have some work to do.”

“Of course,” Aldric says. He turns his cup once between both hands and does not press the point.

The meal moves on. More dishes arrive; the storm beyond the windows turns the glass white at intervals, then leaves the gardens in their ordinary lamplight. One of the servers refills the water at your place. The talk at the table passes from the next day's seminars to a misplaced reading list, from the dining hall's bread to the merits of the different garden paths when the wind is up. No one attempts to make the dinner into a ceremony it has not yet earned.

By the time the last plates have gone back through the swinging doors, the long table has softened into rings left by glasses and a scatter of crumbs. Ashara rises first, already fastening the cuff of one sleeve. Rashid follows a moment later, collecting a folded schedule from beside his plate. Caelindra lingers only long enough to tell a departing server that the pear tart was better than the soup, which the server receives with a surprised nod. Ysolde leaves by the garden doors, pale hair briefly silvered by the lamps outside. The others disperse through the house in twos and singles, their voices fading into separate corridors.

The Commerce annex lies beyond the quadrangle, under a low run of dragon-carved arches where the old black stone gives way to newer brick. The lamp-globes have fully opened by the time you reach it. Their light pools on the wet flagstones, and beyond the terrace rail the storm spreads below the Nexus in an unbroken, silent field.

The lower workshop is closed for the night, but the notice board beside its door remains lit by a hooded lamp. Through the small pane of glass, the hand press is visible in the dimness: a squat iron frame, a wooden handle, cabinets of type rising behind it in shallow labeled drawers. A sharper smell of old ink clings to the threshold.

The use-sheet is pinned beneath a brass plaque. Most of tomorrow's spaces are claimed by lecture handouts, a Commerce society circular, and an Arcana lab's request for labeled boxes. The hour after the midday seminar is empty, as Thessaly said. Two adjoining evening bells three days from now are open as well.

Beneath the schedule, the workshop rules permit personal editions for enrolled students. Two bells may be reserved without further approval; longer runs require the steward's signature. The printer must give a name, estimate the number of sheets and pages, and leave one finished copy in the Academy archive. A clean sign-up line waits below the open times, with the pencil in your hand and the dark workshop beyond the locked door.
