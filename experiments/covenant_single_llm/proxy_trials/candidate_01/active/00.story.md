At three hundred feet above the storm, evening does not arrive by darkening. It arrives when the garden lamps kindle one by one along the pale paths below, their light caught briefly in the wet leaves before the wind bends the branches again. Beyond the garden wall, one of the hard-light bridges burns a steady white into weather that never seems to exhaust itself. Lightning moves far beneath the plateau, slow and soundless.

Your room has been made ready with the care of people who knew an estate was receiving its heir. The bed is tightly turned. A row of clothes sent ahead by the lawyers hangs in the wardrobe: good wool, sober colors, alterations done by an unknown hand. Your charcoal coat is darker than the gathering dusk at the window; the mending at its cuffs gives it a different kind of finish. On the desk, the Conclave primer waits beside an oil lamp and a blotter too clean to bear the marks on your fingers. The Garvey signet catches lamplight when the room shifts around it.

The framed letter hangs above the desk. Its paper has yellowed gently behind the glass, its handwriting fine and crowded. The name in the salutation belongs to a great-nephew who never came to Garvey House.

The house is larger than the room makes it seem. Sounds travel through its old joins: a door closing on the floor below, the subdued roll of a serving cart, the faint ring of glass set carefully on wood. Then voices begin to rise from the dining hall with the practical impatience of people preparing a table together.

“The second runner,” a woman's voice says from below. Low, clear, and accustomed to being heard. “The first one has a tear by the end chair.”

“It has a frayed edge,” answers a man. His tone remains even, though the words carry farther than they need to. “We are eating dinner, not conducting an inspection.”

“A frayed edge becomes a tear when someone catches a heel in it.”

There is a pause, then the scrape of furniture moved with deliberate restraint.

“You may have won,” the man says.

“I did not enter a contest.”

The exchange breaks apart beneath the clink of plates. Somewhere in the hall, someone laughs once, briefly; another voice says something too quiet to make out. The welcome dinner has begun to take shape without waiting for the new place at the table to be filled.

A knock comes at your door: three even taps, neither tentative nor loud.

“Garvey,” says the woman from downstairs. Up close, her voice is just beyond the wood, and a dry, banked warmth gathers in the hall with it. “Ashara vel Kothren. The dining hall is straight down the central stair. The bell will ring in a few minutes, and the kitchen regards it as a binding schedule.”

From farther down the landing, the man's voice returns. “Ashara, the runner is now perfectly safe.”

“Then stop standing on it, Rashid.”

The floorboards settle. Below, a chair is drawn back from the long table, and the first low test-note of the dinner bell hums through Garvey House.
