Ashara walks for several paces before she answers. The garden walls have kept the wind from the path, but a cool thread of it finds its way through when the hedges open toward the quadrangle.

“The first sequence has two exits,” she says. “By the fourth, there are more. You are meant to see which one a fighter chooses when the first answer stops working.”

Rashid glances over his shoulder as he fastens the last button of his coat. “She makes the same mistake appear until it becomes tempting to call it a habit.”

“Only when the answer works,” Ashara says.

“That is a considerably less poetic version of what I said.”

“I am relieved you noticed.”

The lamps of Garvey House are visible before the building itself: long amber rectangles caught in the wet leaves, then the angular roofline above the gardens. By the time you reach the outer door, the faint clatter of cards has begun again from the common room.

Dena and her friends have claimed the low table by the fire. A score slate rests beside the deck, marked with three columns of quick, slanted numbers. Dena sits with her splinted wrist propped on a folded cushion and fans a small hand of painted cards with the other. The two students beside her have made room by pushing their cups farther back from the table’s edge.

She looks up as Rashid enters. “You are exactly half an hour late.”

“I know.”

“That is less useful than arriving on time.”

“Also true.” He takes off his coat but does not set it aside yet. “How badly am I losing?”

“You are not losing. You have not begun. It is a different kind of failure.”

One of Dena’s friends snorts into their cup. Rashid’s mouth tightens, then eases.

“Then I will begin.”

Dena reaches for the deck. When you approach the table and ask for a place in the game, she studies you for a moment, then shifts her cards into a neat stack.

“We are accepting another player,” she says. “That makes five, which is still legal and considerably more interesting than four.” She nudges the empty chair beside hers out with the heel of her boot. “Sit. The game is Banners.”

You take the chair. The fire has warmed the leather through, and the table bears the small evidence of a game already underway: a discarded card painted with a green-stemmed flower, a draw pile squared at the center, and a little brass token in front of each player.

“Five cards to a hand,” Dena says. “Each has a suit and a number. On your turn, you draw either from the pile or from the discard. Then you throw one card away. Three of a number, or three in sequence from the same suit, makes a banner.”

“Two banners win the hand,” Rashid adds, pulling out the chair across from you. “Unless Dena changes the rules because she is behind.”

“I change the rules only when the rules have failed to reward good judgment.”

“That is changing the rules.”

“It is Commerce.”

Dena ignores him and turns the brass token in front of her so its stamped flame faces up. “A banner must be shown before your next draw. You cannot add to it afterward, and you cannot take a card back once you have discarded it. No wagers. No favors. No pretending a promise is part of the game because you dislike the score.”

Thessaly’s rule might have found agreement here, though she is nowhere in sight.

Rashid settles his coat over the back of his chair. “She added the last two after a third-year tried to make the game into a lesson.”

“A third-year tried to make everything into a lesson.”

“That was his principal flaw.”

The deck passes to Dena. She deals with her uninjured hand, one card at a time, unhurried enough that no edge catches on the splint. Five painted rectangles come to rest before you: a silver tower marked three, a red lantern marked three, a blue river marked four, a gold bird marked five, and a second blue river marked six. The green-stemmed flower remains faceup beside the draw pile.

“You have the first turn,” Dena says. “The game has not had a human player in a while. It can bear the novelty.”

Around the table, the others settle into the brief, attentive quiet of people who know the rules and are waiting to see what someone new will do with them. The discard waits in the firelight; the draw pile keeps its painted faces hidden.
