The storm gives the window no horizon. Far below the gardens, lightning opens soundlessly in the cloudbank and leaves the pale paths blue for a breath before the lamps reclaim them. One of the hard-light bridges is a straight, faint line beyond the hedges, disappearing into weather that has no visible end.

Your room has been made ready in the careful, impersonal way of a place that has expected someone without knowing who would arrive. The bed is smooth. A row of new clothes waits in the wardrobe, their tailoring too exact to feel like yours. At the desk, the framed letter catches the lamp with its old glass. The handwriting within is narrow and brown with age; the salutation is turned just far enough from the light that it cannot be read from where you stand.

The Garvey signet weighs warmly against your hand. Its seal has been pressed into the cream invitation on the desk: FIRST-YEAR WELCOME DINNER, it says beneath the Academy crest. GROUND FLOOR. THROUGH THE COMMON ROOM. A small inked arrow points left, as if the house itself has a habit of losing new residents.

It is not quiet downstairs.

Garvey House has been assembled by people who expected company and did not much care whether that company overheard one another. The central stair rises in an open well from the common room, and voices travel up with the smell of woodsmoke and something browned in butter. A chair scrapes over the dining-hall floor. Someone laughs, abruptly and without apology.

“The octagon after dinner,” a woman says below. Her voice is low and clear enough to carry. “Half an hour, Rashid. Tomorrow is not waiting for your notes.”

“I know what tomorrow is,” a man answers. There is a smile in the answer until the last few words, which are rather more precise. “And I said half an hour.”

“You said that yesterday.”

“Yesterday I was right to correct my notes.”

“That is not a defense.”

Another voice joins them, light and unhurried. “It is a defense,” it says. “It simply fails.”

There is a brief silence, followed by the man’s laugh.

Then the door receives a measured knock.

“Mr. Garvey?” The voice outside is warmer than the one below, formal without being stiff. “Aldric Verantus. I hope I’m not intruding. First bell is nearly due, and I thought an introduction might be less unpleasant before everyone is seated.”

The passage beyond your door falls briefly still. Aldric adds, “The dining hall is straight through the common room at the foot of the stairs. There will be a place for you.”

Footsteps come up two steps at a time and stop nearby. The first woman speaks again, closer now. “He has an invitation, Aldric. The Academy has used written directions for some time.”

“Then I have confirmed them,” Aldric says.

“You have confirmed that you can read.”

“Ashara,” the man below calls, with the weariness of someone who has heard this exchange in other forms, “if you make him regret leaving his room before he has seen the table, I’ll count it against your footwork tomorrow.”

“You may try.”

From the lower floor, a woman’s voice rises in three small lines, each one almost spoken rather than sung.

“The candles have been lit.\n+The soup has lost its patience.\n+We should not teach it cruelty.”

For a moment, the only sound is the storm’s distant, white pulse against the glass.

“Seraphel is right,” Aldric says. “On the matter of soup, at least.” He pauses outside the door, not testing the latch. “No one expects a speech. We are meant to have dinner, learn each other’s names, and survive the evening without making a treaty of it.”

Ashara gives a short sound that might be a laugh. “Speak for yourself. Some of us enjoy a treaty.”

“Some of us enjoy dinner,” Rashid says.

The bell begins below: three clear notes, neither urgent nor easy to ignore. Beyond the door, Aldric’s shadow lies across the threshold in the hall light. Down the open stairwell wait firelight, voices, a long table set for seventeen, and a name newly placed among the others.
