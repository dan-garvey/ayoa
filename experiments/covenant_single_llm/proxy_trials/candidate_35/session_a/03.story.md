Dena gives the deck a small lift in acknowledgment. “Leave the cards unsullied by military honor,” she says. “We’ll see whether that is possible.”

Ashara is already through the outer door when the last of the words reaches the common room. Rashid catches up with her at the end of the garden path, his coat unbuttoned against the climb of the evening. You follow them beneath the sheltering walls, where the storm-light comes and goes beyond the hedges without a sound. The gardens have the damp green smell of soil after rain that never quite arrives. Ahead, the Academy quadrangle opens in pale planes and dark ones: white marble catching the lamp-globes, black stone keeping its heat, a low dragon-carved arch framing the route to the dueling grounds.

They do not speak until the octagon is in sight.

It is an open ring of packed earth, its eight sides marked by waist-high posts and a line of white stones pressed firmly into the ground. A board beside the entrance lists the current rankings under a weathered glass cover. Ashara’s name is at the top. Rashid’s is directly beneath it.

“The south side,” Ashara says. “The wind will be against your left shoulder.”

“There is hardly any wind.”

“There is enough.”

Rashid steps through the gap between two posts, then turns back to wait while Ashara collects a pair of practice blades from the rack. They are dull-edged lengths of pale ironwood, weighted in the hilt and wrapped in dark leather where the hands go. She tests each with a short, economical swing before passing one to him.

“Same sequence,” she says. “You yield the center, I take it, you recover outside my reach.”

“I do not yield it.”

“You have yielded it three times this week.”

“I have made space for you to overextend.”

Ashara’s tail makes one precise curve behind her. “Show me.”

They take their places. You remain outside the stones, where the packed earth gives way to a thin border of grass silvered by the lamps.

Rashid begins with his blade held low and close, feet set at an angle that leaves him room to turn. Ashara’s stance is narrower, her weapon raised without strain in front of her shoulder. For a moment neither moves. Then she crosses the distance in two fast steps.

The first exchange is over almost before the sound has gone from the blades. Ashara drives him high, changes the line of her strike, and turns her wrist at the last instant. Rashid catches the second blow, but his back foot gives ground. The next touch of ironwood lands against the leather at his collarbone.

“Again,” Ashara says.

“That was the opening you wanted.”

“It was the opening you gave me.”

The second pass begins the same way. This time Rashid does not retreat in a straight line. His heel shifts outward, just enough that Ashara’s blade skims past the center of his guard instead of forcing it aside. He turns with the contact and brings his own weapon up toward her ribs.

Ashara has already moved. The faintest red shimmer runs along her forearm and the air between them trembles with heat. Rashid’s answering cut meets her blade with a crack that carries across the empty grounds. He slips free before she can press the advantage, but she has taken the center again.

“Better,” she says. “You stopped protecting the place I wanted.”

Rashid lowers his blade a fraction. “And you used heat earlier.”

“Tomorrow’s opponent will use what she has.”

“Tomorrow’s opponent is not going to be you.”

“That is why you should be grateful.”

For the next several exchanges, the pattern becomes clear by repetition. Ashara comes forward hard enough to make standing still impossible, but not carelessly. Each time Rashid finds an answer, she alters the pressure that created it. When he turns left, she shortens her reach and catches his recovery. When he keeps the center, she makes him defend high and takes his wrist with the flat of the practice blade. When he tries to meet her force directly, the warmth rising from her hands makes the hilt slick against his grip.

Rashid’s answers change as well. His movements grow quieter. The loose ends of his coat cease to be an inconvenience because he has stopped allowing them to pull at his turns. On one pass he yields ground, then plants his foot against the packed earth and pivots so sharply that Ashara’s next strike finds only the air where his shoulder had been. His blade reaches the side of her neck before she twists clear.

Ashara’s expression changes by a degree. “Again.”

“You only say that when I have done something right.”

“No. I say it when you have stopped pretending the right thing was an accident.”

He shows his teeth at that, not quite a smile. “There is no kindness in you at all.”

“There is dinner waiting for you, apparently. I am saving time.”

“That is almost kindness.”

“Do not make it a habit.”

The next attempt begins with Rashid’s feet in the same place and ends nowhere near the same way. Ashara advances; he gives the center; she turns his guard. But instead of fleeing the pressure, he lets the blade ride down his own, catches the hilt against his palm, and uses the close contact to step inside her reach. For an instant they are too near for either long weapon to be useful.

Ashara’s free hand lifts, heat collecting there in a brief, bright pulse. Rashid turns his shoulder before it can touch him and hooks his foot behind hers. The sweep does not take her down. It does make her shift her balance, and in that shift his practice blade strikes cleanly against the outside of her arm.

The sound is small. It is enough.

Rashid steps back at once. Ashara looks at the point of contact, then at him. The heat leaves her hand.

“There,” she says. “Keep that.”

“I intend to.”

“You will forget it when someone raises their voice.”

“I will remember it because someone raised their voice.”

“That is the same weakness in a different coat.”

Rashid rolls one shoulder, breathing more deeply now. “You have a fondness for coats this evening.”

“You have brought two of them.”

The lamp-globes around the quadrangle brighten as the sky loses its last uncertain color. The practice continues. The blades meet and separate. Ashara calls for the same turn, the same recovery, the same refusal to be driven where she wants him. Rashid’s first successful counter does not repeat unchanged; on the following pass, Ashara anticipates it and stops him short with a low strike at the knee. He avoids that one by a narrower margin than before. On the pass after, he sees it coming soon enough to turn the near miss into a clean retreat.

By the time the chapel bell sounds the half-hour, neither of them has won anything that can be posted on the ranking board. Their sleeves are darkened at the cuffs from the damp air and exertion. Ashara rests the point of her practice blade against the ground. Rashid has a thin red mark at his collar where ironwood found him in the first exchange, and Ashara has one across the outside of her arm where he returned the touch.

“Enough,” Ashara says.

Rashid glances toward the garden path leading back to Garvey House. “Dena will have decided I deserve worse than the score she promised.”

“She may be right.”

“She often is. It is an unfortunate quality in a friend.”

Ashara returns the blades to the rack, aligning them until their hilts make a straight dark row. Then she looks toward you outside the stones.

“You kept the boundary,” she says. “Tomorrow’s bout begins after classes. The east gallery has a better view if you intend to come.”

Rashid fastens his coat as he steps out of the octagon. “And if Dena has not emptied the common room of all mercy, there may still be cards.”

He heads back toward the lamps of Garvey House at a quicker pace than he used on the way out. Ashara lingers only long enough to check the white stones where Rashid’s feet disturbed the line, then turns toward the same path. The octagon is quiet again behind them, marked with scuffs, shallow heel prints, and the fading warmth of the last exchange.
