Rashid’s hand completes its reach for the bread basket. He breaks off a piece and sets the larger half within Ashara’s reach before he answers.

“Then watch,” he says. “Most of the preparation is not impressive. It is repetitions, corrections, and Ashara telling me which part I have made too comfortable for myself.”

“You have made several things too comfortable for yourself,” Ashara says.

“There. A representative example.”

Ashara looks at you over the rim of her bowl. “The white stones are enough. Do not call advice into the ring, and do not cross the boundary because a blow looks worse from outside than it is.”

“I hadn’t offered advice,” Rashid says.

“I know. I am establishing the terms before someone else does.”

“That is sensible,” Thessaly says. “Unstated terms are how people make a nuisance of themselves.”

Seraphel’s fingers rest lightly against the edge of her spoon.

“A watcher keeps the line.\n+A fighter keeps the breath.\n+The rest is earned in noise.”

Caelindra has not yet touched the bread. Her attention settles briefly on you, then returns to the arrangement of candles reflected in the polished table.

“Short plays are an exacting form,” she says. “They cannot afford anyone who speaks merely because the author needs them to.”

“They can speak because silence would be worse,” Seraphel says.

“That is a reason,” Thessaly replies. “It is not a contract.”

“A contract is not always a reason,” Ysolde says. She draws the sleeve of her pale blue-grey coat farther from the broth before it can touch the rim of the bowl. “Some are merely old.”

Aldric gives the table an expression of patient appeal that does not require anyone’s obedience. “We have managed to reach philosophy before the first course is gone.”

“You began it by inviting a playwright to dinner,” Caelindra says.

“I did not personally arrange the dinner.”

“Then the Academy began it.”

The servers move through the exchange without hurrying. One replaces the bread basket; another brings a shallow dish of roasted roots, their edges darkened with spice, and a platter of glazed river fish. Ashara’s fingers leave a faint warmth against the silver spoon when she sets it down. Ysolde’s water glass has gathered a clean fog of condensation around its base. Seraphel folds her wings closer before the server passes behind her chair, while Thessaly slides the lacquered cards from the common room into an inner pocket with the unselfconscious care of someone who expects to use them later.

You eat. The broth gives way to the sharper sweetness of the roots, to bread torn and shared, to the quiet changes of plates and cutlery that make a formal dinner feel less like a ceremony and more like time being allowed to pass.

The conversation does not settle on you. Rashid and Ashara dispute whether the ranked board rewards a clean win more than a difficult recovery. They agree about the match that prompted the argument and disagree about almost everything else. Aldric reports that the Conclave seminar room has been reassigned because one of its lamps kept going dark through yesterday’s lesson. Ysolde wants to know whether the replacement room has windows. When Aldric says it does not, she considers this for a moment and announces that it will make poor weather study impossible.

“It is Covenant Law,” Caelindra says.

“Weather changes covenants,” Ysolde replies.

“Most people call that history.”

“Most people do not live long enough to notice the difference.”

Caelindra’s expression holds, then shifts by the smallest degree as though she has found an answer worth keeping for later. “That may be true,” she says.

Rashid glances toward the clock above the sideboard. “Professor Shaan begins Practical Rhetoric at first bell tomorrow. She has a reputation for assigning speeches before introductions are finished.”

“She has a reputation for being useful,” Ashara says.

“Those are not competing claims.”

“They often are.”

Thessaly has eaten the fish with neat concentration. “Does this professor accept written refusals to speak?”

“She accepts arguments that survive being spoken aloud,” Aldric says.

“Then no.”

That earns another short laugh from Ashara, and this one Rashid does not try to hide behind his cup. Even Caelindra’s attention drifts from the table arrangement long enough to watch Thessaly with an interest she does not explain.

By the time the final plates have been taken away, the storm beyond the windows has moved farther from the gardens. The light in the clouds comes less often now, leaving the dining hall to its candles and lamps. Chairs begin to ease back from the table. Ashara has already set her napkin beside her place setting when a voice comes from the common room.

“Rashid. We brought the cards and the terrible luck.”

A young demon woman stands in the archway with two other students behind her, a worn deck tucked under one arm. Her other wrist is held in a clean splint that catches the lamp-glow along its polished fastening. She has stopped just inside the room rather than approaching the table.

Rashid rises at once. “Dena. I should have sent word. Ashara and I have drills.”

Dena’s gaze goes from him to Ashara, then back. “You promised cards.”

“I did. Start a hand without me. I’ll join you in half an hour.”

“You promised the first half-hour.”

Ashara pushes back her chair. “I can find another partner.”

“No,” Rashid says. His courtesy has gone very careful. “I agreed to train with you.”

“And you agreed to play with us,” Dena says. “That is not the same as being in two rooms.”

For a beat, no one adds a joke to make the words lighter. Then Rashid looks toward the cards under Dena’s arm.

“You are right,” he says. “I should have told you before dinner. Begin without me, and I will accept whatever score you have built against me when I arrive.”

Dena studies him, then shifts the deck into the hand without the splint. “Fine. I reserve the right to win without you.”

“You have it.”

Her attention touches on you as if only now recalling the extra place at the table. “Garvey, you are welcome at cards. We use ordinary rules and accuse one another of cheating only when we have proof.”

Ashara turns toward the outer door, where the night beyond the common room waits black and bright with the storm’s retreating glow. “The octagon is this way. If you mean to watch, come now. Once we begin, stay beyond the stones.”

Rashid reaches for his coat. Dena and her friends spread the deck across the low table by the fire. For a moment, the house holds both invitations at once: card backs clicking under Dena’s steady hand, and the open door toward the dueling grounds where Ashara has already begun to walk.
