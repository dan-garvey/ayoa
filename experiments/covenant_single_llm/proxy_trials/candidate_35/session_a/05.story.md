Dena taps the brass token in front of you with one painted nail.

“The five are only the cards you keep loose,” she says. “When you complete a banner, you put those three down in front of you. They are finished. After you discard, your next turn begins with the dealer filling your loose hand back to five from the stack. Then you make the ordinary draw.”

Rashid leans one elbow on the table. “The important part is that a banner must be shown before that refill. Otherwise, everyone would wait until they had a perfect second banner and call it clever.”

“It would be clever,” Dena says. “It would also make the game unbearable.”

She turns the faceup discard so its corner is clear. The green-stemmed flower bears a two.

“You have a river four and a river six,” she says. “If you find the five, that is a banner. Your two threes are only a beginning. Keep beginnings when they do not cost you something better.”

The flower does neither. You leave it on the discard and take the top card from the hidden pile.

It is a blue river marked five.

For an instant, the table’s attention narrows to the card in your hand. Then Dena gives a small, approving click of her tongue.

“Show it,” she says.

You lay the river four, five, and six in a row before you. The painted water runs from one card to the next in a continuous dark-blue current. Dena turns your brass token so its flame faces the same direction as the others’.

“One banner,” she says. “Now discard from what remains.”

The gold bird five leaves your hand. It lands faceup between the deck and Dena’s score slate.

“That was the right one,” Rashid says as the turn passes. “The bird did nothing for you.”

“He says that as though the bird had a duty,” Dena says.

“It had a number and a suit. Those are duties in this room.”

The game moves around the table. Dena draws from the stack and gives up a silver tower seven. The student on her left takes it at once, then lays down a banner of three matching towers with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has been waiting for exactly that mistake. The other friend discards a red lantern four. Rashid studies it, leaves it, and draws blind.

“Why not take that one,” Dena says.

“Because if I need it, she knows I need it.”

“She knows you looked at it.”

“Looking is free.”

“Not in cards.”

When the turn returns to you, Dena replaces the three river cards in your hand from the top of the stack: a black stone eight, a silver tower four, and a green flower nine. The loose cards before you now offer no completed banner, but the tower four sits beside the tower three with enough promise to make the next discard matter.

The red lantern four remains on the table. You leave it there and draw blind again. A silver tower two joins the hand.

“You are gathering too many possible roads,” Dena says, not unkindly. “A pair of threes, a pair of towers, and no reason yet to know which one will lead anywhere.”

Rashid picks up the tower two from your loose cards only long enough to point at its painted roof, then puts it back exactly where it was. “Do not treat every resemblance as a plan. Wait for the table to tell you which suit is moving.”

The correction does not stop the hand. It gives the next few turns a clearer shape. You watch what leaves the other players’ hands. Dena has let two flowers go and has kept every lantern she draws. The student with the tower banner discards a stone six without looking twice at it. Rashid takes no river cards at all, even when one would make a neat run beside the first banner already on the table.

When a gold bird three appears in the discard, it would complete the pair of threes in your hand. Rashid’s eyes move to it, then to the red lantern three and silver tower three among your loose cards.

“That one is worth taking,” he says.

You take it and lay the three matching ranks side by side. Their painted suits have nothing in common but the number at their corners. The second banner joins the river cards in front of you, and for a moment Dena’s expression becomes more intent than her easy correction had been.

“That is two,” she says. “You may have won the hand.”

The student with the tower banner looks down at the score slate. “Only if Dena has not finished first.”

Dena’s hand pauses above the cards. She has not shown a banner yet. Then she takes one last draw from the stack, lays three red lanterns in a bright row, and places them beside three green flowers that have been waiting faceup by her score token.

“Two,” she says.

Rashid looks from Dena’s paired banners to yours. “That is what happens when someone has been holding the useful things all along.”

“That is what happens when someone pays attention,” Dena says. Her gaze shifts to you. “Your river banner was clean. The threes were a good recovery. Next time, you will see the lanterns sooner.”

The cards are gathered into separate piles: banners squared beside their tokens, loose hands turned face down, discarded suits returned to the center. Dena uses her good hand to draw the deck into a neat stack, then reaches for the score slate.

You turn your brass token over rather than set it out for the next deal. Rashid has already begun to shuffle, the soft snap of the cards steady beneath the fire’s low crackle. Dena marks the hand’s result, then leaves the empty place before you clear of new cards while the room settles around the completed game.
