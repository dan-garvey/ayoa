The framed letter behind the desk lamp is addressed to someone else.

Its ink has gone brown at the loops, but the hand is brisk enough to make out: an aunt telling her great-nephew that she has had the blue room aired, that the east-facing window still sticks in damp weather, that he must not let the cook persuade him a second pudding is a duty to family. The room around it is blue only where the curtains manage it. Their faded panels lift and settle with the air that comes through the imperfect latch. Beyond the glass, the gardens lie in their high-walled geometry, pale paths catching the first lamps. Farther out, one of the light bridges cuts a clean white line into the storm.

Your charcoal coat waits over the chair back, the mending at one cuff neat enough to pass unless somebody has a reason to inspect it. The Garvey signet on your hand catches the desk lamp whenever the house shifts its light across the room. It is an unfamiliar sort of announcement: not loud, exactly, but difficult to put away.

Below, Garvey House is gathering itself around supper. A chair leg scrapes over old wood. Someone laughs once, abruptly, and another voice answers too low to separate from the murmur of the dining hall. The smell rising through the stairwell is butter, pepper, and something sweetly charred at the edges. A bell gives a single, restrained note.

Then three knuckles tap against the door, which has been left a hand's breadth open.

"Mr. Garvey?"

The woman in the hall stands with one shoulder against the frame, as if she has decided not to presume on a room that is plainly not hers. Gold light warms her skin more softly than the lamp does. Her small white wings are folded close beneath the fall of white-and-gold cloth, each feather catching a separate sliver of brightness when she moves.

"Dinner is laid.\nThe others have begun\npretending not to wait."

She takes in the letter, then the desk, and gives a small wince at herself.

"I am Seraphel Dawnquill.\nThat was less graceful\nthan an introduction deserves."

From downstairs, a male voice carries up the stairwell. "If we are waiting, we might at least do it near the food."

"You are near the food," a woman answers. Her voice is level and warm in the way of banked coals. "You simply lack patience."

"An old fault." The man sounds untroubled by the charge. "I have several."

Seraphel's mouth turns at one corner.

"Rashid vel Amara,\nwho has arrived hungry.\nAshara vel Kothren,\nwho has arrived armed with criticism."

The downstairs conversation continues with the scrape of a serving dish. Seraphel does not call further introductions down the hall. Instead, she looks past the landing toward the stairs, where the banister curves around a portrait whose painted subject has the same dark eyes as the face in the letter's signature, though none of the same severity.

"There are seven places occupied below," she says, and then, more carefully, "Eight, when you join us. The table has room enough for the names it was set to remember."

The sentence sits between the soft motion of the curtains and the storm-light at the window. Downstairs, someone begins describing a sauce with great indignation; a second person says, with patient precision, that salt is not an insult to the cook. The house does not fall silent for its new heir. It goes on making an evening of itself.

Seraphel steps back from the doorway and leaves the stair clear.

"Would you like company going down?"
