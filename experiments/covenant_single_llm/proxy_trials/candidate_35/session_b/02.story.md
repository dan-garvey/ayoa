For a moment, the small sounds of dinner acquire too much room around them: the faint tick of a cooling lamp globe, the spoon Seraphel turns once against the rim of her bowl, rain whispering against the far windows. Rashid's hand pauses above the bread before he takes a piece and breaks it cleanly in two.

Ashara does not look away from you.

"Not tonight," she says. "Not by sitting at this table. The Conclave is not the Council. It is four years of instruction, scrutiny, and people deciding whether you can be trusted with anything larger than your own name."

Her thumb rests against the stem of her glass. "Your inheritance has given you a place here. It has not made you a Councilor."

"It has made people notice the empty seat again," Rashid says. The courtesy in his voice has grown more precise. "That will feel very much like an expectation, whether anyone is decent enough to name it plainly or not."

Aldric leans back a fraction. "Any appointment to the Council has procedures. The Academy cannot make one by arranging a welcome dinner."

"A relief," Caelindra says. "Though perhaps a disappointment to the people who spent an afternoon polishing the silver for it."

The corner of Rashid's mouth lifts. Aldric takes the remark in without returning it.

Ysolde has not touched the bread. A slender lock of white hair has come free at her temple, untouched by the rest of her careful stillness.

"In my family, people speak of a seat as if it were a chair," she says. "It is not. It is a set of obligations with a chair provided for ceremony. You can refuse a chair. The obligations are harder to locate."

"That is nearly cheerful," Caelindra says.

"I did not intend cheerfulness."

Thessaly folds her napkin once, exactly along an existing crease. "Expectation is not a contract," she says. "A contract needs terms, consideration, and acceptance. I recommend noticing the difference before anyone offers you papers."

"And before anyone offers you a favor," Rashid adds.

Thessaly looks at him. "Sometimes those are papers with better tailoring."

Seraphel has been listening with both hands around her water glass. When she speaks, it is quiet enough that the table settles to hear it.

"A thing may be offered\nwith such ceremony\nthat refusal seems impolite."

"Refusal is not impolite merely because it inconveniences an institution," Ashara says.

Rashid glances toward her. "No. But institutions have ways of making inconvenience expensive."

Ashara's tail has curled loosely around one back leg of her chair. "Then make them state the cost. Do not let them call it destiny and leave the bill unnamed."

No one presses the point into a speech. The words remain on the table among the cups and crumbs, solid enough without ornament.

As for magic, no one treats your uncertainty as a spectacle. Aldric's expression softens first.

"Not knowing is not a failure," he says. "The old accounts make human magic sound like a parlor trick one either possesses or does not. They are not very useful accounts."

"Most old accounts are written by people who prefer a clean ending to an accurate one," Caelindra says.

"Some magic declares itself," Ysolde says. "Some does not. That is all I know of it."

Ashara gives a short nod. "There are people here who can help you learn what you need to know. You needn't let a room full of strangers make it into a test."

The Academy server returns to remove the empty soup bowls. The new course arrives in their place: glazed root vegetables, rice fragrant with saffron, and slices of dark bread still steaming where the knife has cut them. The interruption gives the table a way back to smaller matters.

Rashid reaches for the sauce and finds Ashara already holding it. She passes it across without looking at him.

"Tomorrow," he says, "I am bringing a ladder to the octagon."

"Tomorrow," Ashara says, "you are bringing better footwork."

"I can bring both."

"You can attempt both."

The exchange loosens something in the room. Caelindra asks whether the octagon's board really has been damaged by rain or whether Rashid has simply lost an argument to weather. He assures her that weather has yet to defeat him formally. Thessaly says the distinction may be important to the weather. Even Aldric smiles then, briefly and despite himself.

At the far windows, another ribbon of silent lightning opens the storm below the Nexus. For an instant the light bridges hang over it like paths drawn on black paper. Then the dining hall returns to candlelight, warm food, and the unfinished evening gathered around the long table.
