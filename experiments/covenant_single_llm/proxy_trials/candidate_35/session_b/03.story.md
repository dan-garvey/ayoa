Rashid's laugh is low and unguarded enough to change the shape of the table. Ashara's gold eyes narrow by a degree, but the corner of her mouth gives her away before she reaches for the vegetables again.

"A sensible standard," Aldric says. "The Council can wait until it is at least properly inconvenient."

"It has excellent practice," Caelindra says.

The conversation does not stay with Councils. Once the next plates are in front of everyone, it turns to the Academy with the weary intimacy of people who have already spent enough weeks learning its habits to complain about them accurately. Rashid maintains that every lamp-globe in the quadrangle has been placed to illuminate a different puddle. Caelindra says this is impossible, since three of the globes illuminate only marble and their own self-importance. Aldric begins to defend the original design, loses the argument somewhere between wind direction and drainage, and ends up conceding that the west path is indeed an ambush after rain.

Ysolde, who has been watching the storm beyond the windows, lifts her head.

"The west path will be dry before dawn," she says. "The wind has turned."

"You can tell that from here?" Rashid asks.

For the first time since dinner began, Ysolde looks not merely attentive but interested. She sets one pale hand against the cool window glass.

"The light bridge is vibrating differently. It carries the gusts. The water below is moving east, though you cannot see it in this dark. In an hour, the cloud shelf will break under the gardens." She pauses, apparently noticing the number of faces turned toward her. "It is not difficult."

"That is what people say immediately before describing a thing no one else has spent thirty years learning," Caelindra says.

Ysolde considers this. "Perhaps."

Seraphel's wings rustle with a quiet laugh.

"The storm has manners\nthat we have not learned.\nIt need not apologize."

"It has killed two of my umbrellas," Rashid says. "I consider that a personal campaign."

"Your umbrellas lacked discipline," Ashara tells him.

Thessaly has arranged the crumbs from her bread into a narrow line beside her plate. "Did they surrender in writing?" she asks.

Rashid puts a hand to his chest. "Thessaly, I am wounded."

"That is not an answer."

By the time the plates have been cleared, the dinner has found the ordinary pace of people who have eaten together before and do not require each pause to mean anything. Seraphel tells a brief, rueful story in clipped verse about mistaking a chapel rehearsal for a lecture and spending twenty minutes standing in the wrong place with a hymnal. Aldric tries to excuse the chapel's signage; Seraphel points out, gently and in meter, that the sign had named the room correctly and she had simply not read it. Ashara asks whether the hymn was at least good. Seraphel says it was very good, which makes the embarrassment worse.

Fresh plates of pear cake arrive, glossy with honey and dark spice. The Academy server has just set down a pot of tea when movement in the common room catches at the edge of the open doors.

Three students have come in from the garden path, shedding the damp from their coats. One of them is a young demon woman with a narrow black splint wrapped from palm to forearm. She carries a lacquered card box under her uninjured hand. The two with her take possession of a study table near the fire, moving books into a neat stack and pulling chairs close before the coals.

"Rashid," the woman says when she sees him. Her tone is matter-of-fact, not raised for the whole room. "We found the good deck."

Rashid's hand stops over his teacup. For an instant, his eyes go from the card box to Ashara, then back again.

"Dena. I should have told you I had drills after dinner. Ashara and I need the octagon for half an hour."

Ashara turns toward him. The glow at her throat has gone a shade brighter against the white collar of her coat.

"You said you were free."

"I was trying to be free twice." He rises, smoothing one hand over the front of his sleeve. "That has not worked as well as planned."

Dena shifts the card box against her hip. Her gaze flicks once to the splint, then returns to him.

"We can begin without you. We came for cards, not a committee meeting about your calendar."

There is no softness in the sentence, but neither is there a demand beneath it.

Rashid nods once. "I'll come as soon as we're done."

"Then come as soon as you're done," Dena says. "And don't spend the first five minutes apologizing."

One of her friends snorts into his sleeve. Dena takes the open chair nearest the fire, sets the box on the table, and begins shuffling with practiced care around the splint.

Ashara stands. Her chair moves back without a scrape.

"Half an hour," she says to Rashid. "If you intend to divide yourself, at least keep time honestly."

"A lesson already," he says.

"Several, if you survive them."

They leave by the garden doors together, Ashara's dark tail making one brief line through the lamplight behind her and Rashid falling into step at her shoulder. Cold air slips into the dining hall before the doors close again.

In the common room, Dena deals the first hand. Her two companions bend over their cards. The fourth chair remains empty beside the hearth, its leather cushion catching the firelight. At the long dining table, pear cake and tea still wait among the candle stubs, while Seraphel watches the card game with open interest and Thessaly begins, very quietly, to count the suits in the deck.
