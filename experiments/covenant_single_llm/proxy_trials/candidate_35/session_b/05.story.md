Pell shifts his cards out of the way while you take the empty chair. The felt beneath the deck is old enough to have worn smooth at the places where elbows usually rest. Dena waits until the tea has been set safely beyond its edge before she begins dealing, one-handed but without fumbling: a card slides from the box, turns under the side of her palm, and lands squarely in front of each player.

"Questions are better than confidence," she says. "Confidence is how people play a key on the first trick and spend the rest of the hand explaining why it was strategic."

"It can be strategic," Maera says.

"It can also be loud."

The new cards make a small, bright geography on the felt. Black stone and green leaf are familiar now; there is a white-light card marked with a narrow tower, blue tide with a boat no larger than a fingernail, and two gold keys whose painted teeth point in different directions. Pell taps one of the keys with a single claw.

"They are good cards," he says. "Which means they are dangerous cards."

Dena gives the first explanation as the hand begins, stopping only long enough to answer the rule that becomes necessary. A card must follow the route already led if it can. If no matching suit is held, any ordinary card may be discarded. A key can change which earlier suit decides the trick, but it cannot retrieve a card already swept into a crossing, and it is rarely kind to the person who plays it.

"If you do not know who the change will help," Dena says, "keep it."

"This is excellent advice outside cards," Pell says.

"Do not make it into advice outside cards."

He makes an exaggerated show of returning his attention to his hand.

Pell leads black stone. Its painted arch is the tallest card on the table, and the low black card in your own hand follows it without changing the result. He takes the first trick with satisfaction restrained just enough to make Maera roll her eyes.

"One crossing," he says.

"One crossing that points at a wall," Maera says. "Do not start naming streets."

The second trick comes from Maera: blue tide, its small boat tilted against a wash of silver paint. This time a higher blue card carries the trick, and Dena slides the three played cards toward your side of the felt.

"Keep those faceup," she says. "A crossing is only useful if you can join it to another one."

The marker placed beside it is a little brass arch, cool and heavy enough to make a soft sound against the table. Thessaly, still standing behind the chairs, watches it settle.

"It is a bridge with no other end," she says.

"For now," Dena says.

The next two tricks take some of the ease out of the hand. Dena wins a green-leaf route with an unshowy high card. Pell tries to turn the fourth with a key, then finds Maera has held the card that makes his new route useless. He looks at the key as though its behavior has become a personal insult.

"That was a perfectly defensible play," he says.

"Defend it, then," Maera says.

"I would rather learn from it."

"A rare act of courage."

On the last trick, a white-light card lies led in the center. There is no matching white among the cards left in your hand. Dena answers the necessary point of the rule plainly: an ordinary discard leaves the trick where it is, while a key could alter the route but would give its player no guarantee of winning it. The gold card remains where it is, and a low green leaf goes down instead.

Pell's white tower takes the trick. He gathers it with less ceremony this time. It gives him a second crossing, but not the third he needs. Dena has three markers by the end of the hand, two linked by a route of green and one by the blue card that had been won earlier.

"That is mine," she says, collecting her markers with the same careful movement she used to deal. "By a narrow and entirely respectable margin."

"You had no margin at all," Maera says. "You had a storm card and an injured opponent."

"Pell is not injured."

"His pride is."

Pell puts a hand over his heart, then winces as Dena flicks one of the unused keys toward him.

"You held yours," she says, looking across the table. "That was sensible. You had no route worth changing yet. The key will still be there when you do."

Thessaly considers the cards remaining on the felt. "It is strange that patience looks identical to losing until the next hand."

"That is why people enjoy it," Dena says.

The first round ends with Dena's route laid neatly before her and the other crossings returned to the box. She squares the deck against the table, the fire throwing gold across the cards' worn edges. Around the common room, the evening has settled into its separate pockets of sound: the low hiss of the hearth, cups being cleared in the dining hall, the distant storm worrying at the windows, and the quiet shuffle of a deck ready to begin again.
