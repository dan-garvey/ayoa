Dena looks up from the cards. In the firelight, the splint on her wrist is less conspicuous than the quick appraisal in her dark eyes. Then she shifts the card box a little farther from the table's edge and makes room at the side of it.

"Watch all you like," she says. "I am Dena Arl. The one pretending he doesn't care whether he wins is Pell, and the one enjoying the pretense is Maera."

Pell, a broad-shouldered young man with a short row of brass scales disappearing beneath his collar, gives a modest nod that fails to convince anyone. Maera's pointed ears twitch above a loose fall of dark hair as she lifts her cards in greeting.

"We did hear there was a Rowan," Maera says. "It is nicer to meet one than hear people lower their voices about him at supper."

"That is the kindest version of the rumor," Pell says.

Dena gives him a look. "Play your hand."

The game is called Crossings. Its cards are painted in seven narrow suits: black stone, white light, red flame, green leaf, silver wing, blue tide, and a seventh made of little gold keys. The illustrations have been worn smooth at the corners by years of handling. Each player is building a route across three tricks. A high card takes a trick, but only a card of the suit first led can win it; the key cards change the suit after they are played, and a storm card can wipe out a route already claimed. It has the clean, treacherous simplicity of something designed by people who enjoy watching confidence become a problem.

"The keys are not a suit," Thessaly says from behind your shoulder.

She has brought her tea in with her and stands just outside the little circle of chairs, both hands around the cup.

"They are printed as a suit," Maera says.

"They do not behave as one. That seems relevant."

Dena's mouth twitches. "This is why Thessaly is forbidden to explain games to visitors before they have enjoyed themselves."

"No one has forbidden me," Thessaly says. "They have requested it with unusual force."

Dena leads the first trick with a green leaf card, its painted vine curling around a tiny walled tower. Pell follows with a lower green. Maera lays a key card instead, a gold arch picked out in bright paint. The suit shifts; Dena's fingers pause over the cards in her hand.

"Too early," Pell says.

"You say that every time I do something you didn't predict," Maera replies.

"That is because it is often too early."

"Often is a fragile word."

The last card of the trick lands with a quiet slap. Dena has chosen blue tide, but the key has already turned the route. Maera's card takes it. She draws the three cards into a neat pile with the side of her wrist rather than asking anyone to do it for her.

"One crossing," Maera says.

"An unstable crossing," Dena says. "There is a storm still in the deck."

"There are always storms still in the deck," Pell says.

"That has never stopped you from building things too high."

For the next few turns, the game gives them a language more immediate than the polite one at dinner. Pell gathers black-stone cards, makes an ambitious route, and loses it when Dena produces a storm painted with a green-white burst over the Nexus. He stares at the card as if he might persuade it to take back its decision.

"That is personal," he says.

"It is cardboard," Dena says.

"It knows what it did."

Maera laughs into her sleeve. Thessaly does not laugh, but her eyes follow the discarded storm card until Dena sweeps it into the center pile.

The last trick comes down to three cards held close against the felt. Dena has two crossings; Maera has one, though hers is still protected by the key she played first. Pell has nothing but an impressive spread of cards he can no longer use. He lays a silver-wing card with unnecessary dignity. Maera follows with red flame.

Dena looks at the remaining card in her hand for a beat. It is another key.

"That cannot take the trick," Pell says.

"It can change who takes it," Thessaly says.

Dena plays the key beside Pell's silver wing. The painted arch flashes in the lamp glow. What had been a wing-led route becomes gold-keyed instead, and Maera's flame no longer has any claim on it. Pell's card is highest among the new suit by the game's strange arithmetic. He wins the trick, blinks at it, and then realizes what Dena has done.

"You gave that to me," he says.

"I denied Maera her second route," Dena says. "You are collateral pleasure."

"That is almost affectionate."

"Do not make it embarrassing."

Pell gathers the final cards with a grin. The round belongs to Dena by the two crossings she has already secured, but Maera makes a show of counting every marker before conceding it.

"A narrow victory," she says.

"The only kind worth having," Dena replies.

From beyond the common-room windows comes the distant, softened pulse of the storm. Somewhere outside, too far away to distinguish clearly, there is a sharp impact followed by another; the octagon's practice blades, perhaps, or a door caught in the wind. The card table stays warm within its circle of firelight.

Dena squares the deck against the felt, then slides it toward the empty chair without touching the chair itself.

"The next hand starts clean. The rules make more sense once they have had a chance to misbehave."
