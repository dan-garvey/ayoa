The framed letter above the desk has been waiting longer than anyone in this house has expected a human to read it. Its ink has faded to the color of tea, but the address is still clear beneath the glass: a dead Garvey aunt writing to a great-nephew who never came. The made bed, the clothes sent ahead by lawyers, and the room's generous view toward the gardens all have the same patient, prepared-for quality.

The inherited signet on your hand catches the lamplight.

Below, the dining hall is beginning to sound occupied. Chairs scrape over the floorboards. A voice says, "There are seventeen places. Why are there seventeen places?"

"Because that is how many the table has," another replies.

"An answer with no imagination."

A woman laughs once, short and unembarrassed. "You asked a question with no difficulty, Rashid."

Another voice says, "Food is not improved by counting it."

A third answers in short, careful verse.

"Let the first meal be warm.
We have enough ceremonies."

The words settle into the hall with the light rustle of wings.

A knock sounds at your door. An Academy server waits on the landing in grey livery, one hand held politely clear of the frame.

"Mr. Garvey," she says, "they've begun seating."
