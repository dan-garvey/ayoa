The bread tears open with a faint crackle, sending up a thread of steam that catches the candlelight before it disappears. The conversation about Councils and obligations does not vanish so much as settle among the empty bowls, where no one feels obliged to pick it up again at once.

Rashid reaches for another piece of bread and says, “The rankings board is still too low.”

Ashara does not look up from cutting a glazed root vegetable. “You have made that claim three times.”

“It has not become less true through repetition. The bottom rows are at the height of runoff. Anyone checking their rank after rain has to crouch in the mud.”

“Then they will develop stronger legs.”

A smile passes around the table. Even Aldric’s expression shifts before he smooths it away.

“The board will be dry tomorrow,” Ysolde says.

Rashid turns toward her. “You sound certain.”

“The storm is moving east. It will leave wind along the lower terraces after noon, though.” Her eyes rest on the dark windows rather than on anyone’s face. “The octagon will be dry by the time of the bout. The far edge may not be.”

Ashara sets down her fork. “It will be fine.”

“It will be uneven,” Ysolde says. “That is not the same thing.”

For several moments, she describes the movement of the storm as it passes beneath the Nexus: where it catches against the cliffs, how the bridges make the air break and reform, why the eastern side of the octagon takes the last of the damp. There is no performance in it. She speaks with the calm concentration of someone explaining a place she has watched for years, and when she stops, the candle flames seem briefly too still by comparison.

Caelindra leans back in her chair, silver hair slipping over one shoulder. “The Academy has built six roads from light over a permanent storm and cannot raise a noticeboard above a puddle.”

“The light roads are not maintained by the same people,” Ysolde says.

“That is precisely the problem.”

Thessaly folds a corner of her napkin between two careful fingers. “A board that remains upright has fulfilled one promise. A board that can be read has fulfilled another.”

Rashid looks at her. “You have found a way to make this sound like legal testimony.”

“It is not testimony. No one has sworn to tell the truth about the board.”

Seraphel’s mouth curves over her water glass.

“A splendid school may build  
a bridge across the dark  
and lose a fight with rain.”

Ashara’s tail gives one quick movement against the chair leg. “That is better than it deserves.”

“It is kinder than the board has been to me,” Rashid says.

“The board cannot harm you.”

“You have not stood beneath it in weather.”

The Academy server returns with a shallow dish of baked plums and a small pitcher of cream. The interruption turns the table toward fruit, toward the question of whether the kitchen has used too much clove, toward a story from Aldric about a chapel lantern that went out during a memorial service and had to be relit from the kitchen hearth because every ritual flame in the building refused to catch. He tells it with more embarrassment than grandeur, which makes Rashid laugh openly.

“You are saying,” Rashid says, “that the Divine Light was defeated by bad oil?”

“I am saying that the groundskeeper had filled the lamps himself and did not appreciate the comparison.”

“He ought to have,” Caelindra says. “It is useful to know one’s proper adversaries.”

The talk moves on before Aldric can decide whether to take offense. The new subject is tomorrow’s matches, then the odd assortment of people who use the octagon when no ranking is at stake. Ashara asks Rashid whether his informal practice group is still paused.

“For now,” he says. “Someone needs time to mend. The rest can survive a week without being instructed to hold a blade.”

“They will invent worse habits without you,” Ashara says.

“They have managed before.”

“You say that as if you believe it.”

“I say it as if I am trying to.”

There is no speech after that about reform or injury or obligation. Rashid reaches for the cream, then catches the pitcher before it can tip toward Seraphel’s sleeve. She gives him a small nod of thanks. Across the table, Caelindra begins an exacting complaint about the texture of the plums, and Ysolde listens with the same attention she gave the storm, though her face gives no sign that the matter concerns her.

By the time the serving dishes have been cleared again, the first quarter hour has passed almost without notice. The candles have shortened in their cups. Beyond the open dining-hall doors, the common room has acquired a quieter life of its own: three students have taken a table near the fireplace, cards spread between them. One is a demon woman with a splinted wrist resting beside her cup; she gathers her hand with the other while one of her companions deals.

Rashid sees the table as he rises. His gaze lingers there only a moment before Ashara stands as well, already drawing her coat closed over one shoulder.

“The octagon,” she says.

“You will have your half hour,” Rashid replies.

They leave by the garden door, their voices carrying back only as indistinct fragments of the argument they have resumed. At the card table, a hand comes down hard enough to make the brass lamp tremble. The dining hall has not emptied so much as loosened around its doors, its lingering plates, and the separate evenings beginning within the house.
