For a while, the dinner has the good sense to remain dinner.

The fresh bread breaks with a crisp little crack beneath your fingers, its center warm and sour. A server brings a platter of glazed roots and a dish of dark greens slicked with butter. Across the table, Rashid reaches for the pepper sauce at the same moment Ashara does.

“Tomorrow,” he says, “I intend to demonstrate that the rankings board has no authority over me.”

“It records authority,” Ashara says. “It does not possess it.”

“An important distinction.”

“You seem to be collecting them.”

Caelindra examines a square of roasted squash as though it has personally disappointed her. “The kitchen has buried perfectly adequate vegetables beneath honey.”

“It is not honey,” Ysolde says. “It is reduced pear.”

Caelindra looks up. “That improves nothing.”

“The pears have suffered,” Seraphel says softly,

“for an error of confidence.”

Rashid laughs into his cup. Thessaly takes another piece of squash.

“I have eaten two,” she says. “They have caused me no injury.”

“That is not praise,” Caelindra tells her.

“No. It is useful information.”

The exchange carries on without needing anything from you. Ashara and Rashid argue over whether a ladder counts as preparation for a duel; Ysolde says that rain will reach the lower part of the octagon by morning if the wind keeps turning east. When Rashid asks how she can possibly know that, she looks through the window at the storm below the plateau.

“The western edge is thinning,” she says. “It does that before it shifts.”

“You make weather sound like an opponent with habits,” he says.

“It has habits.”

“Can you defeat it formally?” Caelindra asks.

Ysolde considers this. “No.”

That wins a small, surprised laugh from Ashara. It changes her whole face for the instant it lasts.

The next quarter hour settles into its own loose rhythm. Plates empty and are replaced; candles burn lower in their brass cups. Seraphel listens more than she speaks, but when Rashid tells a story about a Military instructor who called a collapsed practice target an exercise in adaptive terrain, she presses her lips together before offering,

“A ruined target  
is still a target  
if someone must explain it.”

“Precisely,” Rashid says. “You understand the educational philosophy.”

“I fear I do.”

Aldric objects that the Academy has not entirely abandoned standards. Caelindra asks whether standards are what they call bad repairs when they have been written into a handbook. He looks briefly affronted, then smiles despite himself.

Outside, lightning opens beneath the bridges in pale, noiseless veins. The glass at the windows catches it and gives everyone at the table a second, ghostly outline. Ysolde watches the storm with more attention than the argument. Her untouched water glass beads faintly beneath her hand.

“At dawn,” she says, apparently continuing a thought no one else knew she had begun, “the upper cloud layer can look solid enough to walk on. When I was small, I tried.”

Rashid turns toward her. “And?”

“I fell through it.”

Ashara’s tail makes one slow curve behind her chair. “A valuable lesson.”

“It was,” Ysolde says. “I learned not to trust surfaces which resemble floors.”

Thessaly glances at the long varnished table. “A principle with several applications.”

The chapel bell sounds then, muted by the walls and weather but distinct enough to lift Aldric’s eyes. He sets down his cup.

“I am due at evening prayer.”

“May it ask less of you,” Seraphel says,

“than the bell implies.”

Aldric’s mouth softens. “I will endeavor to ask less of it as well.”

He inclines his head around the table and goes, white-and-gold cloth passing through the open dining-room doors before the common room swallows the sound of his steps.

Dessert arrives: small pear cakes glazed to a shine Caelindra regards with renewed suspicion. Before anyone can decide whether this constitutes a second offense, a deck of cards snaps sharply in the common room.

A young demon woman stands at the dining-hall threshold with a deck tucked under one arm. Her other wrist is held in a neat black splint. Two students linger behind her near the fireplace, arranging chairs around a low table.

“Rashid,” she says, “are you coming, or should we deal you an insulting hand?”

Rashid’s expression changes by degrees. His shoulders square first; then he looks toward Ashara.

“I owe Ashara half an hour at the octagon,” he says. “I should have told you before you came.”

“You should have,” the woman agrees. There is no particular anger in it, which makes the answer land more cleanly. “We came to play cards, not hold a meeting about your calendar. Start without him.”

One of the students behind her begins shuffling. The cards make a dry, quick sound.

Ashara sets down her fork. “You promised them yesterday?”

Rashid’s courtesy has gone careful around the edges. “Yes.”

“And accepted drills today.”

“Yes.”

For a moment, only the shuffling continues.

“The half hour has not become shorter because you made two promises,” Ashara says.

“No.” He looks toward the common room again. “Dena, deal me in when I arrive. Do not wait.”

Dena gives him a look over the deck. “Bring something worth taking from you.”

“I will attempt to.”

Ashara rises. Heat seems to travel with her as she steps back from the table. “Then come along. You can be late honestly after you have finished being corrected.”

Rashid’s mouth twitches. “A generous interpretation of our practice.”

“It is the one you have earned.”

They leave together through the common room, Ashara’s dark hair and swept horns briefly visible beyond the doorway, Rashid close beside her. Dena turns back to her friends without waiting for a better ending to the exchange.

The first hand begins amid quiet accusations of cheating. At the table, the pear cakes cool under their glaze, the storm keeps moving beneath the bridges, and Garvey House gathers the separate shapes of the evening around itself.
