That earns a small, surprised laugh from Seraphel, and a less surprised one from Rashid.

“Four years are long  
only when counted all at once.  
Supper is shorter.”

“Encouraging philosophy,” Rashid says. “I approve especially of the final point.”

“Of course you do,” Ashara says.

You break off a piece of the dark bread. Steam lifts from its soft center, carrying butter and the sharper scent of pepper from the sauce boat. With the food moving around the table and the bowls steadily emptying, nobody treats your silence as a space that needs to be filled.

The next quarter hour does not become an inquest. No one asks you to account for the name on your signet, or for the generations between the last human seat and this dinner. The talk takes smaller channels.

Rashid returns to the rankings board with the persistence of someone who has already rehearsed the complaint.

“It’s tilted,” he says. “And the rain has blurred the bottom third.”

“Your name remains beneath mine,” Ashara says. “The board has preserved its most important information.”

“It’s an unreliable witness.”

“It is a wooden board.”

Caelindra sets down her wineglass. “How reassuring. Tomorrow’s contest will be governed by carpentry.”

“It will be governed by footwork,” Ysolde says. “The board merely gets wet.”

Rashid turns toward her. “Thank you. Between you and Ashara, I feel entirely equipped to address the problem.”

“You could bring a cloth,” Ysolde says.

For an instant, Caelindra looks as though she expects a joke to follow. None does.

Then Thessaly says, “Does victory provide money?”

“Not usually,” Rashid says.

“Then the board is an expensive way to distribute public advice.”

“Bragging rights,” he says.

“Ah.” Thessaly nods once. “A currency.”

Ashara’s tail taps once against the chair leg. “You would understand that.”

“I understand useless things can still be valuable. That is why I attend some lectures.”

Seraphel covers her smile with her hand.

The weather comes up because Ysolde notices it first. One of the garden lamps beyond the high windows has taken on a faint greenish cast, almost invisible against the storm-light below the plateau.

“The west bridge will close before midnight,” she says.

Aldric glances toward the window. “There has been no notice.”

“There will be.”

Caelindra follows Ysolde’s gaze. “You can tell that from a lamp?”

“The outer lenses shift before the pressure reaches us.” Ysolde’s attention remains on the thin green edge of light. “The storm is turning beneath the western span. It will lay frost on the parapets first, then make the bridge surface slick.”

Rashid leans just far enough to see past the candles. “That does explain the board.”

“It does not excuse it,” Ashara says.

“No,” Ysolde agrees. “It explains it.”

Seraphel’s voice comes lightly into the pause.

“The weather wins  
most arguments  
without needing applause.”

This time even Caelindra laughs, though she hides it behind the rim of her glass.

The Academy server clears the last of the main plates and replaces them with small dishes of poached pear, dark syrup shining around the fruit. The formal brightness of the table has softened under crumbs, used glasses, and the loosened arrangement of people who have stopped performing a welcome dinner quite so carefully.

Ashara looks toward the narrow clock on the sideboard.

“Rashid,” she says. “The octagon. Half an hour.”

“I know.”

“Immediately after this.”

“Yes.” He reaches for his napkin, then pauses. “After that, I need to be in the common room.”

Ashara’s eyes settle on him. “For what?”

“Cards. Dena and two people from the practice group. I said I’d be there after dinner.”

The words sit plainly between them.

“You agreed to train with me after you had already agreed to that.”

“Yes.”

“And you neglected to mention it.”

“I thought I could do both.”

Ashara’s expression does not change, though her hand closes around the stem of her glass. “Dena is not a gap in your calendar.”

“No,” Rashid says. The answer comes quieter. “She isn’t.”

Thessaly takes a bite of pear. “A delay named beforehand is less irritating than one discovered by waiting.”

“A delay is still irritating,” Caelindra says.

“It can be both things.”

Seraphel looks between Rashid and Ashara.

“Two rooms may wait  
more kindly  
when they know why.”

Rashid exhales through his nose. “You have all made a modest error in scheduling into a tribunal.”

“A tribunal has paperwork,” Ashara says. “Send a message.”

He does not argue. From an inner pocket, he takes a small notebook and pencil, writes quickly on a clean page, then folds it once. When the server returns with the dessert tray, he asks her to have it taken to Dena Arl in the common room. She accepts it without comment.

Ashara rises when the server has gone.

“Practice blades,” she says. “I am not explaining scorched flooring to anyone tonight.”

“As considerate as ever,” Rashid says, getting to his feet.

“It preserves the house.”

Ysolde watches the green-edged lamp beyond the window. “The ground will be wet.”

“I have noticed,” Ashara says.

Rashid gives the table a brief, rueful look before following her toward the door. Their chairs remain slightly askew behind them, the first clear break in the circle of the dinner.

The dining hall begins to become a room again rather than an occasion. A note is already making its way toward the common room, the octagon waits somewhere beyond the warm walls of Garvey House, and the remaining candles burn steadily over the long table.
