Seraphel inclines her head. The feathers at her shoulders shift with the motion, catching the stairwell lamp.

“Then Rowan, as you wish.  
A house grows formal enough  
without help from its guests.”

She keeps pace beside you down the stairs. Below, the sounds from the dining hall separate into chairs, cutlery, and a conversation already underway. A server in blue Academy livery crosses the lower landing with a covered platter balanced on one arm. Butter, pepper, and the sweet dark edge of something roasted have warmed the whole floor.

The dining hall doors stand open. Seven people occupy a close stretch of the long varnished table, leaving most of its seventeen places shining empty in the candlelight. The portraits on the paneled walls have been left to their silence. Here, the living people have gathered near the serving dishes.

Seraphel touches the back of the chair beside hers.

Your smile brings the conversation to a brief pause.

“Thank you for having me.”

The gold-haired young man opposite rises halfway from his chair before seeming to decide the gesture has become too much. “Welcome to Garvey House, Rowan. Aldric Verantus.”

The young man at his right has amber eyes and a collar so precisely set that even the dark fur at his wrists looks orderly. He slides a basket of warm bread away from his own plate. “Rashid vel Amara. Your place has been defended admirably.”

“You mean unsuccessfully,” says the horned woman beside him.

Her black-red horns sweep back from her severely tied hair. She is taller than anyone seated, even before them, and the end of her tail rests neatly around one chair leg. “Ashara vel Kothren.”

Across the table, a white-haired woman has moved a candle chimney a finger’s width farther from the edge of the runner. “Ysolde Thornmantle.”

“Caelindra Vaeyn.” The elf’s silver hair has been arranged with such care that the candlelight seems to have been assigned a place in it. Her violet eyes touch the signet on your hand once before returning to her plate.

The smallest person at the table lifts her glass. Her eyes are entirely black, reflecting both flame and window-darkness. “Thessaly Morrow. The kitchen deserves most of the gratitude, but it is not seated.”

Seraphel takes her chair again.

“You know my name already.  
That seems a kindness  
in a house full of them.”

A server sets down a broad glazed dish of dark roasted vegetables, a copper pan of peppered lentils, and a little crock of green sauce. The serving spoon moves around the table in a practiced direction. When it reaches you, you follow the rhythm of the others: serve, pass, make room for the next dish. The plate warms your hands through the charcoal sleeves.

For a little while, the table concerns itself with supper.

Rashid tastes the sauce, reaches for the salt, and finds Ashara looking at him.

“It needs it,” he says.

“It has salt.”

“It has the memory of salt.”

Caelindra takes the cellar from him before he can use it. “You are both mistaken. It needs lemon.”

“The lemons are by your elbow,” Ysolde says.

Caelindra looks down. A small dish of pale slices has indeed been placed there.

Thessaly tears a piece of bread in half. “The cook told me the sauce was meant to be sharp. She sounded certain of it.”

Aldric lifts his glass. “Then perhaps we can honor her certainty without turning seasoning into doctrine.”

Seraphel’s mouth curves.

“Peace survives by butter,  
by bread,  
and by people pretending  
they did not want a second helping.”

Rashid laughs first. Ashara’s tail taps once against the chair leg.

Names begin to settle into the small things people do with them. Rashid remembers to pass a dish before anyone asks. Ashara eats quickly but not carelessly, listening with her head slightly inclined when someone else has the floor. Ysolde takes lemon with her lentils and watches a drop of condensation travel down her water glass. Caelindra finds the sauce acceptable after all and says nothing about it. Thessaly has claimed the heel of the bread loaf with quiet efficiency. Aldric keeps trying to make the meal feel ceremonious without quite making it solemn.

Before the first dishes are empty, Ashara turns to Rashid.

“The octagon after dinner. Half an hour.”

“I remember.”

“I am checking.”

“I told Dena and the others I’d join them in the common room afterward. They can deal a first hand without me.”

Ashara sets down her fork. “You made another arrangement before accepting mine.”

“Before, yes.”

“Then you should have said so.”

“I am saying so.”

“Now.”

“Now.”

Thessaly looks from one of them to the other. “That is late notice by any ordinary measure.”

“The north garden path is dry,” Ysolde says. “It will save time.”

Rashid turns toward her. “There. A solution.”

“It saves perhaps a minute,” Ysolde says.

“A valuable minute.”

Ashara takes another piece of bread. “Half an hour, Rashid.”

“Half an hour.”

Seraphel breaks off a corner of her own bread.

“Minutes are small creatures.  
They bite hardest  
when everyone has named them.”

The sauce makes another slow circuit of the table. Outside the tall windows, the first lamps along the garden paths brighten against the storm.
