For a while, nobody asks anything more of you. The bread breaks warm beneath your fingers, its dark crust giving way to steam, and you follow the dishes as they make their slow circuit around the table. The pepper sauce stings pleasantly beside the saffron rice. Beyond the windows, lightning whitens the garden walls and vanishes.

Rashid sets down his fork. “That rankings board is going to come down before noon tomorrow.”

“It is not,” Ashara says.

“It’s leaning.”

Ysolde looks up from her plate. “By the width of a thumb.”

Rashid turns toward her. “You measured it?”

“I walked past it on Tuesday.”

Caelindra’s ears shift beneath their amber pins. “A moving account of civic vigilance.”

“It was in my way,” Ysolde says.

Aldric lifts his glass. “I can ask the grounds office to have it repaired.”

“After the bout,” Ashara says.

Rashid’s smile shows briefly. “A remarkably strategic deadline.”

Seraphel’s wings rustle against the back of her chair.

“The weather does not choose,” Ysolde says.

“It still has effects,” Thessaly replies.

That earns a quiet laugh from Rashid. The conversation carries on from there, no longer circling the empty Council seat or the signet on your hand. A server replaces the cleared bowls with small plates of pear tart, the pastry glazed until it catches the candlelight.

Caelindra studies hers with the concentration she had given the carrots. “At last. Something here understands its purpose.”

“A tart?” Rashid says.

“A tart requires no committee, no charter, and no speech about restoration. It knows what it is.”

Thessaly considers this. “Enviable.”

Seraphel lowers her eyes to hide a smile.

“That may be the first kindness  
said of the Academy  
all evening.”

“I praised the soup,” Rashid says.

“You said it contained salt,” Ashara tells him.

“Which it did.”

Ysolde cuts into the tart. “The pears come from the south garden wall. The stone holds heat after storms.”

Aldric glances toward the windows. “I did not know that.”

“You do not visit the gardens in autumn.”

“That appears to be true.”

For several minutes, that is enough: the pear trees trained flat against pale stone, the unreasonable amount of rain that has reached the octagon’s noticeboard, a first-week navigation exercise Rashid insists he nearly won and Ashara insists he barely survived. Caelindra interrupts only to correct his use of the word nearly. Even the table’s silences settle easily among the clink of cutlery and the low movement of servants at the sideboard.

When the tart plates are half-empty, Ashara wipes a trace of syrup from her thumb with her napkin.

“The octagon after dinner,” she says to Rashid. “Half an hour.”

“Half an hour,” he agrees. Then his expression changes by a degree as he looks toward the open doors of the common room. “After that, I’m late to cards with Dena and the others. I promised them the first half-hour.”

Ashara’s gold eyes rest on him. “You should tell them.”

“I will.”

“They should not have to discover it by waiting.”

“I know.” His courtesy has sharpened, though he keeps his voice low. “They can begin without me. I should still tell them.”

Caelindra takes another bite of tart. “How fortunate that this revelation has arrived before you appear at the table with an apology and no useful explanation.”

“It has been an evening of fortunate revelations,” Rashid says.

“It has been an evening of obvious ones,” Ashara replies.

Thessaly folds her napkin along its crease. “Those are often the ones people postpone.”

Rashid exhales through his nose, then inclines his head once toward Ashara. “Octagon first. Cards afterward. I have understood the terms.”

“They were not terms,” Thessaly says.

“No,” he says. “That is somehow worse.”

Seraphel looks from him to you, her expression gentler than the candlelight on the table.
