## Submission 0

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

The staff staircase brings you down beside the café counter, where supper has made a small island in the middle of the unfinished foyer. Opening programmes and a carton of cups have been pushed to one end of the joined tables. Eight places are set among bowls, breadboards, and a pie dish wrapped in a tea towel.

Nell looks up from the office doorway. “Good. You found the route.”

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

A woman with flour on one sleeve sets down a broad blue bowl that steams with tomato and chickpeas. “Jo. I made too much food, so don’t be polite about it.”

“Owen,” says the man carrying the pie. He clears a place between Bea and himself with the edge of his forearm. “This end gets the bread before Martin develops a theory about it.”

“Martin,” the older man says. “And I’ve never had to develop a theory about bread. It’s one of the few things in life that arrives with instructions.”

“Rosa,” says the woman beside him, folding a dark fringe behind one ear. “He means butter.”

Bea gives you a small lift of her hand. “Bea. I’m the one who tells people they can’t afford their more interesting ideas.”

“That’s slander,” Nell says, taking the chair at the end of the table. “You approve plenty of interesting ideas.”

“Eventually.”

You settle into the open place. The dishes begin moving around the table in an easy, practiced circuit: bread, salad, stew, pie, then the bowl of grated cheese that Jo insists belongs with the stew despite Nell’s expression. You follow the traffic of plates and serving spoons. The stew is thick with soft chickpeas and a little heat at the back of the tongue; the pie flakes apart under the fork.

For the first few minutes, the conversation does not require anything of you.

“These are still far too large,” Bea says, looking at the salad bowls.

“They’re the small ones,” Jo says.

“That is a worrying distinction.”

Martin tears off a piece of bread. “The old café had bowls you could lose a child in.”

“The old café also had an urn that tasted faintly of metal,” Rosa says.

“It was reliable.”

“It was brown.”

“It made tea.”

Nell’s mouth twitches over her glass. “A persuasive defence.”

Rosa reaches for the salad. “At seven, Martin and I are photographing six of the boards before he starts rewriting their history.”

“I’m preserving it,” Martin says. “There’s a difference.”

“The red one was painted by Pearl Kaye,” he adds, more firmly. “Not me. Mine was the blue lettering on the one beneath it. Somebody wrote over the date later, badly.”

“It’s all in the notes,” Rosa says. “Including Pearl Kaye.”

Bea nods once, satisfied. “Good.”

The staff stair complains overhead. A moment later Dev comes down with a black equipment case in one hand and his phone in the other. He pauses when he sees the full table.

“Dev,” he says. “Sorry. Booth problem that turned out to be a cable pretending it had principles.”

“There’s stew,” Jo tells him. “Sit.”

He takes the remaining chair, putting the case carefully beneath it. His phone lights against the table. He reads the screen, then looks to Nell.

“I’m telling Hana not to come,” he says. “I should’ve checked the diary before I rang her. Rosa and Martin have the room.”

Nell sets down her fork. “Thank you.”

“Hana’s got another call later. We can test the receivers properly another evening.”

“You’ll have a booked hour,” Nell says. “We’ll put it in the diary tomorrow before anyone gets inventive.”

Dev gives a brief, sheepish nod. “Fair.”

Rosa raises her glass a fraction in his direction. “Thank you.”

Martin leans back. “There’ll be plenty of seats for you to walk round next week. Some of them even work.”

“They all work,” Dev says.

“That’s not what I said.”

Jo reaches across for the breadboard. “Nobody is discussing whether the seats work while I’m trying to feed them.”

Outside the glazed front doors, Harbour Street is turning blue at the edges. A gull lands on the pavement, considers the cinema through the glass, and struts away toward the harbour.

Owen cuts another piece of pie and glances across the table. “Have you been to Lowhaven before?”

## Submission 1

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

Owen’s question has barely settled before Jo sends the breadboard along with an instruction to keep it moving.

“The pie’s not decorative,” she says. “Neither is the salad, despite appearances.”

The bread comes to you, then the water jug, then the stew bowl again as Martin decides he has left too much room for dessert in a meal that has no dessert. You help keep the dishes travelling around the joined tables while the conversation finds its own course.

Rosa points her fork toward the auditorium doors. “The blue board has a whole other title underneath it. You can see the old letters where the newer paint’s gone thin.”

“That’s because there was no money for fresh boards,” Martin says. “You used what was there.”

“I know. That’s why it matters.”

Bea takes a piece of bread. “It can matter without the label sounding as if hardship was a charming decorative choice.”

Rosa nods. “I’m not writing a hymn to poor maintenance. I just want people to see that someone made the thing, then somebody else had to make it work again.”

Martin considers this, chewing slowly. “Pearl was good at that. Making room. She could fit a title across a board that ought to have been twice the size.”

“And whose was Pearl?” Dev says from beside his equipment case.

“Pearl Kaye,” Martin replies. “Worked the box office, painted signs, made sandwiches when the café girl was off sick. Still lives near Durham, last I heard.”

Rosa reaches for the notebook beside her plate and writes the name down.

Dev’s phone vibrates once against the table. He reads the message, thumbs a reply, and puts it face down beside his glass.

“Hana’s taking the hotel call,” he says. “We’ll do the receivers another night.”

“Good,” Nell says. “Tomorrow, we find a proper slot and write it in before you call anyone.”

“That’s fair.”

Jo gives Dev a look over the rim of her glass. “You can be clever with cables after you finish eating.”

“I can be clever with cables while eating.”

“No. You can spill chickpeas into a receiver while eating.”

That earns a short laugh from Owen and Bea. Dev lifts both hands from the table as if surrendering, then takes another spoonful of stew.

The talk moves to the café kitchen. Jo says the oven runs hot at the back, and Nell says she has already written the model number down for the electrician. Bea asks that nothing be ordered until she has seen the price. Jo tells her the oven can wait, but that she will not be serving anything which requires an apology before it reaches the counter.

“That sounds like a menu policy,” Owen says.

“It’s a cooking policy.”

“Much more dangerous,” Martin says.

Beyond the front doors, the light over Harbour Street has thinned to a damp blue. A pair of people pass the cinema window beneath one umbrella, their reflections briefly crossing the gold letters still being fitted above the entrance.

By the time the stew bowl has been scraped nearly clean, Rosa has checked the time twice without making a show of it. She gets up, collecting a camera case from beside the wall.

“I’m going to set the tripod before the light changes entirely,” she says.

Martin pushes his chair back. “I’ll get the first board upright.”

“Gently,” Bea says.

“I know how to carry a board.”

“You know how to carry an engine block. Those are not the same skill.”

Martin gives her a wounded look that does not delay him. Rosa takes her notebook and follows him through the auditorium doors, where the dim room beyond is beginning to take on the shape of their work.

Jo starts gathering the empty serving dishes.

“Leave the plates,” she says. “You’re all getting up at once in ten minutes and I’m not having a queue around the sink.”

Nell moves the unopened crockery carton farther beneath the counter to clear the way. Dev closes his equipment case and sets it by the office wall, out of the route to the auditorium. The table grows quieter for a moment, held by the scrape of chairs and the soft clink of Jo stacking bowls.
