Rashid walks down beside you, leaving enough room for the stair’s narrow turn. The dining hall is brighter than the common room: working lamps shine from the paneled walls, candles burn along the long varnished table, and every place has been set as though all seventeen seats might fill at once.

Seven people look up when you enter. An empty setting waits halfway down the table, between Seraphel’s white sleeve and Ysolde’s pale, still profile.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

“Welcome, Rowan Garvey,” Aldric says. He rises with an economical rustle of white and gold, then sits when Ashara gives him a look from across the table.

“You’ll make the soup nervous,” Ashara says.

Thessaly studies the empty place beside you. “The soup has survived worse ceremonies.”

Seraphel’s smile is quick.

“Then let the bread arrive  
Before the names grow larger.”

Caelindra passes the basket toward you without looking away from the server pouring wine. “The bread is the safest part of the meal.”

“The fish is safe,” Ysolde says.

“It is cooked,” Caelindra replies.

“That does not make it safe.”

Ashara laughs under her breath. Rashid takes the seat opposite you, his hands folded neatly beside his plate. The first serving dish comes around from Ysolde: trout laid over fennel and small potatoes shining with browned butter. You take a portion and pass it onward as Seraphel has done, then follow the movement of the dishes around the table.

For the first few minutes, nobody asks you to account for yourself. Silver touches china. The storm beyond the tall windows puts slow white veins through the dark below the plateau. A server brings more bread; Thessaly takes two pieces and sets one at the edge of her plate with the concentration of someone arranging game pieces.

Ysolde tastes the trout, considers it, and says, “It has been given too much heat.”

“The kitchen is serving eight species,” Aldric says. “Some compromise is inevitable.”

“Compromise is not a seasoning,” Ysolde says.

“It may be by fourth year,” Rashid says.

That earns him a sharper laugh from Ashara. She leans one elbow on the table, her tied black hair falling over the shoulder of her dark dinner jacket.

“Maerin favors his right shoulder after the second exchange,” she says. “He thinks no one notices because he changes stance before he resets.”

“I notice,” Rashid says.

“You notice after he scores.”

“I notice with suitable drama.”

Ysolde lifts her glass. “He also watches the judge when he feints.”

Ashara turns toward her. “You saw that?”

“I was seated where I could see it.”

“That is usually how seeing works,” Thessaly says.

Caelindra’s pointed ears tilt toward her. “A revelation for the Academy archives.”

The conversation rolls on around tomorrow’s ranking bout: Maerin’s tendency to crowd the center of the octagon, a second-year who has begun challenging people above his place on the board, the dubious qualities of an instructor’s favorite practice blade. Ashara and Rashid disagree over whether Maerin’s patience is discipline or cowardice, and neither seems interested in winning the argument quickly.

When the second course arrives, Aldric raises his glass a little.

“Whatever difficulties accompany the return of a human seat, I am glad to see this table less empty.”

Rashid’s expression stays courteous. “The Covenant has always been generous with the word empty.”

A moment settles over the table. The lamps hiss softly in their wall brackets.

“The Council’s terms are inherited,” Aldric says.

“So are the injuries,” Rashid says.

Ashara breaks a piece of bread in half. “If either of you begins drafting amendments over roast vegetables, I’ll have the servers remove the pens.”

“I wasn’t drafting anything,” Aldric says.

“No,” Thessaly says. “You were saying something true in a way that hoped it would end the matter.”

Aldric looks at her. For a moment, his mouth firms. Then he lowers his glass.

“It does not end it,” he says.

Seraphel turns her fork between her fingers.

“A full chair at the table  
Does not mend the floor beneath it.”

No one treats the line as a riddle. Caelindra reaches for the sauce, and the talk moves on to a Commerce lecturer who has assigned three books before the first seminar. Even Rashid joins in, recounting how the lecturer had announced the reading list as if daring the class to survive it.

By the time pear tart replaces the trout, the room has lost some of its first-night stiffness. Aldric excuses himself for evening observance, gathering his white-and-gold coat before he leaves toward the chapel. Caelindra departs soon after with a book tucked against her side, apparently unwilling to let the library spend another hour unsupervised. Ysolde remains long enough to finish the last of her wine in patient silence.

Ashara pushes back her chair.

“Rashid,” she says. “The octagon.”

He rises too, then stops at the open doors to the common room.

A low table has been claimed near the fireplace. Three people sit around it with cards spread between their glasses. One of them is a young demon woman with a dark splint wrapped around her wrist. She holds a hand of cards in her good hand and looks up at him.

“Rashid,” she says. “You promised us the first half-hour.”

Ashara turns her head.

Rashid’s jaw tightens slightly. “Dena. I did promise.”

“I know.”

“I agreed to drills this afternoon. I expected to come by after.”

Dena sets her cards facedown. “You expected us to start without you. That isn’t the same thing.”

Ashara’s gold eyes move from Rashid to Dena and back again. “You had another arrangement.”

“I should have said so before I accepted yours,” Rashid says.

“You should have.”

Dena’s fingers tap once against the card table. “I wanted cards. I did not want to become a reason for you to have an argument in a doorway.”

“You aren’t a reason,” Rashid says. “I made the error.”

Ashara stands with one hand resting on the back of her chair. “Keep the earlier promise. Don’t make it sound noble.”

Rashid meets her eyes. “I’ll find you time before tomorrow’s bout.”

“You’ll ask for it,” she says. “I may have it.”

Then she turns and heads for the front doors, her footsteps crisp across the hall floor.

Rashid watches her go, then crosses to the card table. Dena gathers the deck with her uninjured hand.

“No practice-group speeches,” she says.

“None,” Rashid says.

Thessaly has already pulled out the fourth chair. “Are the rules written down?”

“They’re cards,” Dena says.

“That is not an answer.”

“It will be when you lose.”

Thessaly sits. Dena notices you at the dining-room threshold and nudges the remaining chair back with her boot.

“It’s only cards,” she says. “No welcome speech attached.”
