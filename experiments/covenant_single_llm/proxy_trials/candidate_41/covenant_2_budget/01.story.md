Over the next quarter hour, your hands become useful before your name does. You pass greens, then the salt, then a platter of potatoes crisped at their edges. No one makes a ceremony of accepting any of it.

Conversation finds its own paths. Ysolde explains, with grave absorption, why the fish has been cooked too long.

“It was already dead,” Thessaly says.

“That is not the relevant threshold.”

Caelindra lowers her fork. “I am almost afraid to ask what is.”

Ysolde considers this. “Ice.”

Seraphel’s shoulders shake once with contained laughter.

“The cook has failed  
A mountain standard.”

“The cook has survived it,” Aldric says. “Which seems a better measure.”

Ashara and Rashid trade remarks about tomorrow’s ranked bout: footwork, timing, an opening Rashid insists she keeps mistaking for generosity. Their argument has the worn ease of one resumed many times.

When the server clears the fish plates, Rashid smooths his napkin flat beside his glass.

“Ashara,” he says, “I need to revise our arrangement. I promised people in the common room the first half-hour after dinner yesterday.”

Her tail goes still against the chair leg. “You accepted practice this afternoon.”

“I did. I thought they could begin without me.”

“Did you tell them you would be late?”

“No.”

Thessaly reaches for the water. “That is an avoidable flaw in the arrangement.”

Rashid’s mouth tightens, though his voice remains even. “Yes.”

Ashara studies him for a moment. “Then we train. You can apologize afterward.”

“I can.”

Seraphel looks between them.

“A bell will count  
Whichever promise waits.”

Caelindra, who has been listening with more interest than she pretends, says, “You make an ordinary evening sound exhausting.”

“It often is,” Rashid says.

The next dish arrives under a silver cover, sending up steam scented with cloves and roasted pears. Outside, lightning whitens the gardens below the window, and the house settles around the table as dinner continues.
