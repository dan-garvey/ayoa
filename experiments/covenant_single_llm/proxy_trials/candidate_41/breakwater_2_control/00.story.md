The staff stair brings you out beside the office, where the foyer opens warm and bright around the pushed-together tables. Steam clouds the glass lids of two serving dishes. A cheese-and-onion pie rests on a wooden board with its crust split golden at one corner; beside it, a bowl of salad catches the last light from the front doors.

Conversation breaks as you arrive.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

The woman lifting the stew’s lid smiles over the steam. “Jo. You’ve arrived at the useful moment, which is before everybody’s eaten the crust off the pie.”

“I haven’t,” says the dark-haired man beside her, setting down a stack of plates. “I’ve only tested it.”

“That’s Owen,” Jo says. “He’s conducting quality control.”

Nell has taken the chair nearest the office door, though a thin folder still lies by her elbow. “You know me. Martin and Bea were here before any of us learned what a spreadsheet was for, and Rosa’s making the exhibition look less like we found it all in a skip.”

Martin, broad-shouldered in an old work shirt, raises two fingers from where he is arranging bread in a basket. “Only some of it was in a skip.”

“It was not,” Bea says. She has a neat grey bob and a look of patient amusement. “It was in labelled boxes. Poorly labelled boxes, perhaps.”

Rosa, seated with a camera bag tucked under her chair, gives you a small wave. “Welcome.”

The empty places fill as Jo begins handing out plates. You take one and follow the movement around the table: stew first, then a wedge of pie, then bread passed from hand to hand before anyone reaches for the salad. The tomato smell has deepened with cumin and baked onion. A carton of new crockery remains open on the next table, white bowls nested in tissue paper like something waiting for permission to join in.

Dev comes down from the staircase as Jo is taking her own seat. He has a compact black case in one hand and his phone in the other.

“Sorry,” he says to nobody in particular, setting the case against the wall. “I’ve told Hana not to come over.”

Nell looks up from her plate. “Thank you.”

“I should’ve checked the diary before I got hopeful.” He pulls out the remaining chair and sits. “The receivers can wait until we’ve actually booked the room.”

“An excellent innovation,” Jo says. “Booking rooms before filling them with noises.”

Dev accepts this with a brief tilt of his head. “I’ll put it on tomorrow’s list.”

“You’ll put it in the diary tomorrow,” Nell says. “The list is where things go to become folklore.”

That gets a laugh from Owen, and even Martin’s mouth works around a smile as he cuts into the pie.

For the first few minutes, the talk travels without waiting for you to catch it. Jo asks whether the supplier has sent the right size takeaway lids this time. Owen says the delivery note insists they are twelve-ounce lids and the actual lids appear to have been designed for dollhouse saucepans. Nell says she will not have a public opening defeated by a lid, and Jo tells her that this is exactly how public openings are defeated.

Rosa reaches into her bag and brings out a sheet of notes, held at the corner by a blue bulldog clip.

“The six boards are all against the side wall,” she says to Martin. “I’ve got the measurements, but I want your recollections on tape before I start deciding what goes on the labels.”

Martin wipes a thumb along the edge of his bread. “That big *Jaws* one was Ted Roper’s. The shark anyway. I did the lettering.”

“You said that last week,” Bea says.

“I did the lettering.”

“You also said it was from 1984.”

“It was.”

Rosa glances at her notes. “The programme says 1986.”

Martin pauses, then points his fork at her. “Could’ve been 1986. Ted had the bad shoulder by then.”

“He had a bad shoulder for about fifteen years,” Bea says.

“He did.”

Rosa laughs softly. “That’s why I’m recording you instead of putting a date on everything you say.”

“I’m not against dates,” Martin replies. “I’m against dates being smug about it.”

The room settles again around the clink of cutlery. Through the auditorium doors, the cinema is dark and waiting, its repaired seats hidden beyond the narrow pane of glass. Rosa folds the notes back into her bag.

Bea looks across at you. “Nell said you start with the business papers tomorrow. That’s sensible. Old paperwork tells you more than people think it will.”

Nell nods. “We’ll go through the loan records as well. Nothing exciting, I’m afraid, but it matters that you know what belongs where before you start making lists.”

Jo reaches for the bread basket. “There. First evening and we’ve already mentioned paperwork. We’ll have him running for the train.”

“No danger,” Owen says. “The pie’s better than station food.”

Rosa looks toward the staircase, then back to the table. “Once you’ve seen the archive tomorrow, I’d be interested to hear what you think makes a useful condition note.”
