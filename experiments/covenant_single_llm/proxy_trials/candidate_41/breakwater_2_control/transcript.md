## Submission 0

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

The staff stair brings you out beside the office, where the foyer opens warm and bright around the pushed-together tables. Steam clouds the glass lids of two serving dishes. A cheese-and-onion pie rests on a wooden board with its crust split golden at one corner; beside it, a bowl of salad catches the last light from the front doors.

Conversation breaks as you arrive.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

The woman lifting the stew’s lid smiles over the steam. “Jo. You’ve arrived at the useful moment, which is before everybody’s eaten the crust off the pie.”

“I haven’t,” says the dark-haired man beside her, setting down a stack of plates. “I’ve only tested it.”

“That’s Owen,” Jo says. “He’s conducting quality control.”

Nell has taken the chair nearest the office door, though a thin folder still lies by her elbow. “You know me. Martin and Bea were here before any of us learned what a spreadsheet was for, and Rosa’s making the exhibition look less like we found it all in a skip.”

Martin, broad-shouldered in an old work shirt, raises two fingers from where he is arranging bread in a basket. “Only some of it was in a skip.”

“It was not,” Bea says. She has a neat grey bob and a look of patient amusement. “It was in labelled boxes. Poorly labelled boxes, perhaps.”

Rosa, seated with a camera bag tucked under her chair, gives you a small wave. “Welcome.”

The empty places fill as Jo begins handing out plates. You take one and follow the movement around the table: stew first, then a wedge of pie, then bread passed from hand to hand before anyone reaches for the salad. The tomato smell has deepened with cumin and baked onion. A carton of new crockery remains open on the next table, white bowls nested in tissue paper like something waiting for permission to join in.

Dev comes down from the staircase as Jo is taking her own seat. He has a compact black case in one hand and his phone in the other.

“Sorry,” he says to nobody in particular, setting the case against the wall. “I’ve told Hana not to come over.”

Nell looks up from her plate. “Thank you.”

“I should’ve checked the diary before I got hopeful.” He pulls out the remaining chair and sits. “The receivers can wait until we’ve actually booked the room.”

“An excellent innovation,” Jo says. “Booking rooms before filling them with noises.”

Dev accepts this with a brief tilt of his head. “I’ll put it on tomorrow’s list.”

“You’ll put it in the diary tomorrow,” Nell says. “The list is where things go to become folklore.”

That gets a laugh from Owen, and even Martin’s mouth works around a smile as he cuts into the pie.

For the first few minutes, the talk travels without waiting for you to catch it. Jo asks whether the supplier has sent the right size takeaway lids this time. Owen says the delivery note insists they are twelve-ounce lids and the actual lids appear to have been designed for dollhouse saucepans. Nell says she will not have a public opening defeated by a lid, and Jo tells her that this is exactly how public openings are defeated.

Rosa reaches into her bag and brings out a sheet of notes, held at the corner by a blue bulldog clip.

“The six boards are all against the side wall,” she says to Martin. “I’ve got the measurements, but I want your recollections on tape before I start deciding what goes on the labels.”

Martin wipes a thumb along the edge of his bread. “That big *Jaws* one was Ted Roper’s. The shark anyway. I did the lettering.”

“You said that last week,” Bea says.

“I did the lettering.”

“You also said it was from 1984.”

“It was.”

Rosa glances at her notes. “The programme says 1986.”

Martin pauses, then points his fork at her. “Could’ve been 1986. Ted had the bad shoulder by then.”

“He had a bad shoulder for about fifteen years,” Bea says.

“He did.”

Rosa laughs softly. “That’s why I’m recording you instead of putting a date on everything you say.”

“I’m not against dates,” Martin replies. “I’m against dates being smug about it.”

The room settles again around the clink of cutlery. Through the auditorium doors, the cinema is dark and waiting, its repaired seats hidden beyond the narrow pane of glass. Rosa folds the notes back into her bag.

Bea looks across at you. “Nell said you start with the business papers tomorrow. That’s sensible. Old paperwork tells you more than people think it will.”

Nell nods. “We’ll go through the loan records as well. Nothing exciting, I’m afraid, but it matters that you know what belongs where before you start making lists.”

Jo reaches for the bread basket. “There. First evening and we’ve already mentioned paperwork. We’ll have him running for the train.”

“No danger,” Owen says. “The pie’s better than station food.”

Rosa looks toward the staircase, then back to the table. “Once you’ve seen the archive tomorrow, I’d be interested to hear what you think makes a useful condition note.”

## Submission 1

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

Rosa lets the thought rest without trying to pull an answer across the table.

Jo reaches for the bread basket instead. “He’s been here an hour. Give him until tomorrow before you start making him defend a professional method.”

“I wasn’t making him defend it.”

“You had your notebook out.”

Rosa slips the blue clip back over her papers. “Habit.”

Nell lifts her glass. “Tomorrow can contain all the methods anyone wants. Tonight, nobody has to demonstrate competence.”

“That’s a relief,” Dev says. “I’ve had a bad run with it so far.”

“You haven’t had a bad run,” Nell says. “You found a scheduling problem before it became a room full of people trying to work over each other.”

Dev considers this, then nods once. “All right. I’ve had an unglamorous run.”

“That sounds more accurate,” Bea says.

The dishes make another circuit. You pass the salad toward Martin, then take the bread when Owen offers it across the table. Jo watches the pie disappear with the concentrated expression of someone keeping tally without letting it show.

“You did tell them the blue board has been painted over twice?” Martin says to Rosa.

“The blue one with the train.”

“It isn’t a train. It’s a carriage.”

“It has wheels.”

“That doesn’t make it a train.”

Bea puts down her fork. “The title is painted over. That’s the useful part.”

Martin turns to her. “It was *The 39 Steps*. You could still see the nine in the right light.”

“You said it was *The Thirty-Nine Steps* last Thursday,” Rosa says.

“It is.”

“The board says thirty-nine.”

“It meant the same thing.”

Rosa’s smile pulls briefly at one side of her mouth. “I know it meant the same thing. I’m trying to work out what changed, and when.”

Martin leans back, looking toward the auditorium doors. “We changed titles when we got the wrong posters. Or when the distributor sent the short version. Or when somebody had more paint than sense.”

“And if you don’t know which?” Bea asks.

“Then I say I don’t know which.”

“That,” Rosa says, “is exactly what I need on the recording.”

He gives her a look of mock suspicion, but reaches for his water.

Nell’s folder remains unopened beside her plate. She notices it after a moment and pushes it farther from the table.

“I’m not doing rotas over supper,” she says.

“Good,” Jo replies at once. “I was preparing to charge meeting rates.”

“You don’t have meeting rates.”

“I do now.”

Owen laughs into his sleeve. “How much?”

“More than any of you can afford. Except perhaps Bea, who would ask for a receipt and claim it back from herself.”

Bea’s eyebrows rise. “I would certainly not claim it back from myself.”

“You’d make a spreadsheet about why you couldn’t.”

“That is different.”

Dev glances at the case he left against the wall. “I’ll need an auditorium slot next week, whenever Nell can find one. I’ll ask Hana once there’s an actual time.”

“I’ll look first thing,” Nell says. “And I’ll put it in the diary before either of you plans around it.”

“Thank you.”

The answer seems to settle something in him. He tears off a piece of bread and uses it to clear the last of the stew from his bowl.

Outside, a car passes along Harbour Street, its headlights sweeping briefly over the glass doors. The sky beyond them has gone from grey to a deep, even blue. The small lamps above the cafe counter turn the unopened cartons of crockery into pale, orderly blocks.

Jo begins gathering empty serving spoons. “There’s more stew if anyone wants it. The pie is nearly gone, so that’s the decision made for you.”

“No more for me,” Rosa says. She looks at the clock above the counter. “I should get the recorder set up before seven.”

“I’ll bring the boards out from the wall,” Martin says. “They’re secure enough, but I don’t want to be hauling them around once you’ve got wires everywhere.”

“You mean one wire.”

“One wire’s enough to trip over.”

Bea folds her napkin and stands. “I’ll come down with you. I want to see which one you’ve decided is the blue board with the train.”

“It’s a carriage,” Martin says.

“It has wheels,” Rosa says, and this time he cannot quite keep from laughing.

Their chairs scrape back. Rosa picks up her camera bag and notes; Martin pockets a boiled sweet from the little paper packet beside his plate before following her toward the auditorium. Bea goes with them, still smiling.

At the table, Jo stacks bowls while Owen clears space around the serving dishes. Nell remains seated for a moment longer, one hand resting near the closed folder. Dev looks toward the stairwell and then toward the dark auditorium doors, where the three volunteers’ voices have already begun to blur together beyond the glass.
