## Submission 0

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

The lamps do make the stairs easy to follow.

At the first turn, the common room opens below you in firelight: leather chairs angled toward an oversized hearth, study tables left under stacks of paper, and portraits watching from the dark wood above the mantel. The dining-hall door stands open beyond it. Candlelight pools on the floorboards.

A long varnished table occupies the next room, set far more generously than tonight requires. Serving dishes wait beneath silver covers. At its middle, a tall woman with swept-back horns has one hand resting on the back of a chair.

“If rank is to decide the chairs,” she says, “we may as well replace them with pedestals.”

“That would at least be honest,” says the wolf-blooded man opposite her.

A white-haired woman, still as carved ice, considers the nearest chair. “Pedestals are impractical. They provide no support for the back.”

Seraphel steps into the room beside you.

“An empty place has waited.  
It has not yet learned its name.”

You take the open setting near the middle of the table. The signet on your hand catches candlelight as you settle.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

The horned woman inclines her head. “Ashara vel Kothren.”

“Rashid vel Amara.” His eyes touch the signet before returning to your face. “Welcome.”

“Ysolde Thornmantle,” says the white-haired woman.

The silver-haired elf across from you gives a measured nod. “Caelindra Vaeyn.”

“Thessaly Morrow.” The small woman beside Caelindra studies the covered dishes. “I am pleased the introductions occurred before the food cooled.”

“Aldric Verantus.” The angel’s white-and-gold collar is precise enough to look ceremonial. “Garvey House has been waiting a long time for this evening.”

Seraphel takes the last occupied chair.

“Seraphel Dawnquill.  
The stairs made that clear.”

Academy servers begin lifting the silver covers. Steam carries pepper, wine, and browned butter through the room. There is black bread, a pot of dark lentils, glazed roots, and a wide dish of roast fowl; at Ysolde’s place, a smaller platter holds thin slices of venison over cracked ice.

The dishes begin their circuit. Rashid passes the bread toward you after taking a piece for himself. Ashara adds a spoonful of the lentils to her plate, then reaches for a small bowl of crushed red spice. Ysolde takes the venison with a narrow fork and sets the ice aside rather than letting it melt into the tablecloth.

For the first few minutes, cutlery and serving spoons fill the pauses.

“The Registrar sent a full page about seating procedure,” Rashid says.

Caelindra tears bread with neat, impatient fingers. “Only one page? The Academy is becoming reckless.”

“It did not resolve the question,” Thessaly says. “It merely described the question in smaller writing.”

“The question was resolved when everyone sat down,” Ysolde says.

Ashara looks from the empty chairs to the occupied ones. “A triumph of direct action.”

Aldric’s mouth moves as though he nearly smiles. “The Registrar may survive the embarrassment.”

“Let the chairs remain chairs,” Seraphel says. “Supper has better claims on us.”

That earns a brief laugh from Rashid and, after a moment, Ashara.

When the roast fowl reaches her, Ashara passes it on without taking any. “After dinner,” she says to Rashid, “the octagon. Half an hour.”

“I have it.”

“Tomorrow’s bout is early.”

“You’ve mentioned this.”

“I’ll mention it again if you arrive soft from supper.”

Rashid breaks off a piece of bread. “You said you wanted a partner who knew your habits.”

“I said I wanted a partner who wouldn’t flatter me.”

“Then you’re safe.”

Ysolde sets down her fork. “The north edge of the octagon is loose. Don’t drag a heel there.”

Ashara’s gold eyes shift toward her. “Useful.”

“It is dirt,” Ysolde says. “It becomes useful when someone falls into it.”

Caelindra takes a slow sip of wine. “That is almost reassuring.”

“It is not intended to be,” Ysolde replies.

The conversation moves on without waiting for anyone to make it official. Rashid complains about an assignment in Covenant Procedure that requires three competing arguments for a policy he dislikes. Ashara says that should make it easier. Caelindra says it will only make the footnotes longer. Thessaly announces that any policy requiring three arguments is probably trying to conceal a fourth.

Outside the tall windows, lightning whitens the bridge for an instant. Inside, candlelight holds steady over the dishes and the long table, while the dinner finds its rhythm.

## Submission 1

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

Rashid’s complaint about Covenant Procedure gathers momentum as the lentils make another circuit of the table.

“Three arguments for a policy I think is inadequate,” he says, “one for a policy I think is harmful, and a conclusion required to appear impartial.”

“Write the harmful policy well,” Ashara says. “You may learn why someone supports it.”

“I’ll learn that Professor Kira enjoys assigning misery.”

Caelindra lifts her wine. “Professor Kira enjoys finding out whether students can distinguish misery from difficulty. They are often very attached to the confusion.”

Aldric folds his napkin once, precisely. “Understanding a position before condemning it is not misery.”

“It can be,” Thessaly says. “Especially when the position has already trapped someone.”

For a moment, the only sound is the soft crackle from the common-room fire and the quiet work of cutlery.

Ysolde takes another slice of venison. “A bridge does not become sound because its maker understood why it might fall.”

“No,” Aldric says. “But a person responsible for the bridge may still need to understand the maker.”

Ashara turns the spice bowl between two fingers. “Then I hope the assignment gives you all a bridge to inspect.”

Seraphel’s smile appears and fades.

“A law may wear a crown  
and still be badly made.”

Aldric looks toward her, then at the wine in his glass. “That is not a point I intended to dispute.”

“Good,” says Caelindra. “Disputes are much duller when they begin with agreement.”

The servers clear the first course and bring a fresh platter of mushrooms lacquered with honey and pepper. You help guide it past the wine bottles and down to Thessaly, who takes only two before sending it toward Ysolde.

“I was told,” Thessaly says, “that Academy dinners would involve conversation of great importance.”

“They do,” Rashid says. “We are deciding whether a bad assignment is a moral injury.”

“Ah.” Thessaly nods as if this confirms something. “Then the rumors were accurate.”

Caelindra’s ears shift with the start of a laugh. “At the Amber Court, a lesson like this would be conducted before one’s family.”

Rashid glances over. “So everyone could critique the argument?”

“So everyone could critique the speaker’s posture while they made it.”

“That does sound worse,” Aldric says.

“It is efficient,” Caelindra replies.

Ashara gives Rashid a look across the table. “You would enjoy it.”

“I would survive it.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“You make that distinction only when it benefits you.”

“It benefits me often.”

The exchange does not pause the meal. Rashid reaches for the bread at the same time Ashara does; he draws his hand back first, and she breaks the loaf with a clean twist before passing half toward him. He accepts it with a small inclination of his head.

At the far end of the table, Ysolde has been watching the storm beyond the windows. Another pulse of lightning moves through the clouds below the plateau.

“The western bridge is clearer tonight,” she says.

Aldric follows her gaze. “Is that unusual?”

“The storm shifted south at dusk.”

“It has no preference for this evening’s guests,” Caelindra says.

“No,” Ysolde says. “Storms have very little social sense.”

“That makes them suitable for the Academy,” Thessaly says.

This time even Aldric laughs, quietly enough that the sound nearly disappears beneath the next clatter of plates.

Fifteen minutes pass in that fashion: food moving from hand to hand, conversation wandering through professors, lecture halls, a disastrous attempt by some second-years to petition for later bell times, and the peculiar uselessness of the Registrar’s forms. No one draws the talk toward you. The table keeps finding its own currents around your silence.

When the dessert plates arrive—small pears poached dark in spice wine—Ashara looks at the clock over the sideboard.

“Octagon,” she says.

Rashid wipes his fingers on his napkin and rises. “Half an hour.”

“Bring your balance this time.”

“You say that as if I misplaced it.”

“You did. Repeatedly.”

Ysolde sets down her fork. “The north edge.”

“I heard you,” Rashid says.

Ashara has already moved around her chair. Her tail clears the carved wood without touching it. Rashid takes his coat from the back of his seat, and the two of them leave through the common room, their argument continuing more quietly beyond the dining-hall door.

The remaining candles burn lower over the long table. Thessaly studies the untouched last pear on Ashara’s plate, then reaches across to claim it.
