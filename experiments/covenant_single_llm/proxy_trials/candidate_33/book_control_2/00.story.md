Ysolde inclines her head once. Her gaze goes back to the window before she answers.

“You are welcome. In fog it is prettier.”

Caelindra looks from Ysolde to the white break of storm beyond the glass. “That seems a poor recommendation for a bridge.”

“It is not a recommendation,” Ysolde says. “Fog is weather.”

“Does fog move the light farther?” Thessaly asks.

“No. It hides everything except the lamps.” Ysolde draws a short line across the tablecloth with one fingertip, stopping before the salt cellar. “Then the line is clearer. From the air, it is almost the only useful thing to see.”

“From the ground,” Rashid says, “it makes you certain the bridge is about to shed its railings.”

Ashara takes a drink of water. “You thought that for three days.”

“I thought it until someone explained the lamps.”

“You asked a gardener whether the north bridge was damaged.”

“The gardener had a shovel. It seemed likely he would know about damage.”

“He was digging around a rosebush.”

“A rosebush can be near a bridge.”

Thessaly’s laugh arrives before she can take another piece of bread. “This is an excellent system. It lets the Academy educate people by making them look foolish in mist.”

Seraphel lifts her cup, the warm gold of her skin gentling in the candlelight.

“The weather takes its fee
from every proud traveler
and leaves them damp.”

“It left me wet,” Rashid says. “Pride was not involved.”

“That is not what Ashara has reported,” Caelindra says.

Ashara’s tail gives one measured sweep behind her chair. “I have reported only observable facts.”

“Then we are in safe hands,” Aldric says, though the corners of his mouth shift when he reaches for the water pitcher.

The conversation opens again after that, no longer gathered around the storm. A server removes the meat platter and returns with shallow bowls of baked pear, each half glossy with honey and scattered with crushed nuts. The steam carries cinnamon across the table. Caelindra regards her portion with more generosity than she gave the tarragon sauce.

“At last,” she says. “A food with a coherent purpose.”

“Its purpose is dessert,” Ysolde says.

“Yes. Coherently.”

Rashid has set the folded request beside his plate, but he does not open it. “If we are accounting for purposes, tomorrow’s Covenant Law reading appears intended to punish us for choosing the Conclave.”

“It is ninety-two pages,” Aldric says.

“I know. I counted them while deciding whether the cover had been padded.”

Caelindra cuts into the pear with the edge of her spoon. “The first fifty pages are tolerable. After that, the court begins to explain itself, which is a mistake it repeats at length.”

Rashid turns toward her. “You’ve read it already?”

“Of course I have. I dislike spending annoyance on a thing I have not examined.”

“And the decision?” Ashara asks.

“Correct on jurisdiction. Tedious in every other respect.”

“That is a fair description of most good law,” Aldric says.

“It is a fair description of most law,” Thessaly replies. “Goodness has very little to do with its length.”

Seraphel’s wings settle close behind her shoulders.

“A law may close a door,
then call the hinge
a lesson in patience.”

For a moment Aldric studies the honey melting over his dessert. “That is why there are appeals,” he says.

“And lawyers,” Thessaly says.

“And people who want neither,” Rashid adds.

Ashara looks at the folded paper. “Your request should not become a judgment against the instructor before anyone has read the report.”

“It will not.” Rashid taps the page once. “I will have a clean copy on the sideboard before breakfast. It asks for the report’s conclusion and a supervised hour once the court is safe. Nothing else.”

“No names?” Thessaly asks.

“No names.”

“Then I will read it.”

“I will also,” Aldric says.

Ashara nods. “So will I.”

Caelindra raises her spoon. “If it has no footnotes, I reserve the right to find it aesthetically undernourished.”

“It has no footnotes.”

“Then perhaps it will improve overnight.”

Rashid gives her a look that lasts just long enough to be an answer, then folds the request again and slides it inside his jacket. No one pushes a page toward you or asks for a pledge while the plates are still warm.

Outside, another run of lightning shows the western bridge for an instant: its hard white span straight over the cloudbank, every lamp spilling a long bright stroke sideways into dark. When the cloud closes, the image remains with the scent of cinnamon and lamp oil, the low scrape of forks, and the uneven rhythm of eight people learning how to share a table.

The servers clear the dessert bowls one by one. Beyond the dining-room doorway, the common room fire throws a restless orange reflection up the stairwell, while the library remains lit behind its open doors. At the table, conversation has turned to whether the Academy’s morning bells are deliberately early or merely indifferent to sleep.
