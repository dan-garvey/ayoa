Ysolde inclines her head. “You are welcome. The first view does that to most people.”

“It makes a poor advertisement for engineering,” Caelindra says.

“The bridge is not advertising.”

“Then it has at least avoided making promises it cannot keep.”

Rashid leans back in his chair. “It is different when you are on it.”

“You were not on it for long,” Ashara says. “You stopped halfway across and held the rail with both hands.”

“I was checking its stability.”

“With both hands.”

“That is why rails exist.”

Seraphel lifts her cup.

“A rail may serve
the cautious hand
and the confident one.”

“A generous ruling,” Rashid says.

“It is accurate,” Ysolde says.

The exchange leaves the table in a quieter humor. You finish the last of the meat and the roasted roots at the same unhurried pace, taking the green sauce with them until only its tarragon bite remains on your tongue. Around you, talk travels without needing your help: a complaint about the first seminar’s reading list; Rashid’s claim that Professor Windwhisper has assigned a century of treaties simply to see who complains first; Caelindra’s certainty that she would prefer the treaties to his complaints.

The servers clear the plates and bring shallow dishes of pears baked soft in spiced wine, each with a spoonful of pale cream and a thin almond biscuit balanced across the rim. Candlelight catches in the dark syrup. Beyond the windows, the storm gives another silent flash, and the west bridge appears for an instant with every lamp’s brightness pulled eastward.

Rashid waits until the server has passed before drawing the folded petition out from beneath his napkin. He has already crossed out two lines and written smaller replacements in the margin.

“I removed ‘immediate redress,’” he says to Ashara.

“I can see that.” She holds out her hand. He gives her the page.

Her eyes move over it while she eats a slice of pear. “You have asked for restoration of access after an appropriate review.”

“Yes.”

“Ask what their review is reviewing.”

Rashid takes up his pencil again. “The accident.”

“The report on the accident, the court, the equipment, the wards—whatever they find. If they are going to keep the court closed, they can tell you what has to change before it opens.”

“That is nearly a whole letter.”

“Then write a whole letter.”

Aldric sets down his spoon. “And say plainly that the group is not asking to use it before the Academy considers it safe. Otherwise they will read urgency as carelessness.”

Rashid’s pencil pauses above the page. “I do not intend carelessness.”

“I know,” Aldric says. “They may not.”

Thessaly turns her dessert spoon between her fingers. “A question with an answer someone can actually give is harder to ignore. You may still be ignored. It is only harder.”

“A bright prospect,” Caelindra says.

“I did not offer a promise.”

Rashid looks from the paper to Ashara, then adds a new line beneath the others. The scratching of the pencil is audible for a few moments between the clink of spoons and the windless quiet beyond the glass.

“There,” he says at last. “I will leave it in the library tomorrow after the first seminar. Anyone who wants to read it can.”

“I will,” Aldric says.

“That includes you,” Rashid says.

“It does.”

Ashara gives the page back. “Bring me the clean copy before you put it out.”

“You already read it.”

“I read this copy.”

For a moment Rashid looks as though he may object. Then he folds the sheet along its old creases and slips it into his coat. “Fine.”

Caelindra finishes her pear. “A narrow escape. I had feared supper would end before anyone produced a document requiring three readers.”

“There is still time,” Rashid says.

“Not if I can help it.”

Thessaly glances through the open doorway to the common room, where the fire has settled into a red bed beneath the portraits and a square card table waits beside the hearth. “There is a box of Rooks and Rivers on that table,” she says. “The rules are printed on the lid. No one owes anyone anything when they win.”

“An unusually comforting feature,” Seraphel says.

“A game with enforceable boundaries,” Aldric says. “That seems sensible.”

“It is a game with rules,” Thessaly says. “The boundaries follow.”

Rashid’s mouth curves. “One hand, then I rewrite this properly.”

“You will lose the first hand because you have not played it,” Ysolde says.

“That is a very practical prediction.”

“It is not a prediction. It is the usual result.”

Seraphel’s wings lift a fraction behind her shoulders.

“Then let the first loss be small,
and let the next hand
know more.”

Thessaly looks across the remnants of dinner toward you, her black eyes steady. “Do you know Rooks and Rivers, Rowan?”
