Ysolde inclines her head once.

“You are welcome,” she says. “They work better from above.”

Caelindra looks from the window to her. “That is a generous assessment of a row of lamps attempting to escape sideways.”

“They do not attempt anything.”

“Of course not. They have been trained to accept the design.”

For a moment Ysolde considers this as if Caelindra may have offered a practical objection. Then she says, “The groundskeeper designed them.”

“Then I withdraw my criticism of the lamps.” Caelindra sets down her glass. “It would be cruel to give them responsibility for a groundskeeper.”

Ashara’s mouth shifts around the edge of a smile. Rashid lets out a breath that is almost a laugh and reaches for the bread before the exchange can acquire another layer. Across from you, Seraphel lowers her eyes to her plate, her small wings stirring once behind her shoulders.

“Even a fixed bright thing
may look unsteady
in a moving world.”

“That is either about lamps or dinner,” Thessaly says.

“Both are permitted,” Seraphel replies.

The server replaces the meat platter with a shallow dish of baked pears, their skins split and shining in dark syrup. There is cream in a small silver jug, and a scatter of toasted nuts that makes a dry, warm smell beneath the herbs and pepper still hanging over the table. You finish the last of the green sauce with a piece of bread. It remains sharp enough to wake the tongue after the richness of the meal.

Rashid waits until the serving dishes have passed before he draws the folded petition from beneath his napkin. He does not open it at once. His thumb rests on the crease, where the paper has been folded and unfolded enough times to soften.

“I did change the opening,” he says to Ashara.

“You said that.”

“I am establishing that I listened.”

“One alteration establishes no such thing.”

He looks at the paper. “The request now asks for the instructor’s conclusion, an explanation of the restriction, and supervised hours once the court is safe. It does not accuse anyone of hiding anything.”

“Better,” Aldric says.

“That is not an answer to whether you’ll support it.”

“It is an answer to whether the wording is better.” Aldric reaches for a pear half, careful with the spoon. “I will read it before I decide. You have made it sound as if the Academy has already refused a reasonable request. It has not received one yet.”

Rashid’s fingers tighten briefly on the page. “It closed the court before it told the people who use it what happened.”

“After an injury,” Aldric says.

“Yes.”

The word lands without heat, though the fur along the back of Rashid’s hand lies too flat against the paper. Ashara holds out her hand.

“Give it here.”

He passes the sheet to her. She reads with her dessert spoon still between two fingers, gold eyes moving down the lines. The candles throw a low red sheen across her skin. No one fills the quiet for her.

At last, she taps the page near the middle. “This sentence says students without private training have been ‘excluded from the ordinary practice of their peers.’”

“They have.”

“Some have. Some students are in the Military track and have other courts. Some have tutors. Some do not train.”

“The group trains there.”

“Then say that. Do not make a claim broad enough for someone to dismiss because you wanted it to wound.”

Rashid’s jaw moves once. “You think I want it to wound?”

“I think you are angry.”

“I am angry.”

“Then write accurately while you are.”

He watches her for a beat, then nods. “All right.”

Thessaly has poured cream over her pear in a thin white line. “Does the request say that the people signing it may include their own account?”

“There is a space for comments.”

“A space is not the same as an invitation.”

Rashid turns toward her. “What would you put?”

“That anyone who wants the court reopened may say why they use it. Or why they do not wish to say why. Both are information.”

“The Academy will take an unsigned request less seriously.”

“Probably.” Thessaly lifts one shoulder. “That does not make a name available for your use.”

“I know.” His answer comes quickly, then he looks down at the page again. “I know. I can add that comments may be anonymous.”

“And that signing is voluntary,” Ashara says, still reading.

“It already is.”

“Write it.”

Caelindra cuts into a pear with great care. “You are all making a petition sound like a medical procedure.”

“It is a request to an institution,” Aldric says. “Those are usually worse.”

“Then I am grateful that my future consists of a court and not a council chamber.”

Ysolde looks at her. “Your future does not consist of either thing alone.”

Caelindra pauses with her spoon halfway to her mouth. “How reassuring.”

“It was not meant to be.”

This time the laugh reaches Caelindra before she can prevent it. It is brief and surprised; she looks down at the pear as if it has caused the trouble. Seraphel’s smile brightens.

“A mercy, perhaps,
that truth has no duty
to arrive prettily.”

“It could make a little effort,” Caelindra says.

“No,” Ysolde says. “Then it might be less true.”

The conversation moves on before the thought can harden into an argument. Aldric describes the first-year reading list with the solemn injury of someone who has counted its pages. Ashara says that a book which takes two hands to lift ought to be issued with a weapon permit. Rashid objects that he has seen her carry a weighted practice shield up three flights of stairs without changing pace.

“The shield did not contain footnotes,” Ashara says.

“A distinction your professors will be relieved to hear.”

“My professors are relieved by very little.”

“Then perhaps that is why they assign so many pages,” Thessaly says. “They are trying to feel something.”

The remark makes Aldric choke softly on his water. He recovers before anyone can decide whether to help him. The servers clear the pear dishes one by one, leaving cups of dark tea and the small crumbs of the meal’s arguments on the white cloth.

Outside, the cloud loosens for a few breaths. The western bridge appears again beyond the gardens, level and pale above the storm. Its lamps trail their long bright strokes eastward, as Ysolde promised. Seen through the tall window, they look less like a row of objects than a single white current drawn across the dark.

Rashid gathers the petition, now marked in Ashara’s compact hand. “I’ll revise this tonight. I’ll leave copies in the common room after breakfast for anyone from the group who wants one.”

“Bring the final version to me first,” Ashara says.

“At seven?”

“Seven.”

Aldric folds his napkin beside his plate. “I will read it then as well.”

“You may both have the honor of making it less dramatic,” Caelindra says. “A rare civic achievement.”

“It will still be dramatic,” Rashid says, slipping the page into the inner pocket of his coat. “Just defensibly so.”

Thessaly takes up her tea. “That is a better bargain than the first one.”

Around the table, chairs begin to ease back from the varnished wood. The dining hall remains warm despite the storm pressing light against the windows, and for another moment the eight of you are simply there: cups cooling, candles shortening, the bridge holding its bright line beyond the garden wall.
