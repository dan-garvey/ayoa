Caelindra considers the question with the air of someone deciding whether an old annoyance deserves the effort of speech.

“My mother had said that no human poem could make her laugh,” she says at last. “Lady Garvey wagered two gold crowns that she could read one without producing so much as a smile.”

She takes a measured sip of wine.

“The poem was about a bishop, a turnip, and a remarkably unconvincing ghost. My mother laughed once.”

Ashara's hand pauses over the salt cellar. Rashid looks up from his plate.

“Lady Garvey lost the first wager,” Caelindra continues. “Then she immediately wagered the same amount that my mother would ask to hear the poem again before dessert. She did.”

“Efficient,” Rashid says.

“Obnoxious,” Caelindra corrects. “Mother paid. Lady Garvey gave the crowns to a kitchen boy who had been trying not to laugh.”

For the first time, the elf's attention slips from you to the candle flame between you. “I was very young. I thought the poem was idiotic.”

Seraphel folds a torn piece of bread between her fingers. “It sounds as though it had a useful purpose.”

Caelindra's ear turns toward her. “You would say that.”

“I would.”

The servers begin clearing the first empty dishes at the other end of the long table.
