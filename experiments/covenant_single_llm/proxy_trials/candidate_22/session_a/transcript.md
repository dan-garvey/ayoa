**Story**

Stormlight keeps finding the thin places in the curtains.

Each time it does, the room appears in a different arrangement: the made bed in its dark carved frame; the travelling trunk at its foot; the desk supplied with fresh paper and a pot of ink; the charcoal coat mended so carefully at one elbow that the repair has become a small, deliberate pattern. The inherited signet catches one blue-white flare from the window and then becomes only dull metal again.

Beyond the glass, the gardens are a square of cultivated green held between pale walls. Farther out, where the plateau ends, the storm keeps its distance below the Academy. Lightning moves soundlessly through it, slow enough to watch travel from cloud to cloud.

Someone has left a lamp burning on the desk. Its oil gives the room a warmer, older smell than the rain-bright air slipping around the window frame. Beside it lies the Academy primer, open where a red ribbon marks Article Nineteen. The paragraph beneath has been underlined by a previous reader in a hard, unwavering hand. The rule itself needs no embellishment: the Covenant governs marriages and bodies as firmly as it governs bridges and votes.

The page before it is headed *The Human Seat: Historical Vacancy.* It offers dates, numbers, and a careful account of a plague that ran through human lands sixty years ago. It does not explain why the lawyers who found you had sent three separate letters before you answered the first one. It does explain why tonight's dinner is being called a welcome rather than an introduction.

On the wall above the desk hangs a framed letter in brown, slanting script. It is addressed to a great-nephew with another Garvey name and discusses summer weather, the condition of the roses, and whether he will prefer the blue room or the one overlooking the gardens. The writer has added, in a crowded postscript, that she will not hear of him staying at an inn when there is perfectly good space in the house. Nothing in it seems intended for a stranger reading it decades later.

A bell sounds somewhere below: one low note, then another. It is not grand enough to be ceremonial. It is simply audible through old wood.

The room's door stands ajar onto the landing. From there, the broad stair turns toward the common room and the ground floor, where lamplight has begun to collect in the passage. Voices rise and fall beneath the bell.

"You are calling it a petition because it makes the refusal sound tidy," says a young man's voice. Its restraint is practiced and imperfect at the edges. "It is a request for supervised use. Those are different things."

"The distinction will not mend a wrist," answers another voice, masculine and clear. "Nor will it establish that the court can be used safely."

There is a brief scrape of chair legs. Then a woman's voice, low and cool: "He did not say the court was safe. He said the closure is broad. Those are also different things."

For a moment, no one replies. The house settles around the silence: a distant door closes, a pipe knocks once in the wall, and the storm paints the landing silver.

The first voice returns, drier now. "Thank you, Ashara. I will note the occasion in a calendar."

"Do so accurately," she says. "I have not offered you my training hour."

A soft, abrupt laugh comes from someone else in the room below. It ends quickly, as though its owner has noticed it might be heard upstairs.

Footsteps approach along the ground-floor passage. A server in Academy blue and grey appears at the foot of the stairs with a covered dish balanced on one hand. She looks up, finds the open door, and inclines her head without coming farther into the private floor.

"Dinner will be served in ten minutes, Mr. Garvey," she says. "The others are in the common room."

She continues toward the dining hall before any answer is required of you. The covered dish carries a sharp scent of citrus and pepper in her wake.

Below, the argument has already changed shape.

"Dena asked not to have her name attached to it," Rashid says. Whatever else can be learned from the sound of him, that much is plain. "I am not putting her name on anything."

"Good," says Ashara.

"That was not an accusation."

"It sounded like one from here," says a new voice, light and faintly amused.

The common room waits one flight down. From the landing, you can see the edge of its fire through the open archway and the black curve of the banister leading toward it. Beyond that lies the formal dining hall, with its long table and the first evening of a life the primer has described more confidently than anyone in this house is likely to live it.
