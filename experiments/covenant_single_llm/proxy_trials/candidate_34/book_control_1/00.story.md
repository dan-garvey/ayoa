Ysolde looks at you for a moment, then back at the place where the bridge has vanished into cloud.

“You are welcome,” she says. “Most things here become ordinary if you see them often enough.”

“That is a very dragon answer,” Caelindra says.

“Is it inaccurate?”

Caelindra rests her chin lightly on one hand. “No. Which is why it is difficult to object to.”

The corner of Ysolde’s mouth changes by so little that it might be the candlelight. She takes another bite of her meal.

You finish yours without hurrying. The table does not make a ceremony of your silence. Conversation folds around the business of eating: bread passed from hand to hand, the last of the green sauce claimed by Ashara before Caelindra can decide whether one more taste is required for the integrity of her judgment. The servers clear plates in a soft procession of porcelain and linen.

“Windwhisper’s third commentary is not a reading,” Rashid says as a server replaces his knife. “It is a siege conducted with footnotes.”

“It is an account of the first arbitration,” Aldric says. “You might find its length more tolerable if you did not treat every required text as a personal accusation.”

“I do not. Only the ones that have earned it.”

Ashara leans back in her chair, heat drawing a faint shimmer above the dark wine in her glass. “You complained about the maps before you reached the footnotes.”

“The maps were poorly copied.”

“The maps were copied by hand two centuries ago.”

“Then two centuries ago, someone should have copied them better.”

Seraphel lowers her cup.

“A map may show a road,
but cannot make the walker
enjoy the rain.”

“That is unkindly apt,” Rashid says.

“It is not a defense of the maps,” Seraphel tells him.

Thessaly has been turning the bread basket so that its handles face the same direction. “The commentary says the first arbiters met for eleven days before agreeing where to sit,” she says. “That part seems useful.”

“Useful to whom?” Aldric asks.

“To anyone planning a meeting.”

Caelindra gives her a sidelong look. “You have found the only practical lesson in a scholar’s vanity.”

“I look for practical lessons. They are usually hidden under the vanity.”

The next course arrives in small pale dishes: plum cake soaked dark at the center, cream melting slowly at its edges. Its scent reaches you before the server sets it down. Beyond the windows, another flare of lightning lays the garden walls flat and white for an instant, then returns the room to candlelight.

Rashid waits until the servers have moved away before drawing the folded petition from beneath his napkin. He does not put it in front of you. Instead, he offers it across the table to Ashara.

“I changed the opening,” he says.

She reads in silence. Her gold eyes move once from the page to him and back again.

“You removed ‘the Academy’s neglect.’”

“I removed the word.”

“You removed an assertion you cannot establish.”

“That too.”

Ashara passes the page to Aldric. “Better.”

Aldric reads more slowly. “You ask that the faculty publish its conclusion about the injury, and that it set a supervised schedule once the court can be used safely.”

“Yes.”

“That is a reasonable request.”

Rashid’s hand stills on the tablecloth. “It is still a request for access.”

“I know.” Aldric folds the paper along its existing crease before giving it back. “I may disagree with the timing of the earlier version. I do not disagree that the students deserve an answer.”

For the first time since the paper reappeared, Rashid’s expression eases. “Then read it again tomorrow, before anyone signs.”

“I will.”

Thessaly watches the paper disappear beneath Rashid’s hand. “This version does not pretend to know more than it knows,” she says. “That is an improvement.”

“A triumph for civic repair,” Caelindra says, lifting her spoon.

“Do not sound so moved,” Rashid says.

“I am saving my feeling for the cake.”

“You dislike the cake?” Ysolde asks.

“I have not decided.”

Ysolde considers this. “You have had three bites.”

“I am gathering evidence.”

This time Ashara laughs outright, brief and low. Rashid shakes his head, but he is smiling when he refolds the petition and puts it away.

The last of dinner loosens after that. Aldric declines a second helping of cake with the air of someone declining a moral temptation. Seraphel takes the smallest piece available and says nothing about it. The storm keeps flashing below the windows, patient and soundless.

Thessaly reaches into the small satchel at her chair and brings out a square wooden box, its lid inlaid with a pattern of white towers and black gates. She sets it beside her empty dessert plate.

“After dinner,” she says, “I am taking this to the common room.”

Caelindra studies the box. “You announce an invitation as though it were a change in weather.”

“An invitation can be ignored. Weather cannot.”

“A distinction which does not improve your invitation.”

“The rules are printed inside the lid. There are no favors attached to the outcome.” Thessaly taps the box with one delicate finger. “Only points.”

Seraphel’s wings lift a fraction.

“A board, a hand,
a loss that asks no debt—
that sounds almost restful.”

“It is restful if you do not play against Ashara,” Rashid says.

“I have a ranked bout in the morning,” Ashara replies. “You are safe from me tonight.”

“I will observe the first game,” Ysolde says. “I do not know the rules.”

“Then you may watch someone lose them,” Thessaly says.

Her black eyes settle on you across the candlelit table. “Do you know Crown and Gates?”
