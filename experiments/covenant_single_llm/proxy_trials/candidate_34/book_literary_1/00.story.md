Ysolde turns from the window. Candlelight lays a thin gleam along the white of her hair and leaves her face as composed as it was when she explained the lamps.

“I've never seen anything like that,” you say quietly. “Thank you.”

For a moment, she seems to consider the thanks as if it has been handed to her in an unfamiliar shape. Then she inclines her head.

“You are welcome,” she says. “They look less remarkable in clear weather.”

“Then clear weather has failed them,” Caelindra says.

“No. It makes them useful.”

Rashid looks between the window and Ysolde. “Couldn’t they be both?”

“They are,” Ysolde says. “But usefulness is easier to judge when you need it.”

Seraphel rests her fingertips against the stem of her cup.

“A light can keep a road
and still be lovely
to someone not crossing.”

Ysolde gives this a small, grave nod. “Yes.”

Caelindra looks faintly put upon by the agreement. “I appear to be alone in believing a lamp ought to perform its duties without inviting a discussion of its inner life.”

“You are not alone,” Ashara says. “You have the entire Department of Maintenance.”

That wins a short laugh from Rashid. Caelindra’s expression alters by scarcely anything, but she does not object.

You turn back to your plate. The meat has cooled enough that the browned edge comes away cleanly beneath your knife; the green sauce still holds its sharp, grassy bite. Around you, cutlery resumes its small work against porcelain. A server refills the water glasses and slips away before anyone has to make room for her.

The talk moves, as table talk does, into the parts of tomorrow that can still be complained about safely.

“Windwhisper assigned the old dissent clauses,” Rashid says. “All of them.”

“Not all of them,” Aldric says. “The first three revisions.”

“That is a distinction made by someone who has read the appendix.”

“I have.”

Caelindra lifts her glass. “The appendix is where the useful errors live. The body of an Accord is designed to make everyone feel solemn. The notes tell you who was tired, who mistranslated a word, and which delegate attempted to hide a commercial concession in a definition.”

“That happened?” Rashid says.

“Repeatedly. People are very imaginative when grain tariffs are involved.”

Thessaly tears another piece from the bread. “Then the margins are the honest part.”

“No,” Caelindra says. “They are merely less polished.”

“Those are often the same thing.”

Seraphel’s smile returns.

“A margin grows
where someone has more to say
than the page permits.”

“Which is why pages have edges,” Ysolde says.

This time Caelindra does smile, briefly and against her apparent preference. She reaches for the tarragon sauce again, apparently unwilling to concede that it has improved.

Rashid’s folded sheet has worked its way back beside his plate. He smooths it with the heel of his hand, reading the first lines without speaking. Ashara notices.

“If you are changing it now,” she says, “remove ‘immediate.’”

“I had already crossed it out.”

“You have crossed it out in a way that suggests you miss it.”

“It was a good word.”

“It was an impatient word.”

He takes up his pen. “Then: ‘We request a written account of the court’s safety findings, and supervised access when those findings permit it.’”

Aldric considers the line. “A written account appropriate for the participants. There may be details the Academy should not publish broadly.”

Rashid’s pen pauses. “Yes. That’s fair.”

“It is more than fair,” Thessaly says. “An injury is not obliged to become evidence merely because a petition needs force.”

The deliberate courtesy comes back into Rashid’s face, though his hand stays still on the paper. “No one’s name is on it unless they choose that themselves. The request can stand without one.”

Ashara watches him for a beat, then reaches across for the sheet. “Give it here.”

He passes it over. She reads in silence, one long finger following the sentence he has written. The end of her tail taps once against her chair leg.

“This is better,” she says. “Copy it cleanly. I’ll read the final version before drills.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not make it ceremonial.”

“I was trying to be polite.”

“Try less hard.”

“That is not advice I have often received.”

“You should have.”

Seraphel looks down at the petition as Ashara hands it back.

“Ask for the door,
and do not claim
the key.”

“A sound principle,” Aldric says.

“It is a sound sentence,” Thessaly says. “Principles have more ways to disappoint people.”

No one takes that up. Rashid makes the revision, then folds the page along its old creases and tucks it inside his jacket. The matter has not become smaller, exactly, but it has acquired a shape that can be carried to the students who use the court.

By the time the servers begin clearing the plates, the room has settled into the warm, irregular quiet after a meal. Ashara and Rashid have moved on to arguing over whether the seminar’s reading list counts as a practical warning or an act of hostility. Aldric maintains that a difficult text is not hostile simply because it is difficult. Caelindra says that this is the sort of opinion produced by a person whose family has never had to pay a copyist by the page.

Outside, the cloud shifts. The western bridge appears again for a few seconds beyond the garden wall: its hard line steady over the dark, its lamps flung eastward in pale strokes. Then the storm takes it back.

At the long table, the names from your primer have become voices with particular cadences: Rashid’s careful insistence, Ashara’s clean corrections, Caelindra’s dry refusals, Thessaly’s remarks arriving sideways, Seraphel’s plain little verses, Aldric’s measured certainty, Ysolde’s attention fixed on the things that hold. No one asks you to account for a history that only recently acquired your name. The candles burn lower, and for the moment there is only the warm room, the emptying dishes, and the company around them.
