The cloud takes the bridge before its last stretched lamps have quite faded. For a moment the dark beyond the garden wall is only dark again.

“I've never seen anything like that,” you say quietly to Ysolde. “Thank you.”

Ysolde turns her attention from the window. “It is more obvious in weather like this,” she says. “On a clear night it only looks like a row of lamps.”

“An argument against clear weather,” Caelindra says. “I will preserve it for future use.”

“Clear weather has other uses.”

“Name two.”

Ysolde considers this with apparent seriousness. “Flying. Seeing far away.”

Caelindra lowers her eyes to her plate. “An overwhelming case.”

The corner of Ysolde's mouth moves once. It is gone before the server reaches past her shoulder to take the nearly empty dish of roasted roots.

You finish the rest of your meal at an unhurried pace. The green sauce keeps its sharp, grassy edge against the browned meat; the room carries the warmth of candles, bodies, and the banked fire beyond the open dining-room doors. Conversation travels around the table without waiting for you to fill each pause.

Rashid has set his folded request beside his water glass. He does not pick it up again at first. Instead, when a server brings a flat pear tart dusted with sugar and a dish of thick cream, he says, “Professor Shaan assigned us a letter from a provincial magistrate this morning. Three pages of apology for a decision he then defended in the fourth.”

“A very common form,” Aldric says.

“Is it?” Rashid asks.

“Among people who want to be forgiven without changing their minds.”

Ashara cuts into the tart with a clean, decisive stroke. “Then it is not an apology. It is an announcement with better manners.”

“That is nearly what the lecture said,” Rashid tells her.

“Professor Shaan has sound instincts.”

“You said she spoke too quickly.”

“She does. Those are separate observations.”

Thessaly reaches for the cream. “They often are. People join separate observations together because they prefer fewer things to think about.”

Caelindra accepts a spoonful, then studies the tart as if it has presented a minor legal difficulty. “The magistrate at least had the decency to write four pages. I spent an hour this afternoon listening to two third-years agree with each other in circles. Neither of them ever found the center.”

“What were they discussing?” Seraphel asks.

“Whether the northern tariffs are a moral question.”

Seraphel tilts her head.

“A tariff can be cruel.
It can also be dull.
Those may both be true.”

“They were very dull,” Caelindra says.

Ysolde takes a small bite of tart. “Were the tariffs cruel?”

Caelindra looks at her, caught between dismissal and the fact that the question is plain. “Probably. I did not stay long enough to find out.”

“Then they were at least inefficient,” Rashid says.

This time Caelindra smiles without meaning to. She covers it by taking another bite.

The next flare of lightning does not bring the bridge back. It lights the high window frames and the white rim of Aldric's sleeve, then leaves the table in candlelight again. The servers pass quietly between the sideboard and the doors, gathering plates as they empty. Somewhere in the common room, a log shifts in the fireplace with a soft collapse.

When the tart has been reduced to crumbs and a bright smear of pear on his plate, Rashid draws the folded sheet back to him. He opens it, reads the first lines, and turns the paper so Ashara can see.

“I have removed immediate redress,” he says.

“A triumph for civilization,” she says.

“I now request a written account of the closure, supervised access when the court is cleared, and no public account from anyone who attended the practice.”

Thessaly's black eyes settle on the page. “No names?”

“No names.”

She gives one short nod and returns to her tart.

Aldric leans far enough to read the top half of the sheet, though he does not reach for it. “Put the request for the report first. Then the supervision. It makes the order clear.”

“You are all determined to turn it into a memorandum.”

“It is a memorandum,” Ashara says.

“It could have been a fine memorandum with a little force behind it.”

“Force is not the same as accusation.” Her gold eyes remain on the wording. “Add that the group does not ask anyone to surrender an existing booking. You cannot call the restriction unfair by asking someone else to pay for it.”

Rashid's jaw works once. Then he takes the pen from beside his plate and marks the margin. “Fine.”

“That was not difficult,” Aldric says.

“It was a concession. They are not meant to be effortless.”

“If they are made deliberately,” Thessaly says, “they can still be useful.”

Rashid looks at her over the paper. “You have a talent for making the best answer sound like an inconvenience.”

“I did not make it one.”

Ashara's tail taps the chair leg once, this time without impatience. “Bring the revised copy to the common room after you finish it. I will read it before you circulate it.”

Rashid folds the page along its old crease. “Five minutes.”

“Four.”

“You cannot edit a page in four minutes.”

“Then write faster.”

He gives her a look that would be more severe if the corner of his mouth were not already trying to betray him. “Four, then.”

Aldric sets his napkin beside his plate and rises. His white-and-gold clothes catch the firelight when he steps back from the table. “I have evening observance. Leave the copy where I can find it afterward.”

“On the common-room table,” Rashid says.

Seraphel lifts two fingers in a small farewell.

“Keep your prayer brief,
or the candles will learn
to yawn.”

“They have endured worse than me,” Aldric says, and goes toward the hall with a faint, unwilling smile.

Caelindra watches him leave, then brushes a grain of sugar from her cuff. “I am taking my book to the library before your memorandum acquires a preamble.”

“It will not,” Rashid says.

“That is what every preamble says first.”

Ysolde has returned to the window. “The western current is stronger,” she says. “The lamps will show again soon.”

She does not announce where she will go next, only remains there long enough to see whether the storm gives the bridge back. At the far end of the table, Rashid slips his request inside his folio and stands. Ashara follows him through the open doors toward the common room, their argument already reduced to the scratch of a pen and the occasional low correction.

The long table seems larger as people leave it. Candlelight holds in the varnish, in the silver at the sideboard, and in the pale path visible between bursts of cloud outside. The house has begun to carry everyone into the separate work of the evening, while the fire in the next room keeps burning under the portraits.
