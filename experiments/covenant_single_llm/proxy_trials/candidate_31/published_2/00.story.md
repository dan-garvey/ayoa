Ysolde’s grey gaze meets yours briefly. “You’re welcome,” she says. Her attention returns to the black beyond the window. “The first time I saw those lamps from above, I thought they were birds.”

“The bridge lamps?” Rashid says.

“It was snowing. The bridge had gone under cloud. The lights were moving very slowly.”

Ashara sets down her fork. “How old were you?”

“Thirteen.”

“And you were flying in a snowstorm at thirteen?”

“My aunt was with me.”

Caelindra’s mouth curves. “A reassurance of a very particular kind.”

“I told her there was a flock caught in the weather,” Ysolde says. “She told me not to fly into it.”

“Sound advice,” Rashid says.

“She was correct.”

Seraphel lifts her spoon over the dark wine syrup in her bowl.

“White wings in weather,
a road under cloud—
I might have believed it too.”

“They did not flap,” Ysolde says.

“That would have been my first concern,” Ashara says.

A server replaces the cleared dishes with the pears. Steam carries clove and red wine across the table. Beyond the window, the storm closes over the bridge again, leaving only a dull whitening of cloud where its lamps had been.

Rashid waits until the server has gone, then takes out the folded petition. “Now that the birds have been ruled out. Ashara?”

“Give it here.”

He does. Ashara reads with the page held flat against the table, one hand curled beside her glass. After a few lines she takes the pencil Rashid offers and draws a clean stroke through a phrase.

“‘Punitive closure,’” she says. “Twice.”

“Because closing the court burdens students who do not have a private place to train.”

“Then say that. You have no report. You do not know why they closed it.”

“They have told us nothing.”

“Yes. Ask them what they found. Do not decide it for them.”

Rashid looks at the crossed-out words. “The access is the point.”

“It is. Start there.” She taps the lower half of the page. “Your group loses the covered hour. You want to know what must change before a supervised hour can be offered. That is enough to ask.”

He reads the new sentence as she writes it. “It sounds less satisfying.”

“It sounds like something they may answer.”

Aldric leans forward to see the page. “Ask for any finding that affects safe use, not the whole report. There may be statements in it that do not belong at this table.”

Rashid’s eyes narrow a little. “You think they will answer?”

“I think a refusal to answer a narrow question will be harder to dress as procedure.”

Ashara glances between them. “Write the narrow question first.”

Thessaly has not touched her pear. “Where do the signatures go?”

“At the bottom.”

“Do not put them there.”

Rashid looks up. “You would rather people write separately?”

“I would rather not learn who has been made visible by a sheet I did not read.”

For a moment, Rashid says nothing. Then he turns the paper over and draws a line through the space beneath the request. “I will give people the clean wording. Anyone who wants to endorse it can send a note with it.”

“Better,” Thessaly says.

“I will write one,” Aldric says, “if the clean copy asks no more than this.”

“It will,” Rashid says.

Ashara passes him the pencil and the paper. “So will I. My booked hour remains mine.”

“It remains yours.”

Caelindra cuts into her pear. “How fortunate. A settlement which preserves both access to justice and a personal appointment.”

Ashara gives her a look over the rim of her glass. “You may mock it after you have practiced without a floor.”

“I have avoided that experience very successfully.”

The revised sheet stays beside Rashid’s plate instead of returning to his pocket. He reads it once more while the conversation loses its hold on the court.

Caelindra takes another bite of pear and pauses. “They have cooked these until they have forgotten they were fruit.”

“They are still pears,” Ysolde says.

“Only technically.”

“Their pits are intact.”

“An alarming standard for a meal.”

Ysolde considers the soft slice in her spoon. “I prefer them this way.”

Caelindra looks at her, then at the bowl. “Of course you do.”

Seraphel smiles into her own dessert.

“The pear has yielded.
Perhaps that is enough
for one evening.”

This time, even Caelindra smiles before she takes another bite.

You finish at an unhurried pace while the last of the dinner settles into smaller talk: a complaint about the first seminar’s reading list, an argument over whether pepper belongs in every sauce, Rashid’s muttered revision of a sentence that Ashara has already marked twice. The servers collect the bowls one by one.

When the storm opens again, the western bridge is there between two banks of cloud. Its lamps hold fast along the rail, though their light reaches eastward in long pale strokes over the dark.
