Ysolde turns from the window just enough to acknowledge you. “You’re welcome,” she says. “It is easy to forget that the lamps are strange.”

“Only because you have spent enough time in the air to acquire poor standards for ordinary things,” Caelindra says.

Ysolde considers this without offense. “That may be true.”

“It is,” Ashara says, reaching for the water.

The answer brings a small, uneven laugh from the table. Outside, the western bridge is gone again behind the moving cloud, and the window returns the candlelit room: the long table, the dark panels, the bright line of the serving silver. A server removes the empty dish of meat and replaces it with shallow bowls of poached pears, their skins dark with wine and spice.

Rashid waits until the server has passed, then draws the folded petition from beneath his napkin. “Ashara. You said you would read it.”

“I said I would read it.” Her tail gives one restrained movement against the chair. “I did not say you could make dinner a committee meeting.”

“It is less a committee meeting than a document with poor timing.”

“That distinction has never saved a document.”

Still, she holds out her hand. Rashid gives her the sheet. The paper looks more crowded than it did before, its first lines crossed through in a firm hand and written again beneath. Ashara reads silently while the pears steam in their bowls.

After a moment, she taps the third paragraph with one square-tipped nail. “You have written that the Academy should give a full account of the accident.”

“The conclusion of it.”

“Then write that. A full account may contain Dena’s medical details, or something another student said in confidence. You want them to state why the court is closed and when it can be assessed. Do not demand every piece of paper they possess because you are angry.”

Rashid’s jaw tightens, though his voice stays even. “I am angry.”

“Yes. That is why I am reading it.”

He lets out a short breath that is nearly a laugh. “Fair.”

Thessaly sets her spoon down. “And the signatures?”

“Only from people who choose to add them. The request goes to the faculty office, not on a noticeboard.”

“Can it be copied?”

“Not by me.”

“That is not an answer to what happens after you hand it over.”

Rashid glances at the page, then nods once. “No list of names in the request. I can ask people for separate endorsements if they want to give them. The Dean will have to accept the argument on its own merits.”

Thessaly’s black eyes rest on him for a beat. “That is a better arrangement.”

“It is also more work,” Caelindra says.

“Yes.” Rashid reaches for his pear, then stops before touching it. “It’s still the better arrangement.”

Aldric has been reading over Ashara’s shoulder without leaning close enough to crowd her. “Ask for a supervised hour after the review establishes the court can be used safely,” he says. “And say why an hour available to students without private instruction matters. That is the part you can defend without pretending to know the cause of the injury.”

Ashara looks up at him. “That is almost useful.”

“I am trying to recover from the carrots.”

“No recovery is possible,” Caelindra says. “They have gone on to whatever judgement awaits them.”

Seraphel’s smile reaches her eyes.

“For the carrots departed,
let the petition be brief,
and the pears merciful.”

“The pears are merciful,” Rashid says. “The petition remains under review.”

Ashara takes the pencil he offers. Her corrections are few: a phrase shortened, an accusation removed, the reason for the request made plainer. She passes the sheet back across the table.

“There. It asks for the conclusion, it asks for access when access is safe, and it does not borrow my training time to make itself sound urgent.”

“My training time is not in it.”

“It is not.” She lifts one shoulder. “I will sign this version.”

Rashid’s expression changes before he can quite prevent it—relief first, then the deliberate composure he wears when he thinks someone may mistake relief for a concession. “Thank you.”

“Do not make me regret it.”

“I have the revised draft.”

“You have one revised draft.”

That earns him a real laugh, and when he turns the page toward Aldric, the angel takes the pencil without ceremony. His signature is narrow and exact. Thessaly adds hers below it after reading the paragraph about endorsements twice. Ashara’s name follows, dark and angular beside the others.

Rashid folds the page once more, more carefully now. “I will speak to the group tomorrow. Anyone who wants to put their name to it can decide then.”

“And anyone who does not,” Ashara says, “does not become a symbol in your argument.”

“They do not.”

For a moment, that is enough. The petition disappears into Rashid’s inner pocket, and the table returns to dessert. The pears are soft enough to take with a spoon; the wine syrup leaves a clove-dark shine at the bottom of each bowl.

The first seminar’s reading list takes over the conversation. Caelindra maintains that assigning three founding commentaries and their annotations before the end of the week is an abuse of the word introductory. Aldric says the annotations are optional only to people who enjoy being wrong in public. Ashara points out that he has already read them. He admits it after a pause.

“I read the first volume,” he says.

“With the annotations,” Rashid says.

“Some of them.”

“All of them,” Ashara says.

Ysolde lifts her eyes from her bowl. “There are four volumes.”

“There are?” Aldric says.

“The fourth is an index of objections to the third.”

Caelindra looks briefly, almost reverently, at the ceiling. “At last. A curriculum designed by people who hated youth.”

“The index is very efficient,” Ysolde says.

“I am sure it is.”

The discussion turns from the list to the oldest professors’ habits, then to whether a person can be said to have understood a treaty if they have only read it in one language. The disagreement stays light enough to share with the pears and the last of the bread. No one presses you to supply a history, a position, or a performance for the empty human seat beside your name.

By the time the servers begin clearing the bowls, the storm has shifted again. The western bridge emerges between two banks of cloud, straight and unwavering over the dark. Its lamps cast their long pale strokes eastward, each pool of light drawn out like a feather caught in the same wind.
