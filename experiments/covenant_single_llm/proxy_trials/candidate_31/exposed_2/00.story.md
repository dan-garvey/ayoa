Ysolde keeps her eyes on the bridge until the cloud closes over it. Then she turns back to you.

“You’re welcome,” she says. “The first time I saw the lamps do that, I thought the bridge had tilted.”

Ashara looks up from her plate. “You did not.”

“I did.”

“From the dining room?”

“From the garden. I went to the rail before dawn to decide whether it had moved.”

The edge of Ashara’s mouth shifts. “In weather like this.”

“I had a coat.”

Thessaly tears another piece from the bread loaf. “Did it move?”

“No.”

“Then you found the answer.”

“At dawn.” Ysolde takes a sip of water. “The lamps were straight again, and I was late to breakfast.”

Rashid’s laugh is brief. “That sounds like the Academy’s preferred method of instruction.”

“It did not assign the storm,” Aldric says.

“It may have arranged it,” Caelindra says. “The place has a taste for theater.”

No one takes up the claim. Outside, another wash of lightning turns the window white, then leaves the dining hall gathered closely around its candles again.

You finish the meat and use a piece of bread to catch the last of the green sauce. The table does not hurry you. Rashid has the petition open beside his wineglass, but he only reads the first lines once more before Ashara notices.

“Put it away,” she says.

“I am not revising it.”

“You are thinking about revising it.”

“That is not forbidden.”

“It should be, after supper.”

He studies the page for another moment, then folds it along its old crease and slips it beneath his napkin. “Breakfast, then.”

Ashara gives a single nod and reaches for the water pitcher. The matter leaves the table with the paper.

A server clears the main dishes and sets down shallow bowls of pears poached in wine, their skins dark against pale cream. Seraphel watches the steam lift from hers.

“Has anyone seen
the north roses open?
Or do they wait still?”

“The outer bed opened this afternoon,” Ysolde says. “The ones by the wall will wait for frost.”

Caelindra looks over. “Roses wait for frost?”

“Those do. The cold hardens the outer petals first. They look bruised for a day, then open.”

“An unpleasantly dramatic habit for a flower.”

“They do not perform it for you.”

Caelindra lifts her spoon, considers the pear, and takes a bite without answering. Seraphel smiles into her bowl.

“Then let them keep
their little weather,
until the morning.”

Ysolde’s attention returns to the window, though the bridge is gone behind cloud. She tells Seraphel that the roses smell stronger after the frost, not sweeter exactly, but sharper, and that the groundskeeper cuts none of them until the first bloom has fallen. Her voice stays low, yet she has more to say on that subject than on most. At the far end of the table, Aldric asks Rashid whether the seminar’s second chapter is truly worse than the first; Rashid answers that he intends to find out only under protest.

The pears soften under their wine-dark skins. Plates empty, are collected, and the storm keeps flashing below the Academy while dinner eases into its last quiet stretch.
