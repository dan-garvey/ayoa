Ysolde watches the western bridge until a ragged sheet of cloud takes it. Then she looks back to you.

“You’re welcome,” she says. “It is clearer from the garden. The wall hides the lowest part of the storm.”

She returns to her plate, and the space after her answer stays quiet without becoming awkward. The tarragon leaves its sharp green taste on your tongue; the meat has gone tender beneath its browned edge. You eat at the pace you choose, breaking warm bread over your plate while the voices at the table settle around matters that do not require you to supply anything.

Rashid is the first to abandon the weather.

“The reading list is punitive,” he says, looking toward the folded petition beneath his plate as though it has personally assigned the pages. “The first chapter spends twelve pages explaining that an agreement can acquire different meanings in different places.”

“It can,” Aldric says.

“I understood it by the third page.”

“Then the remaining nine were a test of endurance.” Ashara reaches for the bread basket. “Which you claim to value.”

“I value it when it has a purpose.”

Caelindra turns her wineglass slowly by its stem. “The purpose is apparently to persuade us that treaties are fascinating. A doomed enterprise, but at least an ambitious one.”

“You read ahead,” Aldric says.

“I wished to know the scale of my suffering.”

Ysolde considers the dark window rather than the argument. “The dragon commentary is longer.”

Rashid looks at her. “There is dragon commentary?”

“Five volumes. The third concerns bridge jurisdiction.”

“Are the bridges interesting?” Thessaly asks.

“They are necessary.”

“That was not my question.”

For a moment, Ysolde’s grey eyes narrow in the same serious concentration she gave the gravy boat. “Sometimes.”

Caelindra’s mouth curves despite herself. “There. A recommendation fit for a bookseller’s window.”

Seraphel sets down her cup.

“Five books for a bridge,
and still it carries feet.
I admire its patience.”

“The bridge has no choice,” Thessaly says.

“Neither do most readers,” Rashid says.

That draws a brief laugh from Ashara, low enough that it nearly disappears beneath the clink of a server clearing the serving dish. The server replaces it with shallow bowls of pears poached in honey and wine, their skins dark red against white porcelain. Caelindra examines hers with suspicion.

“Is this another herb with intent?”

“No,” Ysolde says. “It is a pear.”

“You say that as though the kitchen has never complicated fruit before.”

“It has,” Ashara says. “Last winter.”

“That was cloves,” Rashid says.

“An error does not become less an error because it has a name.”

Caelindra takes a cautious bite. Nothing changes in her expression, but she takes another before setting down her spoon.

The folded sheet has not disappeared from Rashid’s attention. When the first bowls are half empty, he draws it free, smooths it against the table, and reads the new opening under his breath. Ashara glances over.

“You’ve changed the first paragraph.”

“It asks for the review’s conclusion and a timetable for supervised access. No accusation. No claim about what caused the injury.”

“And no names?” Thessaly says.

“No names.”

“Who receives the answer?”

Rashid pauses with his pencil above the page. “The students who sign, and the practice group.”

“That is two groups unless every student who signs attends.”

He looks down at the sentence, then makes a small mark in the margin. “Then it should say which one. The practice group.”

Ashara holds out her hand. He passes her the paper. Her eyes move steadily down the page; her tail rests curved close to her chair instead of tapping the floor.

“This is better,” she says at last. “The request for access follows the request for the finding.”

“Because you told me it should.”

“Because it should.” She returns the sheet. “Show me the final version at breakfast.”

“I will.”

Aldric nods once. “As will I.”

“Then neither of you may complain about the phrasing afterward,” Rashid says.

“We have not agreed to that,” Ashara says.

“You have ruined the only efficient part of the process.”

“A process that cannot survive criticism should not be sent to faculty.”

He folds the petition again, this time more carefully, and tucks it beside his plate. The court remains closed; the matter waits for morning and whatever the Academy’s review will prove. Around it, dinner goes on. Seraphel asks whether anyone has seen the north garden’s winter roses this season, and Ysolde begins to describe the way frost gathers on their outer leaves without killing them. Beyond the windows, the storm washes pale light across the glass, while the lamps inside Garvey House hold steady over the table.
