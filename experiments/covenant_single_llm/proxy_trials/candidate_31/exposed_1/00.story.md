Ysolde looks at the window a moment longer before she answers.

“The first time I saw them,” she says, “I told a groundskeeper the western bridge needed repair.”

Caelindra turns her head. “Did you?”

“The globe-light was lying sideways. It seemed obvious.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked whether I intended to repair it from the air.”

Rashid’s mouth curves over the edge of his cup. Ashara looks at Ysolde with frank interest.

“And did you?” Ashara asks.

“No. I had only been here two days. I did not know where the tools were kept.”

Thessaly laughs first, a short bright sound. Seraphel covers a smile with her fingers. Even Caelindra’s expression loosens before she takes another measured sip of wine.

“That makes the groundskeeper’s question rather less absurd,” she says.

“It was not absurd,” Ysolde says. “It was rude.”

“Those are not opposites,” Thessaly says.

Ysolde considers that, then gives the smallest nod. “No.” Her gaze returns to the clouded glass. “I still think the lamps look worse in heavy rain. They function well enough.”

The bridge has vanished again, but the next flash paints the gardens white and leaves the window full of its reflection. You finish the last of the meal while the light fades from the tablecloth. A server removes your plate and replaces the emptied meat dish with a tray of small pear cakes, their tops dark with caramelized sugar. The room smells briefly of clove and warm fruit.

Caelindra studies one of the cakes as if it has made a claim she intends to test. “I have decided Professor Windwhisper dislikes first-years.”

“Because she assigned reading?” Aldric says.

“Because she begins a course on Covenant law with nineteen pages about a procession.”

“The procession establishes the order of precedence,” he says.

“It establishes that someone had enough authority to make six delegations wait in a corridor.”

Rashid glances up from the folded petition beside his place. “You read to page nineteen already?”

“I looked ahead for evidence that the assignment would improve.”

“And?”

“The fourth footnote says the fae delegation refused the gilt cups because an angel courtier had commissioned them.”

Thessaly’s black eyes lift from the bread she is breaking apart. “Were the cups commissioned for the fae?”

“No. The account says they had been intended for the angel hosts.”

“Then refusing them was sensible.”

“It also delayed supper by an hour,” Aldric says.

“Then the angel courtier should have bought different cups.”

Seraphel’s wings settle close to her back.

“Gilt cups on a table,
silver buttons at a throat—
each witness remembers
the insult nearest them.”

Ashara takes a pear cake and breaks it cleanly in two. “Windwhisper assigns the procession because everyone thinks ceremony is decoration until it is their chair that has been moved.”

“That is a better case for it,” Caelindra says. “I still reserve the right to dislike the prose.”

“You reserve a great many rights,” Rashid says.

“Someone must.”

Ysolde has ignored the pear cake in front of her. “The account of the bridges is shorter,” she says. “It is better written.”

Caelindra looks at her. “Of course it is.”

For a while the conversation wanders through the assigned pages without turning into a seminar. Aldric disputes Caelindra’s description of a particular envoy as a peacock; she grants him the word *ostentatious* instead. Thessaly wants to know why anyone would commission a cup they did not mean to use. Seraphel offers no answer, only eats her cake with evident enjoyment. When Rashid reaches for his petition again, Ashara takes the pencil from beside his hand and circles a phrase near the top of the page.

He reads her correction, lets out a quiet breath through his nose, and copies the line over on the back of the sheet.

“That was not a request,” he says.

“It was precise.”

“You have a very broad definition of the word.”

“You have a very broad definition of urgency.”

He does not answer at once. Then he folds the rewritten page into his leather folder. “The group can read it in the morning.”

Ashara gives a single nod and returns the pencil to the table.

The servers clear the pear cakes one by one. The candles have burned low enough that the brass holders show through their pools of wax, and the storm beyond the windows seems farther below the Academy than it did at the start of dinner. A last flare catches the western bridge in the gap between clouds. Its lamps reach sideways across the dark in long white strokes.

Ysolde watches them without speaking. Around the table, Rashid settles his folder at his side; Aldric asks a server for tea; Caelindra turns the little reading packet over in her hands, perhaps looking for another footnote worth objecting to. The room stays warm, and its quiet does not need filling.
