Ysolde’s attention stays on you for a moment, grey eyes steady in the candlelight.

“You are welcome,” she says. “You will recognize them next time.”

“That is a very practical consolation,” Caelindra says.

“It is more useful than being surprised in the same way repeatedly.”

Caelindra considers this over the rim of her wineglass. “I suppose that depends on the surprise.”

“Most of them are not improved by repetition,” Ysolde says.

Rashid’s smile appears briefly, then he turns back to the food. The plates give the conversation a slower rhythm. A server brings a shallow dish of pears baked beneath a browned crust and a small pitcher of thick cream; another removes the meat platter with the quiet efficiency that has kept the table supplied without interrupting any of its arguments. You finish at the same unhurried pace, the last sharp trace of tarragon giving way to butter and pepper and the sweetness of the pears.

No one appears troubled by the space between remarks. Beyond the windows, the storm opens once in a pale sheet and closes again. The bridge is gone behind cloud, but its afterimage remains easy to picture: a straight road over nothing, its light drawn sideways by weather no oil lamp would know how to endure.

“Windwhisper’s reading list is worse than the bridge,” Caelindra says at last. “At least the bridge has the decency to be visibly dangerous.”

“Professor Windwhisper assigned history,” Aldric says. “It is not an attempted murder.”

“One hundred and twelve pages before the first seminar.”

“One hundred and eight,” Rashid says. “The appendix is optional.”

Caelindra turns toward him with measured disbelief. “You counted.”

“I wanted to know what I was complaining about.”

Ashara reaches for the cream. “That has never stopped you before.”

“It has not stopped you.”

“I complain only after inspection.”

“A distinction of great comfort to the inspected thing,” Thessaly says.

Seraphel’s wings shift against the chair back as she looks from one to the other.

“A hundred pages laid down
before a door is opened—
perhaps the lesson is weight.”

“The lesson is that Professor Windwhisper enjoys looking over the top of her spectacles while people fail to prepare,” Ashara says. “Read the chapters on the first arbitration. Skip the account of the opening procession unless you enjoy lists of fabrics.”

“I enjoy fabrics,” Caelindra says.

“Of course you do.”

“I do not enjoy other people deciding which parts of a text I ought to enjoy.”

Ashara’s mouth moves almost to a smile. “Then read all of it and suffer with distinction.”

Ysolde has taken a small spoonful of cream and is studying it with the same seriousness she brought to the bridge lamps. “The procession is useful,” she says. “It shows which signatories insisted on walking first.”

“That is not about fabric,” Caelindra says.

“No. The fabric shows who could afford to make an argument with it.”

For a beat, Caelindra has no answer ready. Then she takes another piece of pear and says, “That is at least a better reason than yours.”

Rashid retrieves the folded petition from beneath his plate. He does not begin another speech. He smooths the page once against the table and reads through the revised lines while the servers collect the dessert dishes.

Ashara leans enough to see the top half of the page. “You have written ‘immediate restoration of access.’”

“I have written ‘a timetable for restoration of supervised access.’”

“The word *immediate* is still there.”

“In the sentence asking when the Academy will say what it knows.”

“Then make the request precise.” She holds out her hand. “Give me the pencil.”

Rashid passes it over. Ashara crosses out two words, writes a shorter phrase in the margin, and returns the pencil and paper together. Her handwriting is narrow and hard-edged.

He reads it. “You want it to say that the students request the instructor’s finding, the current safety plan, and an estimate for supervised hours.”

“Yes.”

“That is three requests.”

“It is three things you actually want.”

“It also gives them three ways to decline.”

“If they decline all three, you will have a clearer problem.”

Rashid looks at the page a little longer. “That is irritatingly sound.”

“You’re welcome.”

“I did not thank you.”

“You were considering it.”

Thessaly reaches across the table for the bread basket, finds only a heel left inside it, and leaves it where it is. “Who will sign?”

“People from the practice group, if they want to,” Rashid says. “And anyone else who wants the court’s closure explained.”

“Do not write ‘the student body,’ then. You have not asked the student body.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good. I am saving us time.”

“You are,” he says, and changes a line without argument.

Aldric watches the page from across the table. “Show me that version before you take it around. A request for information and a safe alternative is reasonable. I will not lend my name to claims about the injury that none of us can support.”

“You will have the wording,” Rashid says.

“I do not attend the court,” Caelindra says. “Nor do I intend to begin because an administrative dispute has made it fashionable.”

“No one asked you to sign,” Rashid says.

“Then we have arrived at an efficient agreement.”

“I like an agreement that does not require my name,” Thessaly says. “They are uncommon.”

Seraphel looks at Rashid’s page, then at the darkened windows.

“Ask what failed,
ask what will hold—
do not name the broken thing
before you see it.”

Ashara lifts one brow. “That is a terrible sentence for a petition.”

“It was not offered for the petition.”

Rashid laughs softly and folds the paper again, this time with the care of someone who knows exactly what still needs changing. “I will bring the revised version to the group tomorrow. People can read it before they decide anything.”

“Send it to me first,” Ashara says.

“I will.”

“Before you take it around.”

“Yes.”

Her tail makes one quiet tap against the floor beneath the table. “Good.”

The servers have cleared the last of the dishes now. Candlelight settles into the varnished grain of the long table, catching on water glasses and the pale curve of Ysolde’s hand beside her empty plate. The meal has left behind a clutter of crumbs, folded napkins, and the small traces of a room used by people who did not yet know one another well enough to disguise their habits.

Outside, another flash moves through the cloudbank. For an instant, the west bridge returns beyond the garden wall. Its fixed lamps spill long, slanting strokes into the storm, each bright pool reaching east after the next.

Ysolde looks toward it. “They will be clearer from the west path in an hour,” she says, more as an observation than an announcement.

“Assuming the west path is not full of rain,” Caelindra says.

“It is sheltered.”

“It is still outdoors.”

“That is usually how paths work,” Ysolde says.

Thessaly laughs into her hand. Across the table, Rashid slides the petition into the leather folder at his side; Aldric asks the nearest server for tea; Ashara glances toward the clock above the sideboard, already measuring tomorrow against the evening that remains. The warm dining room holds them a little longer while the storm works silently below the Academy.
