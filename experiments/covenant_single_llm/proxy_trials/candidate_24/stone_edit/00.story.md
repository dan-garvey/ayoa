You make two small marks on the course sheet: one beside *Magical Literacy and Safe Practice*, and one beside *Treaty Languages and Records*. The choices are not due yet, so the sheet goes back into the primer with the rest of the papers instead of into an official box.

The afternoon's library introduction begins beneath the iron stair. A librarian with grey hair pinned in a neat coil gathers the first-years around her and takes them through the open stacks at a pace that discourages stragglers: lending desks on the ground floor, journals and maps above, study tables available until midnight so long as their lamps remain lit. At the locked cabinet of Covenant histories, she pauses only long enough to name the chancellor's signature requirement.

Then she moves on before anyone can make a discussion of the rule.

The useful parts are less grand. A row of ward manuals bears a card that reads *No casting required for consultation*. The librarian shows the group how to tell when a text assumes its reader can work magic, and where the hazard notices are kept for damaged or incomplete sources. At the records desks, she lays out three copies of the same trade compact: one in formal treaty script, one in a merchant's working translation, and one crowded with decades of marginal corrections. The last is hard to read until she points to the names that have been crossed out.

Caelindra remains beside it after the others begin to drift toward the next shelf. "A translation that conceals every amendment is no translation at all," she says.

"It is often the version people prefer to circulate," the librarian replies.

Ysolde spends several quiet minutes before a weather map of the Nexus, following old storm lines with one pale finger. By the time the introduction ends, the return route to Garvey House has acquired its own landmarks: the garden gate with its chipped stone bird, the low arch whose carved dragon jaw has been worn smooth by passing hands, and the narrow turn where the house's dark roof comes into view.

Dinner is already under way when you return. It is less ceremonial than the night before. Books and folded course sheets lie beside the water glasses, and lentils, roasted squash, and flatbread fill the dishes in the middle of the table. A narrow receipt rests beneath Rashid's plate.

After the first course, he draws it out. "The petition has been logged. The Dean of Student Safety's office says we'll have a written response by tomorrow evening."

Ashara tears a piece of flatbread in half. "A response to which request? Access, the incident report, or a date for reconsideration?"

"All three," Rashid says. "That is how the clerk entered it."

"Keep the receipt, then." Ashara sets one half of the bread on her plate. "Clerks forget less readily when paper remembers for them."

Aldric looks from the receipt to Rashid. "A written answer is reasonable. Whatever they decide, they will have to give a reason."

"That is the hope." Rashid folds the receipt once and puts it away.

Seraphel traces a small circle through the condensation on her glass.

“Let the answer name the rule,
and not merely the locked door.”

"If it names neither," Thessaly says, reaching for another piece of flatbread, "it is not an answer."

That is where the covered court stays for the evening. Later, the course sheets pull the table into smaller disagreements. Ashara objects to a rhetoric seminar's assurance that every disagreement has a mutually useful outcome. Caelindra maintains that records become difficult because someone benefits from being misunderstood. No one settles either point before the dishes are cleared.

By then the lamps are lit in the common room, and the library door stands ajar beyond it.

Professor Stone has cleared the chair beside his desk. The brass frame waits on the blotter, its glass freshly wiped, beside an open household index and a single copied page in his compact hand. At your greeting, he looks up from the book.

"Good evening, Rowan. I have names, and better dates. That is more than I expected to have by supper."

He indicates the chair, then turns the copied page toward you. It is no grand family tree, only a narrow run of entries joined by lines.

"Lenora's younger brother was Tomas Garvey. Tomas's eldest son, Jude, was your grandfather. That is the branch the inheritance follows." He taps the next line. "Willan was the son of Lenora's niece, Elspeth, from another branch of the family. Distant cousins, then, though close enough that Lenora expected him to bring papers when he visited."

Willan's entry gives a city address and, in smaller writing, an occupation: junior clerk in a municipal records office. Beneath it is a last forwarding address, and then nothing. Stone rests one finger in the blank space below.

"The household papers end there. They do not say why he missed the visit, whether he missed it at all, or what became of the blue folio. If you decide that branch matters to you, I can request the municipal ledger through ordinary channels. It will take time, and it may only give us another address."

He closes the index gently. "As for the house: the Garvey trust placed it in Academy use long before you arrived. The furniture and papers came from several households over the years. That letter was in your room last term as well. It belongs to your family, but it was not left there for you in particular."

Stone lifts the frame and offers it back by its sides, along with the copy. "You may keep both. Lenora appears to have been exactly the sort of aunt who worried about fruit, weather, and neglected documents. There are worse people to find in a line."

Beyond the tall library windows, the storm turns the distant bridges into pale strokes against the dark. Stone's lamp holds a small, steady circle over the returned letter, the copied page, and the names that now have places beside one another.
