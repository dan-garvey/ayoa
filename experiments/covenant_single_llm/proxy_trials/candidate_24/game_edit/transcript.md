**Story**

You choose the tile with the pale road running cleanly from the center toward a nearer gate, its green-painted arch beginning to disappear beneath the map's spreading edges. Thessaly leans in to inspect the join, then nods.

“That fits. Put your lantern on either entry circle.” She taps the two marks beside the center. “The road will not be offended by your preference.”

You set a blue lantern on the left circle and move it two marked spaces along the new road. Thessaly takes a forked tile in reply. It carries her amber lantern farther toward the moon gate and leaves a narrow branch reaching east. Glass clicks on painted wood as she moves the lantern two spaces.

After that, the game finds its pace. Thessaly keeps three tiles faceup between you, replacing the one taken; the map pushes outward in crooked spokes, then curls back where neither of you wants an easy route for the other. When a clear way to a gate opens, you take it. Thessaly does too, unless she can make your clear way a little less clear without spoiling hers.

On your next turn, the first blue lantern reaches the green arch. You lift it from the board and put it with the three still waiting at your side.

“Efficient,” Thessaly says. “Most people see a gate and begin arranging a grand arrival.”

Her amber lantern reaches the moon gate a turn later. She returns it to her side of the board and starts another from the center, choosing a tile with two possible continuations as though that were the only interesting kind.

Near the middle of the map, you start to send a blue lantern down a road occupied by amber glass. Thessaly catches the mistake before the lantern leaves your fingers.

“It cannot pass that one.” Her nail rests beside the amber lantern. “It may stop beside it, if the road allows. Lanterns block light.”

You take the open branch instead. It costs you a turn, but it bends toward a gate painted with a little gold fish. Thessaly studies the board, then lays a curved road that lengthens her own route without closing yours.

“You could have used the last tile to slow me,” she says.

The road she means is still visible between the lanterns: one turn of painted wood would have choked the easiest path from her amber piece.

“Most people would.” She moves her lantern. “Even when it leaves both players farther from a gate. They call it strategy because it sounds better than admitting they were bored.”

The next blue lantern comes home through the fish gate. An amber lantern follows through a narrow black arch at the western edge. You keep working the nearest routes that remain open, asking when a turn seems questionable and changing course when Thessaly points out a rule you have missed. The dried apricots dwindle between the board and the lamp.

Somewhere deeper in the house, a door shuts. The fire gives a soft inward sigh of ash.

As the roads begin to crowd one another, Thessaly talks more readily. A tutor from her childhood had insisted that Lanterns taught patience.

“He lost six games in a row to a child who kept every road straight,” she says, placing a tile. “His theory had limits.”

She turns one of the unused tiles between her fingers. This box, she tells you, predates the Academy's version; the gate symbols have not been made simpler for students. The moon's lower curve has three points, not one. The gold fish is a river serpent rendered by a painter who has done it no favors.

“It used to have teeth,” she says. “Someone decided that frightened people.”

By the time the map has filled most of the square between you, three lanterns of each color have reached their gates. Your last blue lantern stands two spaces from a bronze gate, but its road runs into a blank edge. Thessaly's final amber one waits a space from the gate marked with a white hand, equally stranded.

The three tiles before you offer no neat extension for the blue road. You choose the branching turn that carries it around the southern side of the board, move as far as the new road permits, and leave it one turn from another gate.

Thessaly considers the next three tiles longer than she has considered any of the others. At last she takes the smallest: a short bent strip of road that joins her amber lantern to the white hand. She lays it, moves the lantern one space, and returns it to the others.

For a moment she looks at the four amber lanterns gathered in front of her. Then she begins sorting them into their section of the box.

“Close enough to be interesting,” she says. “You kept checking what the rules allowed before you made the map worse. I appreciate that.”

She folds the rules card along its old crease and lays it over the tiles. The remaining apricots stay between you within easy reach.

“I will bring Lanterns here again on Thursday after dinner,” she says. “If you come, the invitation remains free of stakes.”
