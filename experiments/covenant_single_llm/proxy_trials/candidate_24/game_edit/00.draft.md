You choose the tile whose pale road continues from the center toward one of the nearer gates, a green-painted arch half obscured by the growing map. Thessaly studies the fit for a moment, then gives a small nod.

“That joins cleanly,” she says. “Put the lantern on either circle. The road does not care which one you prefer.”

You set a blue lantern on the left-hand entry and move it the full two marked spaces. Thessaly answers with a forked tile that carries her amber lantern toward the moon gate while leaving a side road open toward the board's eastern edge. Her lantern travels two spaces after it, glass clicking lightly against painted wood.

The game settles into its rhythm. Three fresh tiles lie between you after every turn; the map spreads in uneven spokes, then doubles back on itself where neither of you wants it to. You take the shortest open routes when they are offered. Thessaly does the same, except when a turn lets her make the route less short for you.

Your first blue lantern reaches the green arch on your next turn. You lift it from the board and set it beside the other three waiting in your hand.

“Efficient,” Thessaly says. “Most people see a gate and begin arranging a grand arrival.”

She sends her amber lantern through the moon gate a turn later. Then she starts another from the center without ceremony, choosing a tile that gives it two directions at once.

At a narrow join near the middle of the board, you begin to move a blue lantern along a route occupied by one of hers. Thessaly catches the mistake before the glass leaves your fingers.

“It may not pass that one,” she says, touching the amber lantern with a nail. “It can stop beside it, if the road permits. Lanterns block light.”

You take the open branch instead. It costs you a turn but leads toward a gate painted with a little gold fish. Thessaly watches the choice, then lays a curved road that extends her own route without cutting yours off.

“You could have placed the last tile to slow me,” she says.

The board between you has no answer to give. Thessaly looks at the roads a moment longer, then moves her lantern.

“Most people do,” she adds. “Even when it leaves both of them farther from a gate. They call it strategy because it sounds better than being bored.”

The next blue lantern comes home by the fish gate. The amber one reaches a narrow black arch on the western edge. Dried apricots diminish between the turns. The common room grows quieter around the lamp; somewhere deeper in the house a door shuts, and the fire settles with a soft collapse of ash.

Thessaly talks more as the board fills. A game tutor from her childhood insisted that Lanterns was meant to teach patience. He had also lost six games in succession to a child who kept every road straight, which she offers as evidence that his theory had limits. She says the version in this box is older than the Academy's copy because its gate symbols have not been simplified for students. The tiny moon has three points on its lower curve, she explains, and the gold fish is actually a river serpent with a poor painter.

“The serpent used to have teeth,” she says. “Someone decided that frightened people.”

By then, you each have three lanterns returned. The last blue lantern sits two spaces from a bronze gate, but its road ends at a blank edge. Thessaly's final amber lantern is one space from the gate marked with a white hand, equally stranded. The three tiles offered to you contain no clean extension for the blue road, only a branching turn that will take it around the board's southern side. You place it, move your lantern as far as the new road allows, and leave it one turn from a different gate.

Thessaly looks over the next three tiles with unusual care. She chooses the smallest: a short, bent stretch of road that completes the amber lantern's path to the white hand. It moves one space, reaches the gate, and leaves the board.

For a second, she simply looks at the four amber lanterns in front of her. Then she gathers them into their section of the box.

“That was close enough to be interesting,” she says. “You kept asking what the rules allowed before you made the map worse. I appreciate that.”

She folds the rules card along its old crease and places it on top of the tiles. The remaining apricots sit within reach.

“I will bring Lanterns here again on Thursday after dinner,” she says. “If you come, the invitation remains free of stakes.”
