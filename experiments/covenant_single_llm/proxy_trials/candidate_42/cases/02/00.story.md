Ysolde keeps her eyes on the window until the last pale trace of the bridge has gone.

“You’re welcome,” she says. “It looks better from the gardens. The lamps sit lower there, and you can see farther over the storm.”

She returns to her plate without suggesting that anyone go and see. Across from her, Caelindra notices a smear of tarragon sauce at the edge of her bread and regards it with open displeasure.

“If the kitchen puts that in dessert,” she says, “I’m sending it back.”

“To whom?” Thessaly asks.

“Whoever brings it.”

“The server cannot remake it.”

“Then she can tell the cook I disliked it.”

Thessaly considers that. “That seems possible.”

Rashid smiles into his cup. Ashara does not, though the tip of her tail shifts once beneath the table. A server removes the empty meat dish and replaces it with a plate of small pear tarts, their edges dark with sugar.

“No tarragon
in these,” Seraphel says.

“A relief,” Caelindra says.

“Pear tarts.
They look good.”

Ashara studies the plate. “They could have used more pear.”

“You’ve already had two,” Rashid says.

“They were small.”

“There is one left.”

“I can see that.”

Rashid takes it before she reaches across the table. Ashara looks at the empty place on the plate, then at him.

“You did that deliberately.”

“Of course.”

“Fine. Eat it.”

Rashid does, evidently without concern.

The table settles into smaller conversations. Aldric has opened a book whose spine bears the Academy seal, though he seems to have read the same two paragraphs several times. Caelindra notices.

“Is that Windwhisper’s assignment?” she asks.

“The preliminary commentary.”

“Two hundred pages is not preliminary.”

“It is a survey of the founding debates.”

“It is six writers complaining about a treaty they could not stop.”

“That is one reading of it.”

“Do you have another?”

Aldric closes the book over one finger. “It also explains why the treaty held.”

Caelindra looks across the table at him. “Held for whom? Some of those writers did very well from it.”

“For people who would have died in another war,” he says. “That was not nothing.”

“No. But it does not tell me who got the safety and who paid for it.”

Before either can take the argument further, Thessaly reaches for the book. “May I see the page?”

Aldric passes it to her.

She reads in silence, turning the book toward the candles. “I have read this sentence twice. I still do not know what it wants me to understand.”

Caelindra gives a short laugh that breaks the pause cleanly.

Seraphel leans to look over Thessaly’s shoulder.

“I lost the subject
before the end
of the first sentence.”

“Exactly,” Caelindra says.

“I think Professor Windwhisper assigned it because every clause matters,” Aldric says.

“Then she can explain the clauses in class.”

Thessaly hands the book back. “I would appreciate that.”

“You would be good at editing,” Rashid says.

“That sounds like work.”

“It is.”

“Then I would not be good at it.”

The candle nearest the window gutters when a draft slips through the old frame. Ysolde reaches past it and turns the wick down with a practiced pinch of metal between two fingers. The room smells briefly of warm wax.

Dinner eases toward its end without any formal signal. Plates are cleared. The petition remains folded under Rashid’s plate; Aldric’s book stays closed; Caelindra finishes her pear tart after all. Around you, the talk has become lower and less pointed, crossing the table in fragments while the storm flashes beneath the gardens.
