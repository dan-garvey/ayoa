Ysolde keeps her eyes on the window until the last pale trace of the bridge is gone.

“You are welcome,” she says. “I prefer the south bridge. The clouds fall away beneath it, and the lamps have more room.”

“The west is better for weather,” Rashid says.

“Yes.” Ysolde returns to her plate. “That is why I do not prefer it.”

A server removes the empty meat dish and brings a plate of pear tarts, their crusts dark at the edges and their tops lacquered with sugar. Caelindra studies them with the care of someone anticipating betrayal.

“No tarragon,” she says at last.

Seraphel looks at the tarts.

“Fruit in its proper place.
A modest triumph.”

Caelindra takes one without replying.

Beside Ashara’s plate, Rashid draws the folded petition back into view. He does not announce it. Ashara notices anyway and holds out her hand.

He gives it to her.

The page is covered in neat revisions. Ashara reads it while the others attend to dessert or their cups. Her tail lies still along the chair leg.

“Change ‘continued exclusion,’” she says.

Rashid leans slightly toward the page. “Why?”

“Because it implies you had a standing right to the court and they took it away. They will spend a paragraph denying that instead of answering your request.”

“They did take it away.”

“They suspended access after an injury. Say that. You want their reason for doing it, their safety conditions, and supervised hours once those conditions are met.”

Rashid considers the line, then takes the pencil she offers him. “Suspended access.”

“Better.”

Her finger moves down the page.

“No name. No injury details.”

“You told me not to use them.”

“I am checking whether you listened.”

“I did.”

“I see that.”

Thessaly has divided her tart precisely down the middle. “Do the people who sign it agree that the Academy was wrong?”

“No,” Rashid says. “Only that they want a clear explanation and a way back to practice.”

“Then that should be the first sentence.”

He reads it again. “It is nearly the first sentence.”

“Nearly is where people hide things.”

Ashara glances at Thessaly, then taps the page once more. “Put it first.”

Rashid scratches out two lines and begins again.

Aldric watches the revision without reaching for the paper. “I’ll read the finished version before I decide whether to sign.”

“That is what you said.”

“Yes.”

Rashid looks up at him. “And?”

“And I have not changed my mind.”

“Fine.”

The word has no warmth in it, but neither does it reopen the argument. Rashid finishes the sentence, blows lightly across the fresh ink, and folds the petition into his folio.

At the other end of the table, Caelindra notices the book Aldric has set beside his cup.

“You have begun Windwhisper’s commentary already?”

“I opened it.”

“That is not a defense.”

“It is not meant to be.”

Caelindra takes another bite of tart. “The first chapter contains a sentence that begins with a treaty and ends with an apology to three dead ministers.”

“It explains the objections to the third article.”

“It buries them under six pages of ceremonial throat-clearing.”

Ysolde lifts her gaze from the window. “The map in the appendix is useful.”

Caelindra turns to her. “You read the appendix?”

“I wanted the map.”

“That may be the only sensible approach.”

“The map has no scale,” Ysolde says.

Caelindra pauses with her fork halfway to her mouth. “Then it has learned from the commentary.”

Aldric closes the book over one finger. “It is a preliminary text.”

“Then it should have stopped preliminarily.”

Rashid’s mouth pulls briefly to one side as he drinks from his cup. Ashara says nothing, though she has taken the last pear tart before he can reach for it.

The talk loosens after that. Seraphel asks whether pepper belongs in sweet pastry and receives three different answers before anyone notices Thessaly has declined to give one. Ysolde listens to the wind worrying at the old window frame. Caelindra finishes her tart despite her earlier inspection of it.

You finish your meal at the same unhurried pace, with the storm flashing now and then beyond the garden wall. By the time the servers clear the last plates, the candles have burned low and the room has settled into the quieter sounds of cups, pages, and people lingering over an evening that has not asked anything more of you.
