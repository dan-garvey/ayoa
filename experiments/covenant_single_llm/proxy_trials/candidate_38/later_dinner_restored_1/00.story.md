Rashid’s smile returns with his next breath.

“There. A sensible measure of ambition. Nobody should be asked to govern anything before the bread has had a chance.”

“Don’t make a principle of him,” Ashara says. “He has been here for an hour.”

Caelindra lifts one brow over her wine. “Four years is ample time for the Academy to conceal the useful rules beneath six unnecessary ones.”

“Then it has made a poor contract,” Thessaly says.

Seraphel’s mouth turns against the rim of her glass.

“Still, four years  
may be enough time  
to find the good parts.”

The bread breaks open hot between your fingers, its dark crust scattering a little flour across your plate. Butter softens into the torn middle. For a while, the table permits the simple business of eating. No one asks you to account for the signet, the dead relatives in the portraits, or the empty Council chair waiting somewhere beyond the Academy’s walls.

Conversation finds smaller subjects.

A lamp-globe flickers as lightning moves through the storm below, and Ysolde’s attention goes to the window.

“The wind has shifted south,” she says.

Rashid follows her gaze. “You can tell that from here?”

“From the bridge.”

Caelindra looks toward the pale line hanging over the dark. “The bridge is made of light.”

“Yes.”

“That does not explain anything.”

Ysolde considers this. “The light is breaking differently along its lower edge.”

Ashara glances out, then back to her plate. “And that means?”

“The storm is moving beneath it.” Ysolde’s voice gains a little momentum. “If you were above it, you could see the cloud tops folding toward the eastern cliffs. The air catches there. It is—” She stops, perhaps noticing the number of faces turned toward her. “Different from watching it from the ground.”

“Would you fly in it?” Aldric asks.

“No.” Ysolde takes another bite of rice. “I would fly before it.”

“That seems like an important distinction,” Thessaly says.

“It is.”

The server arrives with dessert: small pears baked until their skins have split, dark syrup shining in the bottoms of pale bowls. Ysolde studies hers.

“They would be better cold.”

Rashid puts a hand to his chest. “At last. An opinion strong enough to injure the kitchen.”

“They are still good,” Ysolde says.

Caelindra tastes a piece, then inclines her head with grave approval. “A triumph. We have extracted praise.”

“I made a different statement.”

Seraphel laughs softly.

“The kitchen will endure  
your qualification.”

The next quarter hour settles into that kind of ease: Ashara and Rashid renewing their disagreement over whether tomorrow’s bout requires cleverness or better footwork; Caelindra declaring that any ranking board exposed to rain is evidence of institutional vanity; Thessaly insisting that weather cannot be blamed unless it has accepted responsibility in writing. Your smiles come without needing to be explained.

When the dessert plates have been cleared, a voice carries in from the common room.

“Rashid.”

A young woman stands in the dining-hall doorway with one shoulder against the frame. Her left wrist is wrapped in a padded splint, but in her other hand she holds a painted card. Behind her, two students occupy one of the common-room study tables; a deck lies spread between their cups, its gilt edges catching the firelight.

“We’ve waited long enough to establish that neither of them can shuffle,” she says. “It’s becoming a public concern.”

Rashid’s expression changes by degrees. “Dena. I said I’d come.”

“You said the first half hour after dinner.” Her tone stays even. “I did not ask you to skip dinner.”

Ashara’s gold eyes move from Dena to Rashid. “The cards were yesterday?”

“Yes,” he says. “I thought they could begin without me.”

“We can,” Dena says. “It is unpleasant being expected to.”

The silence that follows is brief, but it has edges.

“And this afternoon,” Ashara says, “you agreed to drill.”

Rashid looks at her. “I did.”

“I don’t need a hearing,” Dena says. “I wanted cards, not a meeting.”

Ashara’s tail makes one slow curve around the leg of her chair. “Then play cards. I wanted the half-hour after dinner because my bout is tomorrow. You said you had it.”

“You’re right,” Rashid says. His voice is careful now. “Dena, I’m sorry. I should have told you.”

Dena’s expression softens only a fraction. “Bring your own luck. We’ve spent ours.”

Ashara rises, pushing her chair in with one hand. Heat lingers around the place where she has been sitting.

“I’ll practice alone,” she says. “If you have an empty half-hour tomorrow, tell me tomorrow.”

Rashid gives a short nod. “Fair.”

Ashara leaves by the garden door, her footsteps crossing the common room without haste. Dena steps aside for Rashid, then looks across the dining hall toward you.

“Dena Arl,” she says. “We’re playing Crowns and Crows. Do you know it?”
