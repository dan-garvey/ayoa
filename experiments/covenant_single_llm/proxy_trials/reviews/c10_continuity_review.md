**Caelindra's wager: distinguish an odd bet from an impossible history.**

In turn 2, Caelindra says Lady Garvey “lost a wager to my mother, invented a second wager, and had all her money back before dessert.” That establishes the broad shape of the anecdote through her testimony. In turn 3, her mother supposedly claims that “no human poem could make her laugh,” while Lady Garvey wagers that she can read one “without producing so much as a smile.” Under the natural reading, Lady Garvey is betting on the absence of the mother's amusement, leaving the mother taking the opposite side of her own stated position. Rowan correctly identifies this tension in turn 4.

This is not, by itself, a logical impossibility. People accept foolish bets, bait each other, and sometimes bet against their stated beliefs. The pronoun and the target of the smile also leave some room for an alternative reading. However, the story supplies no such motivation or alternate referent. It is fair to call the original telling confusing rather than inventing an elaborate scheme to excuse it.

Caelindra's response does more than clarify one pronoun. She says “You are right” and “I told it badly,” then changes both her mother's original claim and Lady Garvey's side of the wager: the mother had denied that a human poem could make her “show pleasure,” and Lady Garvey had bet that it could make her **smile**. A laugh without a smile lets the mother claim a technical victory. Lady Garvey then wins the second bet by obtaining a request for another reading.

That revised sequence works on the stated literal terms. A laugh need not be stipulated to include a smile, and the first wager's formal condition can be narrower than the mother's boast. No established magical or social rule prevents this. Caelindra is recalling childhood and openly correcting her own account; the narration has never independently shown the historical wager taking place. Treating the correction as an admitted error in the telling is therefore warranted, without needing to assert either intentional character error or a model mistake as the hidden cause.

The rescue is nevertheless conspicuous: the distinction preserves the already announced first loss, and the room's collective eavesdropping supplies an additional punch line. These are permitted new details, with a small cost in apparent spontaneity. That cost is partly repaid by Caelindra's embarrassment, her retained dislike of the poem, and the player's explicit response in turn 5: “That version's funnier.” This is a successful clarification in the actual interaction, not an unresolved contradiction that should continue to count as a hard failure.

There is also no necessary financial contradiction in turn 3. Losing two crowns and then winning two crowns gets Lady Garvey her original balance back. Giving the received coins to the kitchen boy afterward does not make it false that she briefly had her money back before dessert.

**Stone's knowledge: initially unexplained, then explained through an offstage encounter.**

The precise information transfer is important:

1. In player turn 6, Rowan tells Stone that there is a letter from Edda in his room, that Caelindra says she once won a wager against her mother, and asks whether Stone knew Edda. He does **not** mention a poem, a first lost wager, the laugh/smile distinction, or a request for a second reading.
2. In story turn 6, Stone says, “The version Caelindra told you sounds like her,” and specifically judges “getting Lady Vaeyn to ask for a second reading” to be Edda's finer victory.
3. In player turn 7, Rowan asks whether Stone has heard “the story about the poem before.” This is the first direct mention of a poem in Rowan's conversation with Stone. It cannot explain Stone's earlier, more specific reference to a second reading.
4. In story turn 7, Stone answers, “No” and “I had not heard that story before tonight,” then describes recognizing Edda's character and Lady Vaeyn's reliance on technical distinctions. He does not identify a source.
5. In story turn 8, after Rowan points out exactly what he had omitted, Stone says he overheard Caelindra from the common room while the dining-hall doors were open.

At turn 6 alone, Stone's knowledge needs a source; it is not a plausible deduction from “won a wager” plus familiarity with Edda. A person can infer that a particular kind of bet sounds characteristic of an old friend. They cannot infer the exact request for a second reading from those facts with this level of confidence. But Stone could already know the anecdote independently, so turn 6 by itself does not prove information leakage either.

Turn 7 does not resolve that question. “I recognized Edda in it” explains his judgment of the anecdote, not his possession of its missing details. The answer also risks making Rowan's brief account seem more informative than it was. Still, “not ... before tonight” is narrower than “I first heard it from you.” The wording explicitly leaves room for an encounter earlier that evening. It would be incorrect to quote it as an absolute denial of having overheard dinner.

Turn 8 provides an actual acquisition route: Stone passed through an adjoining common room, heard the story through open doors, and later discussed it with Rowan in the library. The brief expressly connects the common room to both the dining hall and library. Stone being elsewhere initially does not prevent him from passing later. He is not one of the four conspirators whose location is restricted to outside the Nexus. No prior passage says he remained in the library continuously, and his presence did not need to be visible to Rowan. This offstage activity is permitted invention and is spatially plausible.

His earlier “No” remains conversationally misleading, but his more precise wording and later explanation are mutually compatible. Humans may be embarrassed about overhearing, evasive, or simply imprecise. Stone says he should have made the source explicit and calls his stopping rude. This gives the exchange a possible character-level explanation. It does not establish that the earlier omission was intentionally characterized from the outset, so I would not turn that possibility into an unquestioning defense of the scene.

**The remaining snag is the sequence of Edda's name and the anecdote.**

Stone's final explanation includes: “I stopped because I heard Edda's name.” In the visible dinner exchange, Caelindra calls her “Lady Garvey” through the original story in turn 3 and the correction in turn 4. The first spoken **Edda** occurs in turn 5: “Then you may have liked Edda.” By then the poem, laugh, second wager, and second reading have all been discussed.

Two readings need to be kept distinct:

- On the most natural causal reading, hearing Edda's name draws Stone to stop and listen to the anecdote. The visible order does not support that sequence: the personal name comes after the relevant details. Another earlier mention of Edda, or another later telling of the anecdote, would have to be supplied. Both are possible inventions, but neither is presently stated. Merely imagining them should not be counted as evidence already present in this transcript.
- On a narrower literal reading, Stone is already hearing the conversation while moving through or spending time in the common room, hears the relevant details, and only **stops** when he subsequently hears Edda identified by name. His stopping need not mark the start of his hearing. Nothing in the packet definitively rules out this order. Hearing the surname earlier could also have made the conversation recognizable or interesting, though he does not give that explanation himself.

Consequently, the name timing is a real weakness in the repair's causal clarity, but it is not enough to establish impossible knowledge. There is a coherent reading that requires no new occurrence of the name or repeated anecdote. The transcript simply fails to make that reading clear, and its more intuitive implication points the other way. The strongest supported classification is **a convenient retroactive rescue with unresolved timing ambiguity**, rather than either “fully clean clarification” or “proven contradiction.” Confidence is high about the visible order of disclosures and moderate about the intended causal reading.

**The corrections impose a material, localized cost on narrative quality.**

Three of eight player turns contain checks on logic or the source of knowledge: turn 4 challenges the wager, turn 7 asks about prior familiarity, and turn 8 explicitly challenges the information transfer. Turn 7 also advances the scene by bringing down the frame, and asking about a source can be natural conversation, so it would overstate matters to call all three turns wasted. Nevertheless, two correction chains consume a substantial portion of this short opening.

The dinner correction creates a credible small embarrassment and a joke the player says they prefer. Its cost is low. The Stone chain is more expensive: a quiet attempt to understand a dead relative becomes an inquiry into why the professor knows something he was not told, and the scene ends with an account of overhearing rather than developing the relationship or the letter. That could become meaningful character material, but the current excerpt has not yet earned such an interpretation. The final explanation also gives the reader another timing question instead of fully restoring confidence. This is a moderate loss of narrative flow and trust, even though the available evidence does not justify a categorical impossible-knowledge verdict.
