**Original.** The quiet fireplace setting, muted harp note, and request for another reading support the evening already in progress. Seraphel gives Rowan evidence that she followed the conflict and attended to its comic ending. Her request to leave a breath before Ada answers makes this a shared performance, with something concrete for Rowan to try or decline. That is useful participation, even though she spends more of the response interpreting the writing than revealing her own experience of hearing it.

The different clocks are relevant to Nell's hoped-for future and Ada's immediate experience of the collector. This is verse about this particular scene, not an unrelated maxim. The burden comes from accumulating finished critical formulations: they are not living in the same amount of time; the coat becomes a third person; Ada is funny without being made foolish. Each is defensible, but together they make Seraphel sound unusually prepared with an elegant assessment. The last distinction also supplies a critical concern Rowan did not raise. I would retain this as an occasional thoughtful reaction, while preferring responses that let her enjoyment or uncertainty interrupt the analysis. There is no ownership or knowledge violation in these listener interpretations.

**A.** This replaces the original's polished literary account with a direct reading of Ada's fear and difficulty helping Nell. The laugh and the request to hear the ending preserve pleasure and a practical reason to keep reading together.

The reduction in aphorism is real, although “she does not know / how to help Nell” is still a fairly complete psychological verdict. The successive instructions—read Friday, then the end, then leave a moment—make her a little more of a performance coach than a companion, but they fit her earlier direct “Bring your page. / Read it here.” They do not compel Rowan to obey.
