Four here means strong enough to retain without material rewrite, not exceptional. The constructed cases do not raise the full-session scores.

Slate gives the player a coherent, accommodating sequence of encounters, and its strongest material is the gradual recovery of family history. Its literary problem is substantial enough to keep prose/dialogue at 3. Miriam’s letter in turn 3 contains a succession of generalized instructions: decoration as protection, the distinction between owning a house and its residents, obligation versus affection, usefulness as a leash, kindness versus clues, and dinner as refuge. The combined effect is an unusually complete handbook of the story’s ethics, rather than a particular relative’s correspondence. This is the clearest concentration of the grating aphorisms the user dislikes. The tendency spreads across voices: Ysolde concludes a concrete observation about changing weather with “Getting used to it would mean ceasing to look” in turn 8; Thessaly gives the board’s verdict about asking for help in turn 12; Stone supplies “Context belongs to a letter, too” in turn 16. The issue is cumulative sameness and instruction, not the mere presence of polished sentences.

There are meaningful counterexamples. Stone’s account of Miriam teaching the conflicting translations of a transit clause in turn 4 gives her professional intelligence a concrete object. The plum cake is more humanizing than another declaration that she was wise. The damaged maps, misprinted names, repaired kettle, and final illness in turns 5 and 17 develop the family through domestic particulars. The progression of the six dated letters ends quietly without dictating Rowan’s grief. Some wit is properly contextual: Thessaly’s story about Rill arranging a bad board in turn 13 follows the game and the player’s question; her correction of “forgiving” answers something Rowan actually said. These passages should not all be flattened into a prohibition on humor or insight. Nevertheless, enough exchanges close on a miniature lesson that a general pass over the dialogue is warranted. Repeated lamps, cooling tea, squared papers, and rooms settling into quiet also become a dependable prose template.

Character distinction earns 4 because conduct differentiates people more successfully than their polished cadences do. Stone’s guarded, qualified account in turns 4–6 differs from Ysolde’s sparse attention to weather in turns 7–9. Thessaly is already occupied with a game, teaches through a specific board problem, and leaves the next attempt to Rowan in turns 10–14. Rashid manages a political effort, while Ashara supports the principle without yielding her own training reservation; Aldric withholds support pending evidence and later demands explicit safety conditions in turns 2, 18, 20, and 22. These are distinguishable dispositions, not interchangeable approval of the protagonist. The counterweight is that most selected conversations remain courteous and controlled, with several speakers sharing the same habit of making an observation into a general principle. Caelindra and Seraphel are scarcely tested after dinner. The score reflects the characters actually developed, not proof that the entire ensemble already has equally engaging voices.

Independent interests also earn 4. Rashid introduces the existing court dispute in turn 2, has obtained signatures and a hearing by turn 18, and carries his own argument at the hearings. He never needs Rowan to become its indispensable organizer. Ashara keeps a separate practical interest in her training; Aldric’s concern about an unexamined injury persists. Stone returns to his reading when dismissed in turn 7, and Ysolde’s tea and weather-watching are underway before Rowan asks to join. Thessaly’s game begins as her activity rather than entertainment summoned for him. The limitation is that these interests are mostly represented by orderly, available people, and the later political proceedings smooth the competing positions toward agreement. There is less evidence of irritation, inconvenient personal priorities, or friction between friends than the brief could support. That limits exceptionalness; it does not erase the demonstrated independence.

Direction earns 4, with two clear developments receiving meaningful follow-through, including one initiated by an NPC. The family inquiry goes from Rashid’s referral in turn 1 to Stone’s recollections, a definite appointment in turn 6, the brought correspondence in turn 16, and all six marked passages in turn 17. It produces a changed body of knowledge and a retained object—the copied letters—without artificially becoming a conspiracy reveal. Separately, Rashid’s turn-2 request becomes a hearing, a preliminary report, and a conditional supervised pilot in turns 18–22. The discovered equipment fault arrives through an investigation, rather than being assumed at the first dinner. The maintenance inquiry remains open and repairs still require certification; the ending accurately presents a substantial decision with conditions, not completed implementation. There are smaller payoffs too: Ysolde’s predicted wind arrives in turn 9, and the game instruction is used in another round. The main weakness is that the hearings become administrative recitations with little memorable interpersonal exchange. Their direction is sound, but their scene life is less strong than their causal bookkeeping.

Adaptive initiative earns 4. Rowan leaves the court argument in turn 3, and the story follows his family inquiry and tea rather than repeatedly pulling him back. Turn 9 completes his requested quiet half-hour with Ysolde without manufacturing a confession or crisis. When he returns to the court issue in turn 18, it has progressed through Rashid’s work. Their narration is not an unauthorized takeover of present choices. This is effective adaptation, although it is more reliable than surprising, and the procedural material could retain more human texture while respecting observation.

Juniper’s prose/dialogue earns 4. Its best sustained exchange is the cards-to-courtship conversation in turns 2–7. Rashid’s wish for an evening when Ashara agrees with him, her short objection to his definitions, and his later account of losing to her create social texture without requiring him to announce his devotion or exhaustion. “Apparently, I could not” in turn 7 is earned self-deprecation after a specific failed challenge. Slate does have two local documentary continuity errors worth repairing. The visible opening of Miriam’s letter in turn 0 is “To my great-nephew, should one ever come to Garvey House—”; the purported full transcription in turn 3 begins “My dear great-nephew,” then continues differently. It does not reproduce the text already seen. In turn 17, a letter presented as Miriam’s correspondence to Stone suddenly says, “They are determined you will know the world before you are asked to represent it,” using the apparent intended address to Rowan inside a letter to somebody else. No intervening forwarding or embedded address is established. These are concrete textual slips, not reasons to reject the invented family history or claims that an NPC read Rowan’s thoughts. They require local correction and do not overturn a consequential choice or plot result.

Slate’s game explanation also overstates a dead end as a turn that “cannot be corrected later” in turn 12, although the stated rules do not establish irreversible moves, and turns 13–14 permit correction. The packet does not supply enough board geometry to declare the solution impossible. This is a rules-explanation ambiguity that should be made clearer, not evidence that narrating the player’s authorized retries was improper.

Juniper’s “no names attached” in turn 11 is imprecise beside Ashara’s immediate signature and Aldric’s later one. The request’s signatories and the identification of the injured student are different things; the phrase does not distinguish them. In the injury-dispute context it can refer to not naming the participant or assigning personal blame. I would flag the wording, rather than declare a demonstrated anonymous-petition contradiction. Aldric has not made Slate’s stronger pledge that he will refuse to sign anything before seeing the report; his Juniper behavior should be judged on Juniper’s own exchanges.

Cinder passes its demonstrated boundary tests. In turn 0, Caelindra asks for the unresolved ferrywoman’s choice and returns the pages. Harbor is a successful short continuation of the visible music invitation, with a small callback weakness. It delivers an actual performance, including lyrics, musical change, audience behavior, and an ending, rather than postponing the promised song. The open west window and river-song follow the supplied history. Seraphel’s spoken contributions remain presented as short verse or song; her remarks do not disclose the conspiracy. Ysolde responds to a concrete low sound, Thessaly marks the rhythm, and Rashid relaxes without taking over the scene. The final offer of another song leaves the next choice open, with no prescribed response or feeling for Rowan.

The lyric personification is appropriate to an old river-song, and Seraphel’s remark about explanation works as a performer’s dry introduction. These are not automatically grating aphorisms merely because they are polished. Rashid’s “The chairs have missed a great deal” is less precise: the visible history made the chairs the repeatedly exposed audience, so the literal direction of the callback is awkward. It is figurative praise, not a demonstrated change to who heard a past performance, and needs at most a line edit. This one reply supports the ability to fulfill an ordinary social invitation; it cannot establish full-session range or prove behavior under unseen earlier context.
