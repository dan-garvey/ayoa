I did not consult a comparison key, other candidates, pilot material, source drafts, or editing instructions. Labels are evaluated separately in each case. These are passage comparisons with zero fresh player turns, not a continuation trial or whole-session assessment.

**Premise.** Rowan is answering Caelindra's question about his writing, then explicitly turning toward Seraphel to ask about her music. He has described an unfinished argument, not offered a manuscript or requested a workshop. Some spontaneous discussion is entirely plausible: saying that the ending is unresolved can invite suggestions in ordinary conversation. The relevant question is proportion and responsiveness, not whether another character is ever allowed to offer an idea.

Z best fits that particular submission. Caelindra acknowledges the premise, the other diners need not all join in, and Seraphel answers the question and offers something she can contribute after dinner. The harp invitation is sensible initiative from a person who enjoys music; it neither presumes Rowan's acceptance nor establishes sudden intimacy. The racial detail of her wings stays physical rather than interpreting a gesture as a hidden feeling. The other diners' brief actions let the exchange change direction without forcing an interruption or a new plot.

This is a narrow preference because Z's acknowledgment is thin. Caelindra initiated the inquiry, but her response mostly repeats the setup and registers its intelligibility. The silent spoon, paper, water, and broth business supplies placement more than independent purpose. That is permissible in a quiet dinner, but it is not by itself richer characterization. A reader who wants a lively table could reasonably prefer Y. I would not call Z an unqualified improvement in prose or entertainment.

Y has the strongest compact observation about the premise: Caelindra distinguishes the immediate consequence of pawning the coat from the uncertainty of obtaining work. That is a substantive response rather than generic encouragement. Rashid's proposed coordination and Ashara's objection also give the group something to disagree about. The problem is that the exchange rapidly assigns four diners a response to Rowan's story before honoring his change of addressee. Ashara's certainty about neither sister having the coat when needed exceeds what the bare premise establishes about timing and location. This is an inadequately supported opinion within dinner conversation, not a demonstrated inconsistency in the manuscript. Thessaly then summarizes the situation through a bargain, which feels more like her assigned thematic function than a particular thing she wants here. The wit is not inherently objectionable; its distribution makes the table feel arranged around the premise.

X contains the most energetic disagreement and some recognizably different instincts: Caelindra gives craft direction, Rashid challenges her distinction, Ashara suggests a practical obstacle, and Thessaly tries a practical resolution. Those are real strengths, and unsolicited suggestions remain NPC speech rather than changes to Rowan's story. However, this is the most intrusive response to a player already addressing someone else. Caelindra's appointment-versus-deadline dispute takes up space without convincingly establishing why the interview lacks urgency, and the coat suggestions make the diners behave like a writing group almost immediately. Her rejection of selling after the interview is insufficiently explained to become an interesting substantive disagreement. X's later music invitation is good, but largely the same opportunity exists in the other versions.

There is no definite player-authorship violation in any premise version: none says Rowan adopts an idea, supplies him with fresh speech, or fixes his ending. Nor does any diner require undisclosed information to understand the premise. X and Y's problems are chiefly overconfident inference and conversational proportion. Z's gain is restraint at this moment, bought with a somewhat bland reaction from Caelindra.

**Reading.** This is a reciprocal private exchange after Rowan has listened to Seraphel's complete songs and she has invited him to bring his page. The submission supplies the twelve lines themselves, names the sisters, and authorizes two performed voices. An interpretation or personal preference from Seraphel is therefore welcome. A successful response can remain quiet; it need not propose revisions, advance a romance, or introduce intrigue.

Z is the strongest. Seraphel notices Ada's earlier burden of answering the collector and then responds to the last line's practical absurdity. Her stated preference for Ada makes her a reader with a taste, rather than a device for confirming that the two sisters have understandable positions. That preference is modest enough to leave room for disagreement; the passage does not pronounce the author's meaning or require Rowan to endorse it. Her interest in a next line develops naturally from having enjoyed this one page. The delivery remains brief verse, fitting both her binding and the intimacy of listening beside the fire without manufacturing a revelation.

Y is also good. The precise hours and compressed image of the coat make the scarcity legible, while her attention to the bottom of the page gives a concrete sign that the final line caught her. This is more immediate than X's statement of willingness to wait for more. Its limitation is that most of her response remains organized around the premise's clock and central object; it tells us less about her individual allegiance or enjoyment than Z. The poetic compression is appropriate to someone responding to performed writing and is not a fault merely because it is polished.

X is coherent and considerate. It recognizes both sisters and acknowledges the unfinished ending without trying to resolve it. Its weakness is largely generic: the reader reports that she heard both sides, then registers interest in continuation. That could fit many listeners and many two-person arguments. Y makes the reaction more concrete; Z makes it both concrete and personal.

Z's image of a closing door is an interpretation of an actual submitted line, not an assertion that Rowan wrote an additional event. All the material she singles out is on the page she has just heard. None of these alternatives invents manuscript dialogue, adds Rowan's speech, imports private biography, or claims privileged knowledge of the sisters' eventual lives. Z's greater emotional specificity is not a boundary problem. The gain is clear but local: it is a better literary response within an already functioning exchange, not proof of sustained relationship development.

**Game.** Thessaly has independently offered a game while Ysolde reads and Rashid writes elsewhere in the common room. Rowan asks about exhaustion of the seven stones, then explicitly delegates one complete game played under the explained rules, with efforts both to connect white and block black. That authorizes summarized placements, tactical attempts, and a completed result. It does not authorize fresh player speech; all three versions repeat only the question he actually supplied.

X and Y answer the missing rule plainly and carry out the requested activity. Each reaches a draw after seven stones per side, with no movement of placed pieces. Each also lets Thessaly invite another game, an ordinary expression of her own interest. They are quiet but successful continuations, not failures because she does not create a new problem or resist Rowan. Their differences are mostly cadence and compression. Y's shorter invitation has a little more ease; X's language about joining the notches is a little more exact. I see no meaningful overall gap between them.

Z adds enough interaction to earn a slight preference as social fiction. Thessaly reacts to a contested route and anticipates a blocking move, so her enjoyment and competitive attention emerge during play instead of only in the result. Her teasing is tied to the board, rather than being an unrelated observation about contracts or Rowan's personality. The passage also preserves the independently occupied room around them. It does not make Ysolde and Rashid a watching chorus or reward a single game with private disclosures. Her claim on an anticipated route reads naturally as playful possessiveness, not a newly imposed ownership rule or a deliberate fae lie.

There is, however, a real clarity concern at game packet line 802. After the first white placement and first black answer, the narration describes Thessaly's second black stone before describing Rowan's next white stone. Read as a complete chronological sequence, that reverses the taught alternation. Since this is compressed game narration and the white move is not explicitly numbered as his second, a legal sequence with an omitted white turn remains possible. I would flag the ordering as ambiguous rather than claim the packet conclusively demonstrates two consecutive black turns. The later moment when she holds a stone while predicting Rowan's block is not independently illegal: holding a piece does not place it, and her eventual black placement can follow his white turn normally. Z is less secure than X and Y on this presentational point.

The board has no complete diagram or crossing inventory. None of the versions supplies enough geometry to demonstrate that its blocking routes or final draw are impossible. The last black stone blocking an already unfinished white chain is also legal even though white has no further stones with which to exploit that route. It need not be a strategically necessary defense to be a valid final placement. A novice drawing once with an experienced player is not inherently implausible on the information given. These are limits on what can be established, not reasons to invent additional rules.

No game version gives Thessaly access to Rowan's expressly private recollection of Jory Hale, and the background activity in Z was already established in the room. There is no unsupported knowledge relay to repair. With the move-order qualification, Z offers a small gain in character presence; X and Y remain cleaner descriptions of the delegated activity. That qualification prevents treating Z as an unequivocal replacement on every dimension.

Across the three comparisons, the visible improvement is uneven and bounded. The clearest advance is a listener acquiring a particular preference grounded in the player's actual page. The game gains a little competitive personality, with a continuity cost. The premise gains better respect for a conversational pivot, with a cost in liveliness. These passages favor selective specificity over either universal commentary or universal reticence. They do not establish that any unseen edit or configuration improves a sustained campaign: no fresh player responses, delayed consequences, or longer-run consistency were tested here.
