The lamp on the desk burns with a small green flame, quiet enough that the clock beside it can be heard ticking. Beneath its shade, someone has set the supper card squarely on top of the lawyers' envelopes: **FIRST-YEAR WELCOME DINNER — DINING HALL — SEVEN BELLS.** The inherited Garvey signet catches the light along its worn engraving.

A framed letter leans against the back of the desk. Its brown ink has faded at the edges, but the salutation remains sharp: *My dear Edmund*. The writer complains about the plum trees in the garden, promises a room made ready for a visit, and adds a postscript forbidding muddy boots on the stair. A different great-nephew, apparently, had once been expected here.

Outside the window, pale garden paths wind between clipped hedges and white-flowering trees. Beyond their walls, one of the light bridges reaches away over the storm, a blue-white line suspended above slow lightning. The house itself is warmer than the view suggests. A fire works somewhere below, and the air in the corridor carries wax, roasted onions, and the dry smell of old wood.

Voices rise through the stairwell.

“Tomorrow morning,” says a young man's voice, careful and firm. “That gives people time to put their names down without making the request look improvised.”

“It will look improvised if you continue discussing it at dinner,” another voice replies. A woman, low and level. “Which you are doing.”

“I am discussing when to discuss it.”

“A distinction fit for a speech.”

There is a brief laugh from someone else, then a third voice, more formal. “The court was closed because a student was injured. Surely a temporary precaution does not become an insult merely because it inconveniences you.”

“It inconveniences people who have no other place to train,” the first voice says. “That is the point.”

The low-voiced woman answers after a moment. “And tomorrow I have a ranked bout. I can believe both things without lending you my booking.”

“I didn't ask for it.”

“You were about to.”

The scrape of a chair follows, along with a small burst of laughter that sounds less hostile than the words did. From farther off comes a woman's voice in plain, unrhymed lines:

“If the food is cooling,
the argument has already lost.”

A pair of crisp knocks lands on the door.

“Mr. Garvey?” a server calls from the hall. “The first bell has gone. The dining hall is downstairs, through the common room.”

The door stands unlatched. Warm light lies across the corridor runner toward the turn of the staircase, while below, glasses clink and the conversation gathers itself for another round.
