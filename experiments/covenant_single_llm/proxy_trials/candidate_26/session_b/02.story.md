The server draws out the chair at your place and pours water from a narrow-necked glass bottle. Steam rises from the bread as you break it. Across the table, Caelindra's fingertip remains tucked in her book.

“Then someone has made a careless beginning,” Aldric says. “I do not expect you to know our customs tonight.”

Ashara takes the seat to the left of yours, her tail settling in a loose curve along the floor. “No one should expect a signature from someone who has just said he lacks the facts.”

Rashid sets his folder beside his plate instead of opening it. “Plainly said is better than a gracious answer you don't mean. There was an injury, there are students who need the court, and there is an instructor's report I haven't seen. That is all I will claim tonight.”

“Good,” Ashara says.

“We agree on something already.”

“We agree that supper should occur before your petition.”

At the far end of the table, Ysolde turns her water glass slowly between both hands. “The covered court needs repair whether it opens tomorrow or not. The roof makes a different sound after rain.”

“That is useful,” Rashid says.

“I thought so.”

Thessaly accepts a small dish of butter from the nearest server, examines the spoon in it, and passes the dish on without taking any. “An empty square beneath a petition is better than an uninformed name. I have seen more trouble begin with a name.”

Seraphel lowers her eyes to the place setting before her.

“No seal should be set
before the words are read.”

The first course arrives: a clear, fragrant broth poured around dumplings no larger than walnuts, with bright strips of leek and a scattering of black pepper. The servers move down the table in Academy blue, quick enough that the bowls seem to appear all at once.

Caelindra finally lifts the book from her knee. The cover is dark blue cloth, its title stamped in tarnished silver: *The Mapmaker's Widow*.

“It is a novel,” she says. “A mystery, though the mystery is solved rather early. Four cousins believe a map has been stolen from their grandfather's house. By the time anyone discovers the map was hidden in the frame of an embarrassing family portrait, they have ruined two dinners, one engagement, and a conservatory.”

Rashid's mouth tightens against a smile. “A conservatory is difficult to ruin by accident.”

“One of them has a tiger,” Caelindra says. “The author is determined to make the reader forgive this.”

“Does the largest house own the tiger?” Ashara asks.

“No. The largest house belongs to the second cousin, who owns too many keys and is guilty of something much duller than theft.” Caelindra sets the book beside her bowl. “The house itself is blameless.”

“A generous finding,” Aldric says.

“It has not once made a claim about its own virtue, which is more than I can say for its owner.”

Thessaly looks toward the shadowed portraits along the wall. “That seems a practical advantage to being a house.”

For the first time since the table filled, Caelindra's expression loses some of its practiced distance. It is brief, but her laugh is real and quiet.

“Perhaps. Still, the author keeps describing the windows very well.” She turns the book so its cover faces you, then rests her hand over the title. “What mysteries have you found worth finishing?”
