Caelindra gives a small nod. “Then it will be yours when I finish it.”

Thessaly gathers the red birds into her palm and closes her fingers around them. “One practice round is reasonable. You may complain about your own mistakes as much as you wish. Complaints about the rules must identify the rule.”

“She will make you identify the page,” Rashid says.

“There are no pages.”

“Then the card.”

“There are four cards. That is manageable.”

Dinner moves on around the argument. The broth bowls give way to flat dishes of roasted squash, dark greens, and crisp-skinned fish, with little pots of sharp herb sauce set within reach of every place. Conversation loosens as the servers refill water and clear the first course.

Ashara tells Seraphel, with the air of answering a question that has been asked several times before, that her opponent's high cut is not difficult to stop; it is merely irritating to stop repeatedly.

“A shining blade repeats its boast,
until the hand grows tired,” Seraphel says.

“Precisely.”

Rashid asks whether the open ground will be muddy after the storm shifts. Ysolde says it will not, unless the rain comes from the east, and then describes the drainage channels with enough precision that Aldric lowers his spoon to listen. Caelindra interrupts only to object that the Academy has spent three years repairing a fountain nobody uses while leaving the library handrail black with soot.

“The fountain is ceremonial,” Aldric says.

“So is the handrail, apparently.”

Thessaly has set the game pieces in a tidy row between her glass and the salt. She does not touch them again until the dessert plates have gone and the servers have begun folding the napkins left on empty chairs.

Ashara rises first, fastening the cuff at her wrist. “Open ground,” she says to Rashid. “If your visitors arrive before I return, do not let them mistake your folder for a treaty.”

“I will try to restrain myself.”

Aldric and Seraphel leave together toward the chapel, their white and gold disappearing through the far doors. Ysolde pauses at the garden entrance long enough to look up at the bridge lights, then goes out toward the terrace. Caelindra carries *The Mapmaker's Widow* beneath one arm as she turns toward the library stair.

“The lamps should be filled,” she says. “If they are not, I shall find whoever is responsible.”

“You will need a larger game board,” Thessaly tells her.

“I have no intention of playing.”

When the dining hall has thinned to the clink of plates being gathered, Thessaly leads the way back into the common room. Rashid remains by the hearth with his folder open; two students arrive a few minutes later and take the chairs opposite him. Their voices stay low over the paper.

Thessaly claims the small table near the fire and lays out a board of seven narrow arches joined by thin silver lines. She sets one pale wooden crown at each end, then places a red glass bird beside each crown.

“You are trying to make a circuit,” she says. “Your bird leaves its crown, crosses three different gates, and returns home. I am trying to stop it. On a turn, you move either a bird through an open gate or one of your towers onto a gate. A tower closes that gate until it is moved. Neither player may break a tower.”

She places three black towers beside each crown and pushes one set toward you.

“A bird cannot cross the same gate twice in a circuit. If it has crossed three, it must still find its way home. That is where new players usually lose.”

The first few turns pass with Thessaly naming the possibilities without choosing for you. When a tower closes one route, she points out the other two. When a bird reaches its second gate, she lets the silence sit long enough for the board to become less like a collection of pieces and more like a set of narrowing paths.

By the fifth turn, your bird has crossed three gates and stands one move from its crown. Thessaly's last tower slides into the only open return.

“There,” she says. “You had a circuit. You used the gates in the wrong order.”

She lifts the bird from the board and sets it beside its crown. From the hearth, Rashid's visitors begin a careful account of the accident while he makes notes in the margin of his request.
