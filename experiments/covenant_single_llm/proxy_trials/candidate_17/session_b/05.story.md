“It was the shortest route available,” Ashara says. “You had already found the room.”

Upstairs, the letter waits where you left it, its frame cool beneath your hands. The paper inside is thinner than it first appeared, the ink pale in places but still legible beneath the glass.

*To the great-nephew I never expected,*

*Your grandmother says I am not allowed to make plans for a child who does not yet exist. I say a person can be welcome before he arrives, especially in a house with room enough for too many opinions.*

*I have put aside the garden room, though by the time you read this it may be a pantry, a study, or occupied by a friend you have not yet met. Do not turn anyone out for it. A house that makes you choose between inheritance and company is built badly.*

*You will be told the Garveys had duties to the Council. We did. We also had bad knees, unpaid library fines, and an unreasonable affection for cardamom buns. I hope you inherit the last thing.*

*With love,*

*Aunt Miriam Garvey*

The library is quieter than the dining hall, its lamp-oil air holding the last warmth of the day. You carry the frame up the iron spiral stair. On the upper gallery, a single lamp burns beside a desk crowded with student papers, an open book, and a cup gone cold. The man behind it has iron-grey hair and a lined, narrow face; he looks up when you stop where the light reaches you.

His eyes go first to the frame, then to the signature.

“Miriam Garvey. I knew her well enough to fear her cardamom buns, which she baked like a woman conducting an interrogation. Sit down, Mr. Garvey; I can spare ten minutes, and you deserve at least one answer from someone who knew the name before it became a brass plaque.”

An empty chair faces the desk, with the gallery rail and the darkened shelves beyond it.
