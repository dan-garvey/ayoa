The north ring is smaller than the ranked octagon, bordered by packed gravel and a rack of padded staves. Nine other students wait inside the marked circle when Master Lorne checks the ledger. The instructor gives a short nod at your introduction and passes over a staff wrapped in worn blue leather.

“No rankings here,” Master Lorne tells the group. “Distance, balance, and stopping. If you cannot stop safely, you do not get to go quickly.”

The first drills keep everyone on chalked marks: one foot forward, knees loose, the staff held with space between the hands. Master Lorne moves through the line, correcting grips and shifting elbows with quick taps of a wooden pointer. Then come falls onto the sand, practiced first alone and then after a partner’s controlled push.

At the signal to halt, every staff freezes where it is. One student finishes a swing a fraction late; Master Lorne sends them back to the starting mark and has the whole line repeat the drill.

The final exercise is only an approach and retreat. Padded staves meet, separate, and meet again under the instructor’s count. By the end of the hour, dust has worked into the knees of the practice clothes and the blue leather has left a faint impression across your palms.

As the students return their equipment, Master Lorne looks over the ring. “Same time next week. Practice the stance if you want, but do not practice striking without a partner and a clear halt.”
