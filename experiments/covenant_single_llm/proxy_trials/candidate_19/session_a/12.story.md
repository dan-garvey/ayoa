Thessaly's smile appears promptly this time. She clears a square of tablecloth between the apricot dish and the lamp, then opens the box.

Inside are small painted road tiles, a round board marked with eight gates, and two sets of glass lanterns: one deep blue, one amber. The rules card is written in an admirably plain hand. Each player keeps four lanterns. On a turn, a player lays one road tile against the growing map, then moves one of their lanterns along a connected road. A lantern that reaches any gate is returned to its owner; the first player to return all four wins. Roads must meet at their edges. No tile may be moved after it is placed.

“That is the whole game,” Thessaly says. “The roads become inconvenient on their own.”

She takes the amber lanterns and leaves the blue set nearest you. For her first turn, she selects a tile with a straight road and a branch, fits it against the board's central square, and sets one amber lantern on the new road. She moves it two spaces toward the gate marked with a tiny painted moon.

“Now it is your turn. You may extend a road that helps you, block a road that helps me, or make a mess for both of us. That last option is popular with children and diplomats.” She places three tiles faceup between you. “Choose one, then put a blue lantern on the map.”
