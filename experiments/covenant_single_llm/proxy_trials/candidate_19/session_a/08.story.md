Ashara selects a blade from the rack and stands opposite you, far enough away that the two wooden points cannot meet by accident.

“Right hand below the guard. Thumb along the grip. Your other hand goes here.” She places two fingers against the pommel of your blade, then lets go. “Do not close both hands as though you are trying to wring water from it. A blade needs room to move.”

She sets your right foot toward her, shifts the other back, and taps the outside of your knee with the flat of her practice blade. “Soft. If your legs lock, the first person who touches you decides where you stand.”

For the next ten minutes, the work is small and exact. Advance one step. Retreat one. Keep the point between you and the person across from you. The first time you try the sequence, the blade rises too high; Ashara catches it lightly near the guard and lowers it until the point is level with her shoulder. When your back foot begins to cross behind the other, she stops the movement with a word and has you begin again.

“No apology,” she says after the second correction. “Again.”

The timer clicks steadily at the court wall. She changes the distance, takes a slow step toward you, and makes you retreat without turning away. Once, the heel of your front foot lands flat and she has you reset it. Once, your grip tightens again; she taps your knuckles with the tip of her blade. By the final repetitions, the sequence remains awkward but no longer collapses as soon as she moves.

At the last click, Ashara lowers her weapon. You thank her and return the practice blade to its place.

“You listen when corrected,” she says, closing the rack. “That is more useful than pretending the first attempt was meant to look like that.” She picks up her case. “Practise the stance without a blade for five minutes at a time. If you decide you want formal lessons, find an instructor with room to give them. Do not make promises about training to someone who has not offered them.”
