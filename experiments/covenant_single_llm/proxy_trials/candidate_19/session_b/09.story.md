The two choices sit together on the course sheet without asking you to make more of them than they are. In the afternoon, the library introduction gives the building a shape: open shelves on both floors for Conclave students, tables near the lamps for shared work, and the iron stair leading to older collections above. A librarian points out the locked cabinet of Covenant histories with a brisk reminder that it opens only on the chancellor's signature. The rest is less forbidding—catalogue drawers, maps kept flat beneath felt, and enough alcoves that a person could read without becoming part of anyone else's conversation.

You have time afterward to retrace the route through the quadrangle and return to Garvey House without treating the gardens as a puzzle. Dinner passes in the ordinary noise of plates and voices. When you return to the library that evening, Stone has cleared a space on his desk and set the brass frame beside a single sheet copied in his small, even hand.

“Good evening,” he says. “I found enough to answer the question properly.”

He turns the copy so it faces you. “Lenora was the sister of your great-grandfather. The legal inheritance did not pass through her, but it ran alongside her branch; she was not a stranger whose name happened to survive in the house.” He taps a second line. “Willan was one of her great-nephews. The household index gives a forwarding address in the city and, later, the note ‘bookbinding apprentice.’ Nothing in it records why he missed the visit—simply a letter to someone who did not arrive and an aunt who kept it.”

Stone leans back in his chair. “Lenora handled household correspondence for years. She wrote invitations, complaints, reminders, requests for supplies. The surviving index makes her sound formidable about fruit and entirely unwilling to let anyone forget a promised book.”

He leaves the copy within reach of the desk's edge. “It is not much, perhaps. But it is a real person at the end of the line, rather than a title on a lawyer's page.”
