“Keep it,” Stone says. “I made the copy for that reason.”

The date beside Willan's forwarding address is written in the same compact hand as the rest of the index. Stone follows your attention to it.

“Sixty-eight years,” he says. “Seven before the collapse.” He folds his hands over one another on the desk. “Willan was already old enough to be apprenticed. If he lived, he would be an old man now. The index has no death entry for him, but its records become thin after that year. Absence is not an answer.”

The library's lamp gives the copied address a yellowed, older look than the ink deserves. “I would not post a letter to that address and tell you it had a good chance of finding him,” Stone continues. “I cannot even promise that street is still called the same thing. But the Commerce office keeps civil-post inquiry forms for old residences. They can search later directories and, if a household is still registered, forward a letter or return the inquiry. It is slow, and it may return nothing.”

He draws a blank sheet from a drawer and sets it beside the copy without pushing it toward you. “If you decide to write, begin with the introduction you described. I can help you make the inquiry afterward. A letter does not have to promise more than its sender knows.”
