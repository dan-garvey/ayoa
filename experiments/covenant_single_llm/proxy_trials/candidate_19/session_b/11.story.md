Stone reads the letter where it rests on the desk. He does not reach for a pen or offer an alteration.

“Yes,” he says when he finishes. “It says what it needs to say.”

From a lower drawer, he brings out a thin blue form stamped for civil-post inquiry. The questions are plainer than the library's papers: last known address, approximate date, name of the person sought, return address, and reason for the search. Stone fills in the old city address from the index, records the year beside it, and writes *family correspondence* in the final space.

“The Commerce office receives this form first,” he says. “Your letter stays sealed until they find a current forwarding address or a registered household willing to receive it. A clerk has no need to read what you wrote.”

He folds the form around a narrow envelope, copies Garvey House and the Academy beneath your name, and presses the edge closed with a small dab of red sealing wax. The seal bears no family crest, only the library's open-book mark.

“Outgoing inquiries are collected at dawn,” he says. “I will put this in the history courier pouch. If the search turns up nothing, the form comes back here, and the letter comes back unopened.”

He sets the sealed packet beside the brass frame, separate from the books and close enough to the lamp that its wax catches a dull red shine.
