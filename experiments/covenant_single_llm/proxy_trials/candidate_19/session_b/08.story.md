The proof of admission is in the lawyers' packet beneath a list of garments and household effects: a stiff cream card stamped with the Academy seal, naming Rowan Garvey as a first-year Conclave student. The Garvey signet is noted beside it as identification, not decoration.

Breakfast is quiet at this hour, the long dinner table reduced to a few places and a pot of strong tea. By the time you leave the house with the primer in hand, the gardens are bright with flowers that have no business blooming together. The diagram proves reliable. Pale paths carry you past clipped hedges and the low dragon-carved arch into the quadrangle, where black stone, white marble, and living timber meet under the slow flicker of the lamp-globes fading with the morning.

The seminar room's oval door is open when you arrive. Inside, the fifteen chairs circle a table in polished dark wood; the framed Accords around the walls repeat themselves in seven different scripts. There is time to choose a clear seat before the other first-years begin to file in, speaking in low fragments or not speaking at all.

At third bell, Chancellor Ashworth enters with a narrow stack of course sheets. Age has silvered his hair, but there is nothing fragile in the clean line of his white-and-gold robes. He waits until the last chair has stopped moving.

“The Conclave is not a reward for being finished,” he says. “It is preparation for work that will outlast the Academy. This term, every one of you will study Covenant Law, practical rhetoric, and comparative institutions. The rest of your programme is chosen for what you need to understand, not for what anyone expects you to become.”

He passes the sheets around the table. Beneath the required courses are smaller studios: Treaty Languages and Records; Political Economy; Land, Borders, and Supply; Conflict De-escalation; and *Magical Literacy and Safe Practice.* The last is marked open to any student, with a note in smaller type: *No casting required. Covers active workings, consent, wards, hazard recognition, and requesting qualified aid.*

Ashworth's finger rests briefly on that line as he continues. “No student is admitted on a promise of power. If you cannot cast a ward, you may still learn to read one, question its terms, and recognize when it is unsafe to proceed. If you are uncertain whether you can cast, the Arcana faculty offers private aptitude consultations after the first week. They are assessments, not verdicts.”

At the bottom of the page, a separate line names the afternoon's introduction to the Academy library and tomorrow's first meetings with Professors Windwhisper and Kira. The studio choices are due at the end of the week.
