The next morning begins before the house fully wakes, and the course sheet remains tucked inside the primer through both of its first meetings.

Professor Windwhisper's room smells faintly of rain-damp leaves and old ink. She gives the class a dispute over a river crossing, then asks them to find the point at which a practical agreement becomes a legal obligation. Her voice never rises. “A treaty is not made fair by being admired,” she says, one long finger resting on the copied clause. “Read what it permits. Read what it requires. Then ask who must live inside the difference.”

Professor Kira's Practical Rhetoric meeting is warmer in every sense. She has the students turn a simple request for shared study space into three different arguments: an appeal to a schedule, an appeal to a relationship, and an appeal to the person with the key. “Do not begin by proving yourself clever,” she says, moving between the tables. “Begin by knowing what answer you need.”

By the time the afternoon has thinned toward sunset, the words on the course sheet have acquired faces, rooms, and particular kinds of work. At the west stair, the wind has already found the seams of the quadrangle. Below the balustrade, the storm moves in slow black folds without thunder, lit from within by occasional white threads.

A few minutes later, Ysolde comes along the western walk before the sun has reached the horizon. Her white hair is tied back more simply than it was at dinner, and she carries no book. She looks once at the bank of cloud below the plateau, then at the western sky.

“The cloud base held,” she says. “That is useful.”

She takes the stairs toward the terrace at an unhurried pace. The western edge of the Academy opens beyond a low stone wall, wide enough for the storm to fill the view. Gardens lie behind it under their first lamps; ahead, the sun lowers through a narrow break in the clouds, turning the high air pale gold.

Ysolde rests one hand on the cold wall. “Do not look directly at the lamps. Look where their light meets the underside of the cloud.”

At first there is only blue-grey weather and the white flicker of distant lightning. Then the lamps brighten one by one behind you. Their light catches the low cloud from beneath, and a muted green spreads through the storm's folds, not vivid but deepening as the sun slips lower. Ysolde watches it without speaking until the color has settled over the nearest bank.

“It never looks the same twice,” she says.
