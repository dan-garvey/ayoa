The common room has settled into the softer part of evening. The fire has burned down to a red, steady bed beneath the portraits, and most of the study tables are empty. Rashid sits in one of the leather chairs with his jacket unbuttoned, a second sheet of paper balanced on the armrest beside him. At your words, he draws the petition from his pocket and hands it over.

“Read all of it,” he says. “I would rather have a refusal based on the words than a name added because I asked.”

The page is headed in a clear, compact hand:

*Petition for Limited Supervised Access to the Covered Court*

*We request that the current full closure be replaced, pending review, by two supervised evening practice periods each week. Those periods would use inspected equipment, follow the posted safety limits, and exclude ranked or unsupervised sparring.*

*We further request a short written account of the incident's relevant safety findings, without naming the injured student; a statement of what supervision the Academy considers necessary; and a date and criteria for reconsidering full access.*

*No signatory contends that the injury was trivial. We contend that a precaution meant to prevent another injury should not eliminate the only regular supervised practice available to students without private instruction or court time.*

Below that, the signatures begin with a handful of unfamiliar names and leave room for more. There is no account of the injury beyond what Rashid said at dinner, no guess at fault, and no attempt to make the injured student into an argument.

“I can ask them for access, a supervisor, and an explanation of the rule,” Rashid says when you have had the page a moment. “I cannot honestly claim I know why the accident happened. If the report says the court itself is unsafe, we will need a different request.” His eyes settle on the wording rather than on you. “What would you change before you put your name anywhere near it?”
