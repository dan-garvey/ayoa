The storm below the Nexus reaches your window as light first. A white vein opens beneath the gardens and closes before any sound can follow; for an instant it finds every darker stitch in your charcoal coat and turns the Garvey signet bright against your hand. The mending is neat, patient work, the sort meant to pass for new from a little distance. The ring makes no such effort.

Garvey House has made the room suitable for an heir with the impersonal thoroughness of a hotel preparing for an important guest. The bed is squarely made. A row of unfamiliar clothes sent ahead by lawyers hangs in the wardrobe, their sleeves still holding the flatness of travel. The desk faces the window and carries an Academy primer in blue buckram, a blotter, and a brass lamp whose globe has already come awake with dusk.

Above it hangs the framed letter.

It is addressed to another Garvey, a named great-nephew expected for a visit that apparently never happened. Its cramped, slanting hand concerns itself with ordinary matters: whether the guest room will be warm enough, whether someone ought to speak to the gardener about the loose pale stones by the rose wall. The writer has taken trouble over the details. The letter has survived its intended occasion with the stubbornness of paper and a good frame.

The primer's gilt title catches the lamplight: *A First-Year's Guide to the Covenant Academy*. Its account of the Covenant is dry enough to make three centuries of bargains sound inevitable. Article Nineteen receives a page of its own, printed in the same calm type as the rules about bridge permissions and library hours. A prohibition made of executions does not become less severe because the margins are tidy.

Outside, the garden paths lie pale between sheltering walls. Their flowers keep improbable color in the stormlight: blue bells under a clipped yew, a spill of orange bloom near an arch, white petals moving almost imperceptibly where the wind cannot reach them. Beyond the garden's far wall, one of the hard-light bridges rises into cloud. Lightning glides along its length without touching it.

The house is less still below. China meets china in the dining hall, followed by the soft, offended scrape of a chair being corrected against the floor. Someone laughs once, briefly. Then a man's voice carries up through the common room, controlled enough that it may be trying not to carry at all.

“It is a blanket restriction.”

“It is temporary,” another voice replies.

“Temporary things still happen to people,” says the first.

For a moment there is only the whisper of a server's skirts and the low crackle of the oversized fireplace. Then a woman's voice enters, dry and level.

“If either of you intends to improve the soup by arguing with it, begin before it is served.”

That earns a quieter laugh, this one from more than one person. The disagreement does not vanish, but it changes shape around the interruption. Somewhere downstairs, glass is set carefully on the long table.

Your room opens onto the second-floor hall. From there, the stair curves past the portraits in the common room and down toward the dining hall, where candlelight has begun to gather in the paneling. The welcome dinner waits below with its places laid and its inhabitants already forming opinions in one another's hearing. Behind you, the lamp keeps its small circle of warmth around the primer, the letter, and the desk.
