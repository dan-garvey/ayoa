## Submission 0

Begin the story shortly before the shared supper, in my room at the Breakwater Picture House. I play Rowan Garvey.

## Story

Your room is small enough that the harbour seems close through its single window. The last of the light has found the water beyond Harbour Street and turned it a flat, pewter colour. Below, the front of the Picture House has begun to glow behind its glass doors.

Your luggage waits beside the bed. The desk lamp makes a neat yellow pool over the welcome sheet: supper, 18:30; staff staircase; Nell Ward’s number; induction, 09:00 tomorrow. Beyond the door, the landing is quiet except for a faint, patient ticking from somewhere near the projection booth.

Downstairs, crockery gathers in small clinks and stacks. A chair scrapes across the foyer floor. The smell reaching the stairwell is warm with tomato, baked onion, and something sharper beneath it—mustard, perhaps, or cheese browning at the edge of a pie.

A knock comes at the door.

“Rowan? It’s Nell.”

Her voice carries the briskness of someone who has been moving between rooms all day and has not yet stopped long enough to notice it.

“I realized I gave you keys and a timetable and called that a welcome. Supper’s nearly ready. Jo’s fed half the town at one point or another, so you’re in capable hands.” A brief pause. “And archive work starts tomorrow. You are not being quietly recruited to unpack crockery tonight.”

The projection-booth door opens farther along the landing. A man’s voice says, “Nell—sorry. Before you go down. Have we got the auditorium free after seven?”

Nell’s shoes shift on the boards. “No. Rosa and Martin have it from seven until twenty to eight. It’s in the diary.”

There is a moment of silence, then the man says, “Right. I’d hoped to test the hearing receivers while Hana could help. She’s free till half seven.”

“Can it wait?”

“It can. I was trying to save myself a trip next week.”

“That I understand,” Nell says. “But Rosa’s booked it, and Martin’s bringing the boards down specially. Don’t ask Hana to come over for a test that can’t happen.”

“No. Fair enough.” A phone screen lights the landing for an instant as the man looks down at it. “I’ll tell her not to set off.”

“Thank you.”

Nell’s tone softens when she turns back toward your door. “That’s Dev. He’s making the projector behave before anyone trusts it with paying customers. The foyer’s through the stair door on your left whenever you’re ready.”

Her footsteps move away down the stairs. A few seconds later, another voice rises from below—low, amused, impossible to make out—and someone answers with a laugh that makes the crockery rattle.

The smell of supper thickens in the stairwell.

## Submission 1

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

The foyer is warmer than the stairwell, bright with the cafe lights and the last blue of evening held behind the glazed front doors. The tables have been pushed together beneath a scatter of folded opening programmes. An empty chair waits between Bea Finch, who has a square blue cardigan over one shoulder, and Rosa Chen, whose camera bag rests under her feet.

Jo appears from the kitchen carrying a dish wrapped in a tea towel. “There you are. Sit down before Nell decides you’ve arrived early enough to carry boxes.”

“Too late,” Nell says from the far end of the table. “I was only waiting for the right moment.”

“See?” Jo sets down the cheese-and-onion pie. “Sit.”

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

For a moment there is the small, practical bustle of people making room: a chair leg lifted clear of someone’s shoe, a water jug moved, bread passed along the table.

“I’m Nell,” says the woman at the end, though her name is already on the welcome sheet upstairs. Her dark hair is pinned up with a pencil, and an office keyring lies beside her plate. “Manager, occasional carrier of boxes, frequent recipient of other people’s keys.”

“Bea.” The woman beside you gives a quick nod. “Treasurer. I promise not to ask you to explain your receipts at dinner.”

“Martin.” Across the table, a broad-shouldered man with silver hair has one hand around a glass of water. “I fix things slowly enough that nobody can accuse me of rushing.”

Rosa lifts two fingers from beside her plate. “I’m Rosa. I’m doing the exhibition, which means Martin and I get to disagree about dates and lettering in a civilised manner.”

“Mostly civilised,” Martin says.

Owen comes through from the kitchen with the stew in both hands. He has flour on one sleeve and sets the dish down with the care of somebody accustomed to fitting awkward things through narrow spaces. “Owen. I’ve been promoted to carrying hot objects because I was nearest the door.”

“That’s how careers start,” says a man arriving from the stairs with a compact black equipment case tucked under his arm. He puts it beside his chair and takes the last place at the table. “Dev. Sorry. Booth held me up.”

Jo serves the stew first, deep red with chickpeas and soft pieces of pepper, then points the spoon at the bread. “Help yourselves. There’s salad if you want to pretend we’ve made an effort.”

The pie breaks under the knife with a crisp crackle. Plates begin to fill. For the first few minutes, the talk flows around the table with the serving dishes.

Martin has just reached for a second piece of bread when Rosa says, “Don’t take that much. We’ve got to move six boards after we eat, and I need you upright enough to remember who painted them.”

“I remember perfectly well.”

“You said the *Jaws* board was from 1984.”

“It was.”

“It was 1983.”

Martin considers this with solemn offence. “The lettering was 1984.”

“That is not how dates work,” Bea says.

“It’s how repainting works,” Martin replies, then turns the bread in his hand. “The original title was too wide. We altered it for the reissue. Alan did the shark. I did the letters.”

Rosa’s expression changes at once from teasing to attentive. “Alan Prentice?”

“Alan Prentice. He’d hate that anyone called it a shark. Said it was an animal with a marketing problem.”

Bea smiles into her stew. “That does sound like Alan.”

Nell reaches for the salad bowl. “Rosa and Martin have the auditorium from seven, so they’ll be away before anyone can persuade them into a washing-up rota.”

“Excellent,” says Martin.

“Coward,” Jo says.

Dev looks briefly toward the auditorium doors, then back to his plate. “You’ve got it booked, so I’ve pushed my test. Hana was only free for a short window, but there’ll be another evening.”

Rosa shakes her head. “You didn’t know.”

“No harm done,” Nell says. “We have a diary for a reason.”

The conversation moves on before it can settle into apology. Jo asks Bea whether the boatyard accounts are as grim as she claims; Bea says payroll is only grim when people decide the numbers have moral opinions. Owen laughs quietly at that, then asks whether the pie needs more salt. Jo tells him he has spent enough of the afternoon trying to improve things that were already working.

Outside, a gull crosses the strip of darkening window above the front doors. The building gives a low settling creak around the table, and the smell of hot food softens the new paint and dust of the foyer.

Rosa wipes a thumb clean of pastry and glances toward her camera bag. “After this, Martin, you can show me which boards are the ones you’ve been talking up.”

## Submission 2

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

The serving dishes make their way around the joined tables until there is only a shallow red sheen of stew in the bottom of the bowl and a ragged wedge of pie left beneath its tea towel. You pass bread, salad, and the water jug as they come within reach. Around you, the conversation changes course without waiting for anyone to steer it.

Jo is the one who brings it back to work.

“The opening menu is staying short,” she says, cutting herself a smaller piece of pie. “I’m checking what everything actually costs tonight, and then I’ll have it printed. Nobody gets to invent six more items because they had a good idea while standing in the foyer.”

Nell holds up both hands. “I accept the ruling.”

“Do you?” Jo says.

“I do. Publicly.”

Martin reaches into the pocket of his work trousers and produces a striped boiled sweet. “Could you put pickled walnuts on it?”

“No.”

“They’d keep.”

“They’d clear the auditorium.”

Bea takes one of the sweets from his palm before he can close his hand around the packet. “He’s trying to make the cafe fail before it opens.”

“I’m trying to give it character.”

“It has character,” Jo says. “It has soup, pie, decent coffee, and nobody making you eat in a car park.”

Owen leans back enough to look through the foyer toward the front doors. The last light has gone from Harbour Street, leaving the glass dark except for the reflected cafe lamps. “That ought to be on the sign.”

“Too many words,” Rosa says. “Also, the sign’s already been restored.”

“It was a joke.”

“I know. I’m still right.”

Martin breaks his sweet with a sharp little crack between his teeth. “The old sign had fewer words. That was its strength.”

“The old sign also lost the letter R every winter,” Bea says.

“Only in wet weather.”

“Lowhaven has wet weather.”

For a few minutes the talk settles into the easy arguments of people who have heard one another’s versions before. Nell tells Jo that the ticket desk has finally received its card terminal. Jo says that is good news for Nell and neutral news for anyone who has ever taken cash. Dev, with one elbow resting near the black equipment case, explains that the new hearing receivers are smaller than the previous model but have twice as many ways to be put in the wrong cupboard.

“Label the cupboard,” Owen says.

“I will.”

“Label the labels.”

Dev looks at him. “That is exactly the sort of advice which makes a person seem useful and sinister at once.”

Owen’s mouth pulls briefly to one side. “I’ll take useful.”

Rosa finishes the last of her stew and wipes her fingers on her napkin. “Martin, before we go in: the *Starlight* board. The one with the blue border. Did you paint the border, or did somebody else?”

“June did the border,” Martin says at once. “I did the title after they changed the billing. She’d used up the good blue, so I had to mix it.”

“That explains it.” Rosa reaches down for her camera bag. “It’s two different blues.”

“It’s one blue after ten years of sun and three different cleaners.”

“Still two blues.”

Bea glances at the clock above the counter. “Quarter to.”

Martin sets his sweet packet back in his pocket and pushes his chair in. “All right. We’d better get our evidence before Rosa decides every mistake was mine.”

“I only decide things after I’ve photographed them.”

The two of them rise from the table together. Rosa slips the camera strap over her shoulder; Martin pauses by the auditorium doors to take a folded cloth from the pile of programmes, then follows her through. The doors swing shut behind them with a soft double thud.

Dev gathers his equipment case from beside his chair. “I’ll go back up before I start imagining I’ve done a full evening’s work.”

“Don’t turn the volume up just because you’re annoyed with it,” Nell says.

“I never do that.”

“You absolutely do.”

He gives her a look that does not quite deny it, then heads for the staff stairs.

The foyer feels larger with three chairs empty. Jo begins collecting plates into a careful stack, and Bea pours the last of the water into her glass. Beyond the auditorium doors, there is the faint scrape of a board being moved into position, followed by Rosa’s voice, too muffled to make out.
