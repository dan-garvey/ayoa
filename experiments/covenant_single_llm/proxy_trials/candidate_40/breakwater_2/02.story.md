The serving dishes make their way around the joined tables until there is only a shallow red sheen of stew in the bottom of the bowl and a ragged wedge of pie left beneath its tea towel. You pass bread, salad, and the water jug as they come within reach. Around you, the conversation changes course without waiting for anyone to steer it.

Jo is the one who brings it back to work.

“The opening menu is staying short,” she says, cutting herself a smaller piece of pie. “I’m checking what everything actually costs tonight, and then I’ll have it printed. Nobody gets to invent six more items because they had a good idea while standing in the foyer.”

Nell holds up both hands. “I accept the ruling.”

“Do you?” Jo says.

“I do. Publicly.”

Martin reaches into the pocket of his work trousers and produces a striped boiled sweet. “Could you put pickled walnuts on it?”

“No.”

“They’d keep.”

“They’d clear the auditorium.”

Bea takes one of the sweets from his palm before he can close his hand around the packet. “He’s trying to make the cafe fail before it opens.”

“I’m trying to give it character.”

“It has character,” Jo says. “It has soup, pie, decent coffee, and nobody making you eat in a car park.”

Owen leans back enough to look through the foyer toward the front doors. The last light has gone from Harbour Street, leaving the glass dark except for the reflected cafe lamps. “That ought to be on the sign.”

“Too many words,” Rosa says. “Also, the sign’s already been restored.”

“It was a joke.”

“I know. I’m still right.”

Martin breaks his sweet with a sharp little crack between his teeth. “The old sign had fewer words. That was its strength.”

“The old sign also lost the letter R every winter,” Bea says.

“Only in wet weather.”

“Lowhaven has wet weather.”

For a few minutes the talk settles into the easy arguments of people who have heard one another’s versions before. Nell tells Jo that the ticket desk has finally received its card terminal. Jo says that is good news for Nell and neutral news for anyone who has ever taken cash. Dev, with one elbow resting near the black equipment case, explains that the new hearing receivers are smaller than the previous model but have twice as many ways to be put in the wrong cupboard.

“Label the cupboard,” Owen says.

“I will.”

“Label the labels.”

Dev looks at him. “That is exactly the sort of advice which makes a person seem useful and sinister at once.”

Owen’s mouth pulls briefly to one side. “I’ll take useful.”

Rosa finishes the last of her stew and wipes her fingers on her napkin. “Martin, before we go in: the *Starlight* board. The one with the blue border. Did you paint the border, or did somebody else?”

“June did the border,” Martin says at once. “I did the title after they changed the billing. She’d used up the good blue, so I had to mix it.”

“That explains it.” Rosa reaches down for her camera bag. “It’s two different blues.”

“It’s one blue after ten years of sun and three different cleaners.”

“Still two blues.”

Bea glances at the clock above the counter. “Quarter to.”

Martin sets his sweet packet back in his pocket and pushes his chair in. “All right. We’d better get our evidence before Rosa decides every mistake was mine.”

“I only decide things after I’ve photographed them.”

The two of them rise from the table together. Rosa slips the camera strap over her shoulder; Martin pauses by the auditorium doors to take a folded cloth from the pile of programmes, then follows her through. The doors swing shut behind them with a soft double thud.

Dev gathers his equipment case from beside his chair. “I’ll go back up before I start imagining I’ve done a full evening’s work.”

“Don’t turn the volume up just because you’re annoyed with it,” Nell says.

“I never do that.”

“You absolutely do.”

He gives her a look that does not quite deny it, then heads for the staff stairs.

The foyer feels larger with three chairs empty. Jo begins collecting plates into a careful stack, and Bea pours the last of the water into her glass. Beyond the auditorium doors, there is the faint scrape of a board being moved into position, followed by Rosa’s voice, too muffled to make out.
