The foyer is warmer than the stairwell, bright with the cafe lights and the last blue of evening held behind the glazed front doors. The tables have been pushed together beneath a scatter of folded opening programmes. An empty chair waits between Bea Finch, who has a square blue cardigan over one shoulder, and Rosa Chen, whose camera bag rests under her feet.

Jo appears from the kitchen carrying a dish wrapped in a tea towel. “There you are. Sit down before Nell decides you’ve arrived early enough to carry boxes.”

“Too late,” Nell says from the far end of the table. “I was only waiting for the right moment.”

“See?” Jo sets down the cheese-and-onion pie. “Sit.”

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

For a moment there is the small, practical bustle of people making room: a chair leg lifted clear of someone’s shoe, a water jug moved, bread passed along the table.

“I’m Nell,” says the woman at the end, though her name is already on the welcome sheet upstairs. Her dark hair is pinned up with a pencil, and an office keyring lies beside her plate. “Manager, occasional carrier of boxes, frequent recipient of other people’s keys.”

“Bea.” The woman beside you gives a quick nod. “Treasurer. I promise not to ask you to explain your receipts at dinner.”

“Martin.” Across the table, a broad-shouldered man with silver hair has one hand around a glass of water. “I fix things slowly enough that nobody can accuse me of rushing.”

Rosa lifts two fingers from beside her plate. “I’m Rosa. I’m doing the exhibition, which means Martin and I get to disagree about dates and lettering in a civilised manner.”

“Mostly civilised,” Martin says.

Owen comes through from the kitchen with the stew in both hands. He has flour on one sleeve and sets the dish down with the care of somebody accustomed to fitting awkward things through narrow spaces. “Owen. I’ve been promoted to carrying hot objects because I was nearest the door.”

“That’s how careers start,” says a man arriving from the stairs with a compact black equipment case tucked under his arm. He puts it beside his chair and takes the last place at the table. “Dev. Sorry. Booth held me up.”

Jo serves the stew first, deep red with chickpeas and soft pieces of pepper, then points the spoon at the bread. “Help yourselves. There’s salad if you want to pretend we’ve made an effort.”

The pie breaks under the knife with a crisp crackle. Plates begin to fill. For the first few minutes, the talk flows around the table with the serving dishes.

Martin has just reached for a second piece of bread when Rosa says, “Don’t take that much. We’ve got to move six boards after we eat, and I need you upright enough to remember who painted them.”

“I remember perfectly well.”

“You said the *Jaws* board was from 1984.”

“It was.”

“It was 1983.”

Martin considers this with solemn offence. “The lettering was 1984.”

“That is not how dates work,” Bea says.

“It’s how repainting works,” Martin replies, then turns the bread in his hand. “The original title was too wide. We altered it for the reissue. Alan did the shark. I did the letters.”

Rosa’s expression changes at once from teasing to attentive. “Alan Prentice?”

“Alan Prentice. He’d hate that anyone called it a shark. Said it was an animal with a marketing problem.”

Bea smiles into her stew. “That does sound like Alan.”

Nell reaches for the salad bowl. “Rosa and Martin have the auditorium from seven, so they’ll be away before anyone can persuade them into a washing-up rota.”

“Excellent,” says Martin.

“Coward,” Jo says.

Dev looks briefly toward the auditorium doors, then back to his plate. “You’ve got it booked, so I’ve pushed my test. Hana was only free for a short window, but there’ll be another evening.”

Rosa shakes her head. “You didn’t know.”

“No harm done,” Nell says. “We have a diary for a reason.”

The conversation moves on before it can settle into apology. Jo asks Bea whether the boatyard accounts are as grim as she claims; Bea says payroll is only grim when people decide the numbers have moral opinions. Owen laughs quietly at that, then asks whether the pie needs more salt. Jo tells him he has spent enough of the afternoon trying to improve things that were already working.

Outside, a gull crosses the strip of darkening window above the front doors. The building gives a low settling creak around the table, and the smell of hot food softens the new paint and dust of the foyer.

Rosa wipes a thumb clean of pastry and glances toward her camera bag. “After this, Martin, you can show me which boards are the ones you’ve been talking up.”
