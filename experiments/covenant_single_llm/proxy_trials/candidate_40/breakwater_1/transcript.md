## Submission 0

Begin the story shortly before the shared supper, in my room at the Breakwater Picture House. I play Rowan Garvey.

## Story

At quarter past six, the upstairs room still has the orderly air of somewhere prepared for a person rather than lived in. The bed is made tight against one wall. The wardrobe stands closed beside it, and the little desk holds its lamp, the welcome sheet, and nothing else that claims the space as yours.

Through the window, Harbour Street slopes toward the harbour in the last pale light. A gull settles on the roofline opposite, folds itself into a white knot, and begins shouting at another gull it cannot see. Downstairs, crockery meets wood in careful intervals. A pan lid lands with a bright metal click. The smell reaching the landing is tomato, onion, and something warm with cumin.

The welcome sheet gives the route to the foyer in a neat hand: down the staff staircase, past the office. Supper at half past six. Nell’s number beneath it. Tomorrow’s induction at nine.

A door opens somewhere below, and voices rise clearly through the stairwell.

“Nell? Sorry. Have you got a minute?”

There is the scrape of a chair from the office. “Dev?”

“I’ve got Hana available at seven. The hearing receivers turned up early, so I thought we could run the test while she’s here. Twenty minutes, maybe less.”

A pause follows, long enough for the kitchen tap to run downstairs.

“Rosa and Martin have the auditorium from seven until twenty to eight,” Nell says. “They booked it for the boards.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“I know.”

“Hana can only do half seven. I can test them next week, it’s just—” Dev stops. “It would save a separate call-out.”

Nell’s reply is quieter, though the stairwell carries it anyway. “We’ll talk after food. I’m not cancelling Rosa and Martin’s time while standing between floors.”

“I haven’t asked you to cancel it.”

“Good. Then nothing’s cancelled.”

Footsteps cross away toward the foyer. For a moment, there is only the muted bustle below: a chair leg dragged over the floor, somebody laughing at something too low to make out, a cupboard shut with a hip rather than a hand.

Then a woman’s voice says, “If those bowls are for the salad, they’re much too large.”

“They’re the small bowls,” another voice answers.

“That is a worryingly small distinction.”

A man laughs, more openly than the first voice had. “You should’ve seen the ones they had in the old café.”

“I did see them,” the woman says. “That’s why I’m worried.”

The building seems to gather itself around the sounds of them: people who already know which doors stick, where the spare cups have gone, how much room there is for a joke before somebody needs the next thing done. A moment later, the kitchen door opens and closes. Plates begin arriving on the tables one after another.

At half past six, footsteps take the staff stair at an unhurried pace and stop outside your room.

A soft knock comes at the door.

“Rowan?” Nell says. “It’s Nell. We’re putting the food out now. Come down whenever you’re ready.”

## Submission 1

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

The foyer is brighter than the room above, its broad front windows still holding a little of the evening. Two café tables have been pushed together beneath the unopened cartons of crockery and the folded opening programmes. Eight chairs wait around them. The food has already made the smaller space feel occupied: a deep dish of tomato and chickpea stew, a cheese-and-onion pie with its top split at one corner, a bowl of salad bright with cucumber, and a cutting board crowded with bread.

Nell looks up from the far end of the table as you come down the stairs. “There you are.”

You say, “Hello. I’m Rowan.”

“Welcome,” says the woman standing by the stew, serving spoon in hand. She has flour on one wrist and does not seem concerned by it. “I’m Jo. Take that chair before somebody puts a box of cups on it.”

The older man beside the bread rises halfway from his seat, then seems to decide the gesture is unnecessary. “Martin.”

“Bea,” says the woman opposite him, with a small lift of her glass.

The woman beside Bea gives you a quick, assessing smile. “Rosa.”

“Owen,” says the man at the other corner of the tables. He shifts a stack of side plates out of the way with one hand.

Nell nods toward the empty place beside him. “And Dev’s about somewhere, unless he’s managed to become part of the projection booth.”

“I heard that,” comes a voice from the corridor.

Dev appears a moment later, tucking his phone into his pocket. “Dev. Sorry. I was trying not to let a cable become the evening’s main event.”

“You’ve failed already,” Jo says. “Sit down.”

You settle into the available chair. The serving dishes begin their circuit without anyone directing them. Jo spoons stew into bowls, Martin cuts the pie with more enthusiasm than accuracy, and Bea takes the salad when it reaches her before passing it on. You follow the rhythm of it, taking what is offered and sending each dish onward.

For the first few minutes, the talk stays with the food.

“This is better than the trial batch,” Martin says after a mouthful.

“The trial batch was the same recipe.”

“It wasn’t made in an oven that smells of plaster dust and new wiring.”

“That’s not a culinary observation,” Jo says.

“It’s a strong one.”

Rosa breaks a piece of bread apart over her plate. “He’s got a point. Everything in here tastes faintly of paint at the moment.”

“Only because you’ve spent the last week in front of those boards,” Bea says.

“They smell of paint because they are paint.”

“They smell of history,” Martin says.

Rosa glances at him. “History can smell of paint.”

Nell has eaten only a few bites when she puts her fork down. “Before we get too comfortable, there is one thing that needs sorting before seven.”

Jo looks at her over the rim of her glass. “Nell.”

“I know. I know this wasn’t meant to be a meeting.”

“It isn’t. It’s dinner.”

“It can be both for sixty seconds.”

Dev leans back in his chair. “The hearing-assistance receivers arrived early. Hana can help me test them at seven, but I didn’t know Rosa and Martin had the auditorium booked.”

Rosa’s hand stills over her bowl. “We do.”

“For the programme boards,” Bea says. “Photographs and Martin’s recollections.”

“I know that now,” Dev says. “I’m not asking anybody to pretend the booking wasn’t there.”

“The test will make noise,” Rosa says.

“It will.”

“And people walking through the seats.”

“Yes.”

Martin wipes a crumb from the table with one finger. “We could do the photographs first.”

“We’re doing the photographs first,” Rosa says. “And then I need to record what you remember without test tones going off behind you.”

“It’s only twenty minutes,” Martin says, not offended, merely hopeful.

“It’s twenty minutes in the middle of forty.”

Nell looks from Dev to Rosa. “No one is losing their booking. Dev, you need to decide whether tonight is worth rearranging around, and Rosa, I need to know what flexibility you actually have. We can talk properly once we’ve eaten enough to think.”

Jo picks up the serving spoon again. “There. A meeting adjourned by chickpeas.”

“That should be on the trust letterhead,” Owen says.

Bea laughs into her glass. Even Rosa’s mouth gives way for a moment.

Martin taps one knuckle lightly against the table. “For what it’s worth, the blue board is definitely Lou Mercer’s work. The way the letters lean.”

“Definitely?” Rosa asks.

“Near enough.”

Bea turns to him. “Those are different words.”

“They are when you work in payroll,” Owen says.

“They are when you label an exhibition,” Rosa says. “I can put down that Martin Vale identifies it as Lou Mercer’s, and that’s useful. I just don’t want somebody fifty years from now treating your memory as a paint sample.”

Martin considers this, then nods. “Fair enough. He always did make the R’s too narrow, though.”

“That,” Rosa says, “is exactly the sort of thing I want you to tell me.”

The pie comes round again. Beyond the front doors, a pair of pedestrians pass through the remaining wash of light on Harbour Street, their shapes briefly reflected in the glass before the foyer takes them away.

Jo reaches for the breadboard, then looks across the table at you.

“So, Rowan—have you been to Lowhaven before?”

## Submission 2

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

Jo does not press the question. She takes a drink, then turns the breadboard toward Martin instead.

“We can save the census for another night,” she says.

Martin tears off a piece of crust. “Lowhaven’s still here whenever you feel like looking at it.”

“You make it sound threatening,” Bea says.

“I mean it hospitably.”

The dishes keep moving. When the pie reaches you again, you pass it on to Rosa; when the salad comes round, Owen steadies the bowl with one hand while you take it. Conversation gathers and loosens around the table without requiring anything from you.

“Those blue boards have always been in the cupboard, haven’t they?” Owen says.

“Not always,” Martin replies. “They were behind the old sweets counter for years. You could see them whenever you went looking for the ice-cream signs.”

“You went looking for ice-cream signs?”

“I went looking for a fuse box.”

“Through the sweets counter.”

“It was a complicated building.”

Rosa has a notebook beside her plate, unopened for now. “The blue one is the best of the lot, regardless. Somebody altered the title twice without repainting the whole thing. You can see the first letters through the green.”

“Lou did that,” Martin says.

“Probably Lou,” Bea corrects gently.

Martin points his fork at her. “You didn’t work the booth.”

“No, but I can read a date.”

“You make a fellow sound unreliable.”

“I’m trying to make the labels reliable.”

Dev has been watching the exchange with one thumb resting against his glass. At last he says, “For what it’s worth, I should have checked the diary before I started planning an auditorium test.”

Rosa looks across at him. “That would have helped.”

“I know.”

Nell reaches for her phone, checks the screen, and sets it facedown again. “Hana is available from seven until half past. Dev needs about twenty minutes with the receivers.”

“What does the test actually involve?” Rosa asks.

“One test track through the speakers,” Dev says. “Then Hana and I walk the receivers through the stalls and check where they drop out.”

“You need the front rows?”

“Not necessarily. Most of the trouble, if there is any, will probably be farther back.”

Rosa looks toward the auditorium doors. For a few seconds, only the scrape of cutlery and the faint traffic beyond the glass fill the foyer.

“I can photograph the boards while you do the back rows,” she says at last. “But I need quiet from twenty past for Martin’s recording. And nobody moves the boards.”

“I won’t touch them,” Dev says. “I can keep the test audio down once I know it’s coming through. We’ll be out of your way by twenty past.”

Martin nods. “I can wait twenty minutes before I tell anybody who painted an R badly.”

“You’ve been waiting thirty years,” Bea says.

“Not continuously.”

Nell looks at Rosa. “Does that give you enough time?”

“It gives me the time we agreed, with an interruption at the beginning.”

“It gives you the room,” Dev says. “The interruption is the part I’m asking for.”

Rosa studies him, then gives a small nod. “All right. Seven to twenty past, you use the back of the auditorium and leave the boards alone. At twenty past, the sound goes off.”

“It goes off,” Dev says.

Nell’s shoulders settle by a fraction. “Good. I’ll write it down properly. Rosa and Martin still have the booking. Dev and Hana have a limited test inside it.”

Jo lifts the stew dish before anyone can return to the details. “Excellent. The cinema has survived its first scheduling dispute. Somebody take this before I decide you’re all full.”

Bea accepts the dish. “There’s more bread as well.”

“There is always more bread,” Jo says.

The conversation moves on. Martin tells a story about a projector belt snapping halfway through a Saturday matinee, though Rosa keeps stopping him to ask whether the disaster happened before or after he had gone upstairs for a spanner. Owen asks whether the manager at the time really kept spare change in a biscuit tin. Bea says he did, but that the tin was not the strangest thing in his office. Nell refuses to say what was.

Outside, the light has thinned from the harbour end of the street. The foyer windows begin to reflect the table more clearly than the pavement beyond them: bowls, hands, the pie dish nearly empty, and eight people fitting themselves around the joined tables.
