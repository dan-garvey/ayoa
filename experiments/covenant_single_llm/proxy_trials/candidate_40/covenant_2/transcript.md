## Submission 0

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

## Story

The Garvey signet sits broad and old against your ink-stained hand, its worn seal catching the amber light from the desk lamp. Beside it, a framed letter leans against a stack of Academy primers. The handwriting inside is brown with age, full of apologies about a delayed visit and gardens that ought to be seen in spring. The salutation names a great-nephew who is not you.

Beyond the window, pale paths thread through the gardens below. One of the hard-light bridges hangs over the storm like a line drawn in a trembling hand. Lightning opens soundlessly beneath the plateau.

A bell sounds from the ground floor: one deep note, then silence.

Your door stands ajar. Across the landing, the stair descends toward firelight and the clink of serving dishes. A server in Academy livery passes through the common room below with a covered platter; rosemary and browned butter rise briefly through the house.

Footsteps come up the stairs at an unhurried pace.

The man who appears at the landing is tall, dark-haired, and dressed with the care of someone who has already decided where every seam ought to fall. Fine fur shows at his cuffs and along the backs of his hands. He stops outside the threshold rather than filling it.

“Rashid vel Amara,” he says. His amber eyes flick once toward the dining-room light below, then return to you. “I thought it was poor form to leave you to find the table alone. Garvey House has fewer corridors than it pretends to, but the stairs have a way of becoming ceremonial when people are waiting.”

From downstairs, a woman says, “If you make an entrance sound dangerous, Amara, he may decide to stay upstairs.”

Rashid’s mouth shifts at one corner. “Ashara vel Kothren.”

“I heard that.”

“You were meant to.”

Another voice drifts up, cool and unhurried. “The fish will not improve while we identify ourselves from different floors.”

“That is Ysolde Thornmantle,” Rashid says. “She has inspected dinner.”

“I have not inspected it,” Ysolde replies. “I can smell it.”

A light, exasperated sound follows. “Which is somehow worse,” says a woman with a voice made precise by effort. “Caelindra Vaeyn.”

Then another voice, warm and clear, speaks from the dining hall in short lines.

“Bring him bread before heraldry.  
The order seems kind.”

“Seraphel Dawnquill,” Rashid says. “And she is right.”

“I am right about bread,” Seraphel answers. “The heraldry remains untested.”

A chair scrapes faintly against the floorboards. “I have no objection to bread,” says a man, measured and resonant. “Though I do think a welcome merits some dignity.”

“That is Aldric Verantus,” Rashid tells you.

“And Thessaly Morrow,” says a final voice, dry and close enough to the table to suggest she has not bothered raising it. “I have no objection to dignity either. I simply do not know what it tastes like.”

The dining hall answers with a brief, uneven collection of laughter. Even Ashara’s is there, low and quick.

Rashid steps aside from the doorway. The stairwell lies open beyond him, warm with firelight, and the conversation below has already begun moving on without deciding what sort of evening it will be.

## Submission 1

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

Rashid turns toward the stairs, and the common room opens beneath you in firelight and old varnished wood. Portraits watch from the walls above the oversized hearth; their gilt nameplates catch the glow, then vanish again as the flames move. Beyond the open dining-room doors, servers set the last covered dishes along a table large enough to make eight people seem provisional.

A place card marked **GARVEY** waits midway down the right side. Rashid takes the chair on one side of it. On the other sits the snow-pale woman from below, white hair gathered at her nape and grey eyes fixed on the platter nearest her.

When you reach the table, the conversation gives you a space.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

“Ashara vel Kothren,” says the woman at the far end. Her swept horns clear the high back of her chair by an inch. “Welcome.”

“Ysolde Thornmantle,” says the woman beside you. “The fish has survived your arrival.”

Across from her, Caelindra inclines her silver head with careful economy. “Caelindra Vaeyn.”

“Seraphel Dawnquill,” says the angel beside her.

“Sit. Eat.  
The names will keep.”

Thessaly Morrow’s black eyes settle on you for a beat, then on the basket between you. “She is right. The bread is getting cold.”

Aldric straightens slightly in his white and gold. “Aldric Verantus. Welcome to the Conclave, Rowan Garvey.”

Rashid reaches for the nearest serving spoon. “And you’ve met everyone who intends to be civil before supper. It’s a better beginning than most first nights get.”

Ashara lifts one dark brow. “Most first nights?”

“Most things,” Rashid says.

The dishes begin their slow circuit around the table. There is a shallow bowl of smoked river fish under a crust of herbs, glazed roots bright with pepper, a green bean dish swimming in butter, and a copper pot of broth fragrant with fennel. You follow the movement of hands and spoons, taking portions as the others do. Rashid passes the bread without letting the basket catch on the curve of Ashara’s tail.

“I could have managed that,” she says.

“I know.”

“Then why did you move it?”

“Because I could manage it too.”

Caelindra cuts into a piece of fish. “A moving demonstration of shared competence. We are fortunate to witness it.”

Ysolde tastes the broth, pauses, and reaches for the salt.

“It needs it,” she says.

Aldric looks toward the server standing discreetly by the wall. “Could we have a little more?”

“We have some,” Ysolde says, already passing the cellar.

Thessaly studies the small silver vessel as though it has been introduced under suspicious terms. “Is there a custom concerning how much salt one may take?”

“No,” says Ashara.

“Yes,” says Caelindra at the same moment. “One takes enough not to ruin the dish.”

“That is advice, not a custom.”

“It becomes a custom when enough people survive it.”

Seraphel breaks a corner from her bread.

“Salt, bread, and fish.  
A treaty has begun.”

A short laugh escapes Rashid. Even Aldric’s expression gives way for a moment.

The first minutes settle into the small sounds of dinner: knife against plate, the low hiss from a lamp wick, a server replacing an empty bowl before anyone asks. The storm’s light comes and goes through the tall windows, whitening the paneled walls without reaching the candles.

Ashara eats with the concentration she might bring to a bout. Rashid, after finishing his first helping, asks the server whether the kitchens can spare the recipe for the pepper glaze and receives a flat refusal that appears to please him. Caelindra makes an annotation in the margin of a slim book propped beside her plate, then closes it when Ysolde looks at the ink.

“You cannot possibly be reading through dinner,” Ysolde says.

“I can read at any reasonable hour.”

“This is not an hour. It is a meal.”

“That is a distinction humans make because their hours are so short,” Caelindra says, then glances across the table as if the sentence has arrived somewhere unintended. “No offense intended.”

Thessaly takes another piece of bread. “I cannot tell whether that is better or worse than offense.”

“It is worse,” Ashara says.

“It is more honest,” Rashid adds.

Aldric folds his napkin with exactness. “It is also an unfortunate way to welcome someone.”

Caelindra’s ears move sharply, then still. “Then I withdraw the phrasing. Not the observation.”

“Fair,” Rashid says.

For a few moments, no one presses it further.

Ashara sets down her fork. “Amara. The octagon after dinner.”

“I remember.”

“Half an hour.”

“At least.”

“Tomorrow’s ranking does not care whether you have been charming at supper.”

Rashid’s courtesy becomes almost formal. “I have no intention of losing because I ate well.”

“Good,” Ashara says, and returns to her plate.

At the mention of tomorrow’s ranking, Aldric’s attention goes briefly to the posted schedule tucked beneath the sideboard clock. “The Chancellor will have the full first-year Conclave in seminar before then. I imagine he will make something of it.”

“He makes something of most arrangements involving witnesses,” Thessaly says.

“He is welcoming a restored signatory presence,” Aldric replies.

“The human seat has been empty,” Rashid says. His tone remains even. “That is different from restoring what was there.”

Ashara looks toward the dark crest on your hand, then up at you. Her gold eyes do not leave room for a polite evasion.

“Did you choose this, Garvey?”

## Submission 2

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

For a moment, the only answer to Ashara’s question is the soft scrape of cutlery and the low flicker of the lamps.

You keep eating. When Rashid passes the bread basket, you take it and send it on to Ysolde; when the bowl of glazed roots reaches your place, you steady it for Thessaly while she serves herself.

Ashara does not repeat herself. Her tail shifts once beneath the table, then stills.

Aldric sets down his glass. “We needn’t turn the first meal into an examination.”

“I asked one question,” Ashara says.

“And received no answer,” Rashid replies. “Let it remain that way for now.”

Thessaly considers this. “Can a question remain somewhere? I thought it simply became unanswered.”

Seraphel folds her hands beside her plate.

“Then let it be unanswered  
without becoming a hunt.”

Caelindra’s attention stays on the fish as she says, “A surprisingly sensible proposal.”

“I make them occasionally,” Seraphel says.

“I did not say it was common.”

Ysolde reaches for the pepper glaze. “The fish improves with this.”

“The fish improves because the kitchen has prepared it properly,” Ashara says.

“It improves further.”

Rashid takes the sauce when Ysolde passes it. “A convincing argument. Perhaps you should join the rhetoric faculty.”

“I do not want to teach people to speak longer than necessary.”

“That rules you out,” Caelindra says. “The faculty appear to regard brevity as a personal insult.”

Aldric’s mouth tightens around the beginning of a smile. “Professor Shaan is concise.”

“Professor Shaan is armed,” Ashara says.

“She has never struck a student.”

“Not physically.”

The conversation moves on before anyone decides whether that counts as a defense. A server removes the first empty platters and replaces them with a broad dish of roasted squash, browned at the edges and scattered with nuts. Steam rises through the room’s lamplight. Beyond the tall windows, lightning turns the gardens white for an instant; the hard-light bridge beyond them remains a thin, steady line above the storm.

Rashid reaches for the serving spoon again. “Ashara’s ranked bout is tomorrow afternoon. She has spent the day pretending she does not care.”

Ashara looks at him across the table. “I have spent the day preparing.”

“Those are not opposites.”

“They are when you’re losing.”

“I have never lost to you.”

“You tied twice.”

“Which is not losing.”

Ysolde takes another portion of squash. “You are both very interested in a distinction with no practical result.”

“It has a practical result,” Ashara says. “He remains insufferable.”

Rashid gives her a small, formal inclination of his head. “An honor to be understood.”

Thessaly turns to Aldric. “Do you duel?”

“Not competitively.”

“He carries bandages,” Rashid says. “Which has saved more pride than most people realize.”

Aldric looks faintly annoyed at the praise. “I carry them because the infirmary is across the quadrangle, and people regularly decide that a split brow is less urgent than winning an argument.”

“Is it?” Thessaly asks.

“No.”

“Then you have answered that efficiently.”

Seraphel lifts her cup.

“Bread, bandages, salt.  
Civilization continues.”

This time the laughter reaches nearly the whole table. Caelindra tries to suppress hers behind her glass and fails when Ashara mutters, “A thin civilization.”

The quarter hour gathers itself out of small exchanges: the passing of dishes, the server’s quiet refilling of water glasses, Rashid’s account of a Military-track student who challenged a practice dummy and lost an argument with its counterweight. Caelindra objects to the story’s chronology twice, then admits the ending is good. Ysolde says the dummy was poorly designed if it could not distinguish a foolish strike from a dangerous one.

When the sideboard clock gives its restrained quarter chime, Rashid glances toward it.

Ashara catches the movement at once. “The octagon after dinner.”

“I remember.”

“Half an hour.”

“At least half an hour.”

“Good.”

Rashid’s fingers rest briefly against the stem of his glass. “You make every plan sound like a threat.”

“Only the plans you try to escape.”

“I am sitting directly across from you.”

“For now.”

The server returns with a shallow tart of dark berries and honey, already cut into narrow slices. As the plates begin another circuit, the house beyond the dining-room doors carries on around the table: fire settling in the common room, footsteps on the upper landing, the distant hush of the storm below the Nexus.
