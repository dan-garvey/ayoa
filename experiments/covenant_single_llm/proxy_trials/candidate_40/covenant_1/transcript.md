## Submission 0

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

## Story

The framed letter on your desk is addressed to a great-nephew whose name is not yours. Its brown ink has faded beneath glass, but the apology remains legible: a planned visit, a room prepared, a regret that he never came. Someone has placed it beside the Academy primer as though both belong among your effects.

The primer lies open to Article Nineteen. Its printed language is brisk enough to make the rule seem almost administrative: procreation between signatory races is forbidden; the penalties are final. A blue ribbon marks the next page, where tonight’s dinner is called a welcome rather than an introduction.

Beyond the window, the gardens are pale beneath the last of the evening light. Farther out, a bridge of hard light disappears into the storm that circles the Nexus below. Lightning moves inside the clouds without sound. The glass gives a faint tremor against its frame.

From downstairs comes the warmer business of the house: a door closing, the scrape of chairs on old wood, a burst of laughter quickly muffled by the dining hall’s high ceiling. Someone has put lamps on. Their glow reaches the second-floor landing in a thin gold band beneath your door.

A knock sounds—three light taps.

“Rowan Garvey,” says a woman on the other side. Her voice has the measured shape of verse without the ornament of a performance. “The table is filling. / I am Seraphel Dawnquill.”

There is a small pause, then the soft rustle of feathers.

“The stairs turn twice. / The common room opens below them. / If you follow the lamps, / you will find us.”

Downstairs, a woman’s voice carries clearly through the floorboards.

“If rank is to decide the chairs,” she says, clipped and dry, “we may as well replace them with pedestals.”

“That would at least be honest,” a man answers.

Another voice, cooler and slower, says, “Pedestals are impractical. They provide no support for the back.”

The laughter that follows is brief but real.

Seraphel remains outside the door.

“You needn’t let them arrange you,” she says, “before you arrive.”

## Submission 1

I go downstairs for dinner. At the table I introduce myself: "Hello. I'm Rowan."

I settle into an available place and follow the others' lead with the serving dishes. For the first few minutes I eat and listen.

## Story

Seraphel walks beside you down the narrow stair, her small wings held close enough not to brush the banister. At the first turn, Garvey House opens around you: the common room below with its fire banked low, leather chairs drawn toward one another, and portraits whose painted Garveys have watched generations of dinners from their gilt frames. Light and the smell of pepper, roast onions, and fresh bread lead through the open doors beyond.

The dining hall’s long table is set for seventeen, though only seven places are occupied. Academy servers in dark livery move quietly between candles and working lamps. A vacant chair waits halfway down the table, between Seraphel and Thessaly, with Rashid opposite.

Conversation stops as you enter.

“Hello. I’m Rowan.”

The horned woman at the near end of the table inclines her head. Her black hair is drawn severely back from swept red-black horns; steam curls above the bowl before her without seeming to trouble her.

“Ashara vel Kothren,” she says. “Welcome. Take the stew before the pepper settles.”

“Rashid vel Amara.” The beastkin man across from your place has already reached for the bread basket. Fine dark fur catches the lamplight along his fingers. “The bread is useful with the stew, if that helps.”

“Ysolde Thornmantle.” Across the table, the white-haired woman says it with the plainness of a fact. A plate of thin red meat rests beside a glass filmed with cold.

Caelindra offers a measured nod from farther down. Silver hair falls over one shoulder of her dark green dinner coat. “Caelindra Vaeyn. The potatoes are safer than the stew, though I refuse to say they are better.”

Thessaly’s entirely black eyes turn toward the serving dishes. “Thessaly Morrow. The cheese is allegedly free.”

“The cheese is free,” Seraphel says, taking her place beside you. “The bread, at least, / has asked nothing yet.”

Aldric rises just enough to make the courtesy visible. White and gold catch the candlelight at his cuffs. “Aldric Verantus. Welcome to the Conclave, Rowan.”

The place set for you has a shallow bowl, a heavy silver spoon, and a folded napkin marked with the Garvey crest. Following the traffic of hands around the table, you take bread, roasted potatoes, and a spoonful of the dark red stew. Rashid passes the salt without looking away from Ashara.

“It needs none,” she says.

“It may need mercy.”

“It has pepper.”

“That was the distinction.”

Ashara’s mouth shifts briefly before she returns to her food.

For the first few minutes, the conversation does not demand anything of you. Caelindra complains about tomorrow’s early Covenant Law seminar with such precise irritation that even Aldric lets her finish.

“Eight in the morning is not an academic hour,” she says. “It is a test of whether the Academy can make its students resemble poorly watered houseplants.”

“Professor Windwhisper gave us notice,” Aldric says.

“Advance notice is not a defense of dawn,” Thessaly replies.

“It is a defense in some contracts,” Rashid says.

“Those are bad contracts.”

Ysolde, who has been watching the dark window beyond the candles, speaks without joining the argument. “The southern edge of the storm has moved. The red bridge will close before midnight.”

Aldric looks toward the glass. “Have the wardens been told?”

“I told them last week that the outer light was thinning.”

Seraphel’s fingers rest around her cup.

“The storm keeps no calendar,” she says. “It arrives / when it has decided.”

“That makes it sound like a dean,” Rashid says.

This time the laughter lasts longer. Even Caelindra smiles into her wine.

Ashara sets down her spoon and glances at Rashid. “We still have our half-hour after dinner.”

“We do,” he says. “At the octagon.”

“Good.”

The exchange is ordinary in its brevity, but it leaves the shape of a plan on the evening.

Rashid breaks off a piece of bread, then looks across the table at you with careful courtesy.

“Does the primer tell you anything useful about us, or only where not to put your fork?”

## Submission 2

I keep eating and listening for another quarter hour. I help pass the food and leave the conversation to the others.

## Story

The question rests for a moment among the candles and serving dishes. Then a server arrives with a platter of glazed carrots, and the table’s attention moves with it.

You take the platter when it reaches you and pass it along. Seraphel accepts it with a quiet murmur of thanks and offers it to Ysolde, who takes two pieces with her fingers and considers them as if they require assessment. Rashid does not repeat his question.

“The fork is rarely the difficulty,” he says instead, tearing bread in half.

“It is when someone is holding it badly,” Ashara replies.

Aldric’s expression tightens by a degree. “There are customs worth learning.”

“There are,” Caelindra says. “The question is whether anyone here agrees upon them.”

Thessaly takes a carrot. “Agreement is more expensive than carrots.”

“That explains the portions,” Rashid says.

Seraphel’s smile appears and goes.

“The kitchen has prepared / for peace among us,” she says. “It has made enough / for a small army.”

Ashara reaches for the stew again. “An army would complain about the bread.”

“It would be correct,” Ysolde says.

The conversation turns, as conversations do when no one insists on holding it in place. Caelindra has received tomorrow’s Covenant Law reading list already and objects to its length with increasingly elegant precision. Aldric says Professor Windwhisper’s selections are foundational. Rashid points out that a foundation is not improved by requiring students to carry every stone at once.

“She assigned the petitions from Marrowgate,” he says. “Three hundred pages of officials agreeing that the beastkin trade levy was temporary.”

“It was temporary,” Aldric says. “It was revised twice.”

“Over eighty years.”

“The Council has to balance—”

“The Council has had eighty years to balance a scale it built crooked.”

Ashara’s spoon pauses above her bowl. “And the demon tariffs were revised in forty. I congratulate you on the speed.”

Caelindra leans back in her chair, one violet eye narrowing slightly. “Perhaps the Academy should issue us all ledgers instead of histories. They seem more honest.”

“Ledgers omit the reasons,” Aldric says.

“Sometimes,” Thessaly says, “that is the reason people like them.”

No one raises their voice. The room has the careful, familiar tension of students who have argued these things before and will argue them again, even when the subject began as assigned reading. A server refills the water glasses. Ysolde’s glass frosts at the rim beneath her hand.

After a little while, Rashid glances toward Ashara.

“You still intend to spend our half-hour correcting my footwork.”

“I intend to spend it making you stop calling it correction when I win.”

“You rarely win by enough to make that distinction useful.”

“Tomorrow will be educational.”

“The octagon has no chairs,” Ysolde says.

“It has a railing,” Caelindra replies.

“A railing is not a chair.”

“It permits leaning.”

Thessaly considers this. “Then it is an audience arrangement without a contract. Dangerous.”

Rashid laughs into his cup. Ashara’s tail shifts beneath the table, avoiding a server’s path by an inch.

For the next stretch of dinner, the talk loosens. Seraphel tells a brief story about a chapel choir that lost its conductor halfway through a hymn and somehow finished in six competing tempos. Aldric maintains that the result had been devotional. Caelindra says it had been an assault on harmony. Seraphel, still smiling, declines to settle the matter.

The brass clock above the sideboard advances toward eight. Plates have thinned; the bread basket has not. Ashara glances at the clock, then at Rashid. He sees it and returns to his meal without getting up yet.
