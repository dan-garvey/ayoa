The bridge vanishes behind the next slow fold of cloud. You keep your eyes on the window a moment longer.

“I've never seen anything like that,” you say quietly to Ysolde. “Thank you.”

Ysolde watches until the pale line has gone completely.

“You are welcome,” she says. “The western current is the simplest one. It takes every globe in the same direction.”

Caelindra turns from the window with her wineglass still held by the stem. “Simple is an unexpectedly generous word for lights abandoning a bridge.”

“They do not abandon it.” Ysolde lifts one hand and moves it sideways, as she had above the table. “They show which side of the rail is safe.”

“And the unsafe side?”

“Is darker.”

Rashid’s mouth pulls briefly to one side. “That is useful information delivered with very little comfort.”

“Comfort is not the lamps’ purpose,” Ysolde says.

“Nor, apparently, is beauty,” Caelindra says.

Ysolde considers the bridge’s absent place beyond the glass. “They are beautiful from above.”

That catches Ashara’s attention. “In this weather?”

“At a distance. If you fly low enough to need the lamps, you have made several poor decisions already.” Ysolde returns to her plate. “The bridge is a very regular shape. Storms are not.”

For a little while, no one improves upon that. The green sauce sharpens the last of the meat on your plate, and the warm bread has begun to lose its steam in the basket between Thessaly and you. Servers pass behind the chairs with dishes balanced against their palms. Beyond them, the storm gives the windows a white flash, then restores the gardens to their layered dark.

The dessert plates arrive on a broad silver tray: small pear tarts with sugar glazed along the crimped crusts. The pears inside have gone translucent at their edges.

Caelindra takes one, tastes it, and pauses with her fork lowered.

“This is good,” she says, as though the kitchen has compelled a concession.

Seraphel’s wings lift a little.

“No herb at all,
only pear and sugar,
mindful of their work.”

“You make it sound virtuous,” Caelindra says.

“It has avoided tarragon.”

“A considerable achievement.”

Thessaly looks from the tart to Caelindra. “Have you made it an agreement not to contain tarragon?”

“No.”

“Then it may yet disappoint you.”

Caelindra gives her a cool look that lasts exactly long enough to be playful. “You are finding too much entertainment in this.”

“I am eating a tart. I am allowed to be entertained.”

“Winter pear,” Ysolde says, cutting through the crisp edge of her own. “It holds its shape.”

Caelindra turns to her. “You can tell?”

“Yes.”

“Through pastry.”

“Especially through pastry. Summer pears turn slack.”

Ashara reaches for a second tart. “That is true of several things in summer.”

Rashid catches the tray by its rim before it can leave the table and takes the last one.

“You had one,” Ashara says.

“Then I know they’re good.”

“Your reasoning is defective.”

“Your objection arrived after you chose yours.”

Ashara’s tail makes one short movement against the chair. “I had no objection to a second tart. I object to your taking the last.”

Rashid breaks it cleanly in half and sets one piece on the edge of her plate. “A repair, then.”

Ashara studies the offered half, then takes it without thanks. Rashid’s smile is small, but he returns to his own plate before it can become a performance.

At the far end of the table, Aldric opens the Academy-stamped volume that has been resting beside his water. The pages make a dry, substantial sound.

“Windwhisper has assigned the first forty pages for tomorrow,” he says.

“Only forty?” Caelindra says. “Her restraint is touching.”

Rashid leans far enough to read the title upside down. “A Concise Account of Founding Considerations. That is a malicious use of concise.”

“The actual account is longer,” Aldric says.

“Then it ought to be ashamed.”

Thessaly holds out her hand. Aldric passes the book across, and she turns to the first marked page. The candlelight lies flat on the close black print while she reads.

“It says the signatories reached a consensus,” she says.

“They did reach an agreement,” Aldric replies.

“That is not the same word.”

“It is close enough for an introductory account.”

“No.” Thessaly taps the line once. “An agreement can be counted. A consensus means something else.”

Caelindra looks over the rim of her glass. “It would have cost the author nothing to write the word he meant.”

“It would have cost him a more difficult sentence,” Aldric says.

“That is not a cost. That is writing.”

Ysolde has been listening to the rainless storm beyond the window. “The Draconic copy says counted assent.”

Thessaly looks at her. “Does it?”

“My aunt made me compare the translations when I was young. I preferred the diagrams.”

“Were there diagrams?” Rashid asks.

“There were maps of the borders before the bridges.” Ysolde’s voice has gained a little momentum now. “The original plate folds out three times. If you unfold it on a small desk, the western coast falls off the edge.”

“That sounds like a design problem,” Caelindra says.

“It is a map problem.”

“There is a difference?”

“Yes.”

Seraphel looks down at the open book, then at the remaining half-tart on Ashara’s plate.

“A map should stay put.
A pear should not.
Those are clear distinctions.”

Thessaly closes the volume and hands it back to Aldric. “I will read the maps.”

“There are notes attached,” Aldric says.

“Then I will read the maps and the notes that explain the maps. I decline the account.”

Rashid laughs into his cup. “Windwhisper will admire the precision of your refusal.”

“She may admire it privately.”

The folded petition has slipped out from beneath Rashid’s napkin while he reaches for his cup. Ashara puts one fingertip on its corner.

“Leave the revision at my door before first bell,” she says.

“For your approval?”

“For my criticism.”

Rashid takes the paper back and folds it once more along its existing crease. “I will give the margins room.”

“Do.”

The servers begin collecting the dessert plates. Sugar-dark crumbs remain where the tarts had been, and the candles have burned low enough to brighten the varnished table more than the faces around it. You finish the last of your meal at the same unhurried pace while the conversation loosens into smaller strands: the book’s bad title, the Academy kitchen’s uneven mercy, the westward storm. Outside, lightning moves soundlessly below the gardens. Inside, Garvey House holds its warmth around the table.
