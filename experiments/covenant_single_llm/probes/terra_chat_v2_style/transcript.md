# Terra revised-prompt replays

Exact prior player submissions and new model passages. Earlier conversation is frozen from the original Terra run.

## Original turn 4

**Player**

"Mostly stories. I give imaginary people better lines than I ever manage for myself. Occasionally letters." I glance at my ink-stained fingers. "I'd like to see an instrument improved by being unreliable." Then I turn to Rashid. "You've accounted for Ashara's afternoon. What would you do with one that belonged entirely to you?"

**Revised prompt response**

Ysolde’s grey gaze shifts briefly toward the storm beyond the windows. “The anemometer on the western terrace has taken lightning often enough that it points toward the last strike instead of into the wind,” she says. “It is a poor instrument for weather and an excellent record of the storm.”

Ashara’s tail goes still beside her chair. “Rashid has not accounted for my afternoon,” she says. “I train because I choose to.”

Rashid inclines his head once toward her. “Of course.” Then his amber eyes settle on you across the candles. “As for an afternoon entirely my own, I could name useful things to do with it. I cannot honestly tell you what I would choose once usefulness stopped being the measure.” His thumb turns the stem of his glass a fraction. “That is not an answer I make a habit of giving.”

## Original turn 6

**Player**

"Fifth bell, then. And I'll leave the letter entirely to its author." I finish dinner and take one of the spare cloaks. Before following Ysolde, I say quietly to Caelindra, "You're welcome to come too. I can promise you a few minutes without a reading or an examination." I give her room to decide, then head out with Ysolde.

**Revised prompt response**

Caelindra’s fingers pause against the stem of her glass. “Your promise is unnecessary,” she says quietly. “I have correspondence to answer. Tomorrow will be sufficient.” She turns back to the remains of her dessert.

Ysolde waits in the vestibule with her weather-cloak clasped at the throat. The spare cloak smells of cedar, old wool, and the faint mineral damp of the storm. Together, you leave Garvey House by the garden path, where winter-blooming flowers nod behind pale walls. Beyond them, the western terrace opens onto the black drop of the plateau.

An old brass anemometer stands under a squat iron hood near the parapet. Its copper cups are perfectly still. Behind clouded glass, however, a needle has climbed into the red portion of a faded dial. The terrace lamps burn straight and quiet for several minutes.

Then wind rises out of the storm and strikes the terrace hard enough to wrench at cloak hems and send the cups spinning. Ysolde watches the needle settle with the gust.

“You write stories,” she says over the rushing air. “If a thing tells the truth in a way no one intended, is it defective?”

## Original turn 15

**Player**

"There's a courier who tries to jump a stream rather than pay a penny for the bridge. He loses his boots, the parcel, and most of his dignity. At the end he's sitting on the bank explaining that the mistake was taking too little of a run-up." I leave Ashara room to find that funny or insulting. After the visit and supper, I take my three-page draft to the upper library's south window for Caelindra's reading. If she's there, I ask, "Three pages. Still a good time?"

**Revised prompt response**

Ashara considers the courier with the gravity due a tactical report. Then a short laugh escapes her.

“He had a bridge, a coin, and a parcel. Yes. That is worse.” Her tail loosens from the chair leg. “The run-up is not his problem.”

Rashid’s pen resumes across his sheltered page. The rest of the hour passes with the fire, the scratch of writing, and no further accounting demanded from anyone. Supper follows beneath the dining hall lamps, then Garvey House settles into its evening creaks and closing doors.

The upper library smells of lamp oil and old paper. At the south window, Caelindra sits beneath a green-shaded lamp, a second chair empty across from her. Her violet eyes move from you to the thin stack in your hand. She does not reach for it.

“Three,” she says. “I said five. Why have you brought three?”
