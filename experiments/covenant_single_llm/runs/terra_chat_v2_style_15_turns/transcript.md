# Covenant: one-model playtest

Exact submitted actions and model prose.

## Opening

**Player**

My character: Rowan Garvey. A twenty-three-year-old human man with brown skin, unruly dark hair, and ink-stained fingers, wearing a carefully mended charcoal coat and the inherited Garvey signet. Newly arrived and unsure of Conclave customs. He grew up unaware of his heritage, lost his parents young, and does not know whether he has any magic.

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

**Story**

Garvey House settles around you with the muffled confidence of old money and older treaties. Your room is orderly in a way nothing from your former life had reason to be: a made bed, a wardrobe stocked with clothes sent ahead by lawyers, a desk beneath the window. On it rests the Conclave primer, its pages marked with tabs on etiquette, Council procedure, and Article Nineteen. The Garvey signet catches the lamplight against the mended cuff of your charcoal coat.

A framed letter hangs beside the desk, written in a hand that has faded to brown ink. It is addressed to a great-nephew who never arrived.

Outside, the gardens lie pale beneath the evening lamps. Beyond their walls, one of the hard-light bridges reaches into the storm surrounding the plateau, its far end swallowed by moving cloud and distant lightning. No thunder follows.

Below, the house has begun to fill. Cutlery touches china in the dining hall; voices carry faintly through the stairwell and the open door of the common room. Seventeen places have been laid for the first-years’ welcome dinner, and one of them bears the name Garvey.

## Turn 1

**Player**

I check my coat and signet, then head downstairs for the welcome dinner. I pause just inside the dining-room doorway to see who is already there and how the places are arranged. "Good evening. Rowan Garvey." I give the table a small, slightly embarrassed smile.

**Story**

The repaired seams hold as you reach the ground floor, and the signet remains firm on your hand. The dining room beyond is all varnished wood and dark paneling, its steady lamps bright among low candles. Beeswax, hot bread, and something peppery hang in the air above the long table.

Seventeen settings run its length. Your card waits halfway along the garden side, between YSOLDE THORNMANTLE and CAELINDRA VAEYN; RASHID VEL AMARA is placed directly opposite. ASHARA VEL KOTHREN sits farther down beneath a candle whose flame bends faintly in her heat. Seraphel Dawnquill’s small white wings rest folded close beside Thessaly Morrow’s slowly stirring blue-black hair, while Aldric Verantus occupies a place nearer the far end. A few chairs remain open.

Your greeting stills the low talk. Ysolde lifts grey eyes from her water glass. Caelindra turns from the card at her place. Rashid gives a measured inclination, one furred hand beside his untouched glass. “Good evening, Rowan Garvey.” His amber gaze touches the signet once before returning to you. “What would you have us call you at table?”

## Turn 2

**Player**

"Rowan, please." I take the place marked for me between Ysolde and Caelindra. "And thank you for putting my name down. I was worried choosing a chair would be my first examination." I settle my napkin and make room for the servers.

**Story**

“Rowan, then,” Rashid says. “The Academy staff arranged the cards. They deserve the credit, if credit is due.”

A server in dark livery places a shallow bowl before you, fragrant with herbs and pepper. Across the candlelit table, Ashara sets her spoon aside. Heat shimmers faintly above the back of her hand.

“A seating card is not an examination,” she says. “It is an administrative courtesy.” Her gold eyes rest on you from farther down the table. “Your claim to a Conclave place is another matter. What, Rowan Garvey, do you think qualifies you to hold it?”

## Turn 3

**Player**

"The inheritance explains why I was sent. It doesn't prove I'd be good at governing anyone." I set my spoon down. "I think being here obliges me to learn before I claim that authority. If you're asking what I've earned so far, very little. I won't dress that up as an achievement."

**Story**

For a moment, only spoons touch porcelain. Ysolde watches across the rim of her water glass, still as carved ice. Caelindra lowers her eyes to her broth; near the far end of the table, Seraphel’s folded wings whisper against the back of her chair as a server passes with a covered platter.

Ashara gives a single, contained nod. “Then we agree on the central fact. Your inheritance explains your arrival; it does not establish your judgment. Admitting that does not close the gap.” Her gold eyes remain on you. “I earned my designation before witnesses. I will not call the two things equal.”

Rashid’s gaze dips briefly to the ink on your fingers, then returns to your face. “Before the verification, Rowan—what filled your days?”

## Turn 4

**Player**

"Printing work. Setting type, checking proofs, sometimes writing letters for people who wanted help with the wording. I wrote stories when there was time." I glance at the ink on my fingers, then take another spoonful of soup. "I know very little about your ordinary days yet. What do you all do when nobody is grading you?" I leave the question open to the table.

**Story**

A server in Academy livery threads shared dishes between the candles: roasted roots glossed with oil, spiced lentils, warm flatbread. Ashara serves herself with steady precision.

“Dueling. Conditioning. The law governing what force is permitted to us.” She tears a piece of bread in half. “I do not find preparation and leisure wholly separate.”

“I run before dawn,” Rashid says. “I write home. Sometimes I find a book with no conceivable Council consequence.” Ysolde’s answer follows after a pause. “The western terrace after supper. The storm has a different shape each evening.”

“By garden lamps I read at night,  
And walk the paths in softened light,” Seraphel says.

“The upper library,” Thessaly adds. “The lamps are better there.”

Caelindra has not offered an answer. She lifts her violet eyes from her plate. “You write stories,” she says. “What kind?”

## Turn 5

**Player**

"Usually people making poor decisions and finding excellent reasons for them afterward. One is about a courier who loses his boots trying to avoid a penny toll." I turn a little toward Caelindra. "It's three pages. You're welcome to read it tomorrow if you'd like." Then to Rashid and Ashara: "I'm planning to write in the common room tomorrow afternoon. You're welcome to bring work of your own, or just have tea. An invitation, not a request to abandon your training."

**Story**

Caelindra’s pointed ears shift beneath her silver hair. “Three pages is a tolerable imposition,” she says. “Bring it tomorrow, if you wish. I will determine whether your courier deserves the inconvenience he suffers.”

Ashara breaks another piece of bread. The nearest candle flame narrows in the warmth around her wrist. “Tomorrow afternoon is training. I will not be at tea.” Across the table, Rashid folds one furred hand over the other. “I have correspondence and reading. If they are finished, I may come through. Do not set a place for me.”

Caelindra turns her spoon once in the cooling broth. “Was the toll actually unjust,” she asks, “or did your courier simply mistake thrift for principle?”

## Turn 6

**Player**

"He could spare the penny. He wanted to believe jumping the stream made him clever." I nod to Ashara and Rashid. "Understood. I'll be writing whether anyone joins me or not." Then I turn to Ysolde. "I'd like to see the storm from that terrace after supper. Would you mind quiet company, or would you rather have it to yourself tonight?"

**Story**

“Then he deserves the stream,” Caelindra says, and takes a measured sip of water.

Ysolde looks toward the tall windows, where the distant storm gives the glass a dim, shifting blue. “I would not mind,” she says. “The western terrace after supper. It is usually quiet.”

## Turn 7

**Player**

I finish dinner and let the table's conversation continue. Before leaving, I tell Caelindra, "I'll have the pages in the common room tomorrow after classes." When Ysolde is ready, I go with her to the western terrace, fastening my coat against the weather and keeping a sensible distance from the edge. I take a little time to look out over the storm.

**Story**

The meal continues beneath the lamps as plates give way to roasted roots, lentils, and fruit glazed in dark syrup. Conversation separates into smaller currents: Ashara and Rashid compare tomorrow’s training times with the careful bluntness of people used to disputing in public; Seraphel’s occasional rhymed remarks draw a dry correction from Thessaly; Aldric keeps one eye on the chapel clock beyond the hall doors.

Before the chairs begin to shift, you tell Caelindra, “I’ll have the pages in the common room tomorrow after classes.”

“Leave them on the central table,” she says, folding her napkin with exact corners. “I will read them if my work permits. Do not wait beside them.”

Once the servers have cleared the final dishes, Ysolde rises and takes the corridor toward the quadrangle. You fasten your coat before following her outside. The western terrace is empty, its low stone parapet silvered by the lamp-globes. You stop several paces short of the edge while the storm turns below the plateau: cloudbanks folding over one another, slow white veins of lightning opening and fading in complete silence.

Ysolde stands at the parapet, white hair moving in the wind. “From above, it is not a wall,” she says. “There are currents through it.” Her grey eyes remain on the clouds. “Have you ever been above a storm?”

## Turn 8

**Player**

"No. Most of my experience with heights involves a ladder and someone below telling me to be careful." I look out over the clouds for a while. "I've spent all evening trying not to look overwhelmed. I think I'll give that up for a minute." I let the quiet stand instead of asking her to reassure me.

**Story**

Ysolde does not answer at once. Wind draws at the hem of her pale coat and sends your own collar shifting against your neck. Below, lightning moves sideways through a bank of cloud, bright enough to show the curled ridges and hollows before darkness folds over them again.

“It is overwhelming,” she says at last. “That is one of its qualities.”

She rests both hands on the cold parapet. “The upper clouds look soft when you fly above them. They are not.” After that, she leaves the terrace quiet, watching the currents turn beneath the Academy.

## Turn 9

**Player**

"What are they like from inside?" I keep watching the clouds rather than moving closer to her. "I've only ever had to think about weather as something to get underneath a roof for."

**Story**

Ysolde considers the clouds for several breaths.

“For me: pressure first, against the wing joints. Then cold and wet enough to make sight nearly useless.” A pale flare travels through the storm beneath the terrace, noiseless at this distance. “Cloud fills everything. You cannot see ground or sky. You choose a current by how it moves against you, and hope you chose correctly before lightning makes the question irrelevant.”

Her hands remain on the stone. “A roof is sensible, Rowan.”

## Turn 10

**Player**

"Then I'll try to appreciate them from sensible places for now. Thank you for the company." I stay a little longer without filling the quiet, then wish Ysolde a good night and return to my room. I put my coat away and set the three pages of the courier story on the desk to take with me tomorrow.

**Story**

Ysolde gives a small inclination of her head. “Good night, Rowan.”

The walk back across the quadrangle is colder than the terrace, the lamps casting white pools over black stone and living timber. By the time you return to Garvey House, most of the dining hall has gone quiet behind its closed doors.

In your room, you hang the charcoal coat away and lay the three handwritten pages of the courier story neatly on the desk beside the lamp, ready for tomorrow. The storm continues its silent turning beyond the garden window.

## Turn 11

**Player**

I put out the lamp and go to bed. I sleep until morning; the pages can wait on the desk until I take them downstairs after classes.

**Story**

Morning comes grey-blue through the garden-facing window. The storm has drawn back from the nearest bridge, leaving its hard-light span stark against a lower sea of cloud.

Somewhere below, doors open and close; water runs through old pipes, and the first movements of Garvey House begin around you. Your lamp has gone cold. The courier’s three pages remain square on the desk beside it, undisturbed.

## Turn 12

**Player**

I dress, eat breakfast, and attend the day's scheduled classes. After classes I fetch the three-page draft and leave it on the common room's central table for Caelindra, as she requested. The story follows a courier who tries to jump a stream to avoid a penny bridge toll, loses his boots and parcel, and ends up on the bank explaining that he needed a longer run-up. I put my name on the top page, make tea, and sit by the window with my own reading. I haven't reserved seats or set out extra cups.

**Story**

Breakfast passes beneath the same high lamps and polished paneling, quieter than dinner. The day’s timetable carries you through Covenant Law in the oval seminar room, where Professor Windwhisper speaks with unhurried precision beneath the framed Accords, then Practical Rhetoric with Professor Shaan, who dissects a recent border address until its courteous phrases show their edges. By late afternoon, a reading list and tomorrow’s exercise have joined the papers in your satchel.

You retrieve the courier story from your room and leave it, name uppermost, on the common room’s central table. Tea steams beside your elbow as you settle by the garden window with your own reading. Outside, students cross the pale paths in twos and threes; somewhere upstairs, a door shuts with the soft complaint of old wood.

Caelindra enters from the library corridor carrying a narrow stack of books. She sees the pages, sets her books down without sitting, and reads the draft where it lies. When she reaches the end, she places all three sheets squarely together.

“The courier loses his boots, his parcel, and his dignity,” she says. “Then he calls the matter insufficient running room.” Her violet eyes lift to yours. “Does he ever understand the toll was cheaper?”

## Turn 13

**Player**

"No. He's still arguing for the jump on the last page. I wanted the reader to see what he refuses to." I set my cup down and look toward the draft. "Does that come through without my explaining it?"

**Story**

“It comes through,” Caelindra says. “The last insistence is sufficient. You do not need to explain a man’s foolishness when he has already demonstrated it so thoroughly.”

One silver-nailed fingertip rests on the stacked pages. “Though the lost parcel changes the weight of it. Boots are comedy. A parcel may be someone else’s trouble.” She looks from the draft to you. “Do you mean the reader to laugh at him, or to resent him?”

## Turn 14

**Player**

"Both, though I may have given the laugh too much room. The courier won't admit anyone else paid for his cleverness; that doesn't mean the story should forget it. Thank you." When she's finished with the draft, I take it back and pencil a note to reconsider the parcel's consequences. I spend the rest of the afternoon working at the window, then put my papers away and go to supper when it's served.

**Story**

Caelindra looks down at the pages again. “Then give the resentment somewhere to land. If the parcel matters, its absence must inconvenience someone who did not choose the jump. Briefly is enough. A delayed errand. Someone left waiting. Otherwise he has paid only with his own boots, and the reader is asked to supply the rest.”

She squares the sheets beneath her fingertip. “Do not turn it into a sermon. His excuse is better if the damage remains ordinary.” At your thanks, she gathers her books. “You asked.” Then she leaves the draft on the table and returns through the library corridor.

A penciled note joins the pages before you settle back by the window. The afternoon thins gradually across the gardens: students cross the pale paths below, lamp-globes kindle one by one, and the common room sees only passing footsteps and the occasional creak from the upper floors. By the time you put your reading and draft away, supper’s warm scent has begun to drift in from the dining hall.

Several first-years have already taken their places beneath the candles when you arrive. A card marked GARVEY waits among the polished settings, with covered dishes steaming at the center of the long table.

## Turn 15

**Player**

I take my place for supper. If Ashara is at the table, I say, "You said yesterday that judgment has to be demonstrated. What would you consider a fair first test of it for someone arriving without your training?" I leave her room to answer, and don't mention Caelindra's reading or my conversation with Ysolde.

**Story**

Ashara is at the table, farther along the garden side, with steam thinning above the water at her setting. She sets down her fork at your question.

“Not a duel. That would establish only that you have not had my training. And not a recitation. A person can quote the Accords and still make a ruinous decision.”

“There is no single test that makes you fit for a Council seat. Tomorrow’s Covenant Law exercise can be a beginning. Read the case, choose a recommendation before the seminar, and put three things on one page: the clause you believe governs it, the action you would take, and who pays the price. Then defend that answer in the room. Do not offer a list of possibilities and call it balance.” Her gold eyes remain on you. “Bring me the page afterward if you want my view. Will you take that test?”
