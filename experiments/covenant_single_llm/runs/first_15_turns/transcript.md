# Covenant: one-model playtest

Exact submitted actions and model prose.

## Opening

**Player**

My character: Rowan Garvey. A twenty-three-year-old human man with brown skin, unruly dark hair, and ink-stained fingers, wearing a carefully mended charcoal coat and the inherited Garvey signet. Newly arrived and unsure of Conclave customs. He grew up unaware of his heritage, lost his parents young, and does not know whether he has any magic.

Begin the story shortly before the welcome dinner, in my room at Garvey House. I play Rowan Garvey.

**Story**

The room has been made ready for a man it has never met.

Your charcoal coat hangs over the chair-back, every careful mend visible in the amber light of the lamp. A trunk from the Garvey lawyers sits open at the foot of the bed, full of clothes chosen by someone with strong opinions about wool and no knowledge of you. On the desk, beside the Academy primer, your inherited signet catches the light: an unfamiliar crest pressed into old gold.

The primer lies open to *Conduct Among Signatories*. Its warning on Article Nineteen has been underlined in a clerk’s hard black hand. You have read it twice without becoming any wiser about what one says at dinner to the heirs of nations.

Above the desk hangs the letter in its narrow frame.

*To my great-nephew, should the Garvey line endure—*

The rest is in a hand both elegant and hurried. Your aunt has been dead longer than you have been alive.

Beyond the window, the gardens descend in pale terraces toward the plateau’s edge. Far below, the perpetual storm turns slowly around the Nexus, blue-white lightning moving soundlessly through cloud. One of the hard-light bridges burns across the dark toward a country you have never seen.

Voices drift up from the dining hall: a low, controlled woman’s voice; someone laughing once and then stopping; the clink of plates being laid along the long table. The house smells of beeswax, roast herbs, lamp oil, and rain that never reaches the ground.

Then the dinner bell sounds—three deliberate notes through the old house.

A moment later, footsteps pause outside your door. Someone on the landing says, in a voice dry with amusement, “If you are arranging an escape, Garvey, I advise making it before the soup.”

After the visit and supper, I take my three-page draft to the upper library's south window for Caelindra's reading. If she's there, I ask, "Three pages. Still a good time?"

**Story**

Ashara holds your gaze over the rim of her cup. Then a laugh escapes her—quick, sharp, and apparently against her will.

“Did the courier know the bridge cost a penny?”

“He did.”

“Then he was not outplayed. He was foolish.” Her tail loosens from the chair leg. “That is appreciably worse.”

Rashid’s pen pauses over his sister’s letter. “Does he recover the parcel?”

“Not yet.”

“Make him walk home in wet socks,” Ashara says. “That is consequence enough.”

Rashid slides a clean linen square across the table toward her split knuckle without comment. Ashara takes it, wraps the hand one-handed, and does not thank him; he does not appear to expect it. A moment later, he bends to his letter and writes, slowly, *At dawn the bridge lamps are blue.*

The hour remains quiet in the useful way, without anyone declaring it so. Your courier acquires a riverbank, a grievance against bridges, and three ink-dense pages before supper calls the house back into motion.

Caelindra says nothing during the meal. When the last of the plates are cleared, she rises with her slim book beneath one arm and passes your chair.

“South window,” she says.

The upper library is warm with lamp oil and old paper. Beyond the tall south glass, garden paths lie pale under the stormlit dark. Caelindra has taken a table beneath a green-shaded lamp, an open volume on river oaths pushed aside to make room.

Her violet eyes go first to your draft, then count its pages.

“Three,” she says.

“Three pages. Still a good time?”

One pointed ear shifts. “Three pages are not five. This is either concision or insubordination.”

But she extends her hand for them.

“For a fragment, it is a good time. Sit. You owe me the other two tomorrow.” Her fingers rest lightly on the top page. “And read these aloud. A story can conceal bad writing on paper. It cannot conceal it from its author’s voice.”
