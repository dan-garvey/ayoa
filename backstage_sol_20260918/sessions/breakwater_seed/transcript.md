# breakwater: published story

## Turn 1

**Player**

Begin the story.

**Story**

At a quarter past six, the room still carries the dry, shut-up smell of somewhere that hasn’t had a regular occupant in years.

Your luggage stands where Nell left you with it. The single bed is made with white linen and a faded blue blanket; opposite, a narrow wardrobe tilts slightly towards the wall. On the desk, beneath the switched-off lamp, the welcome sheet gives you four pieces of information in Nell’s tidy print:

**SUPPER — 6.30, FOYER**  
**STAFF STAIRS STRAIGHT DOWN**  
**INDUCTION — 9.00 TOMORROW**  
and her mobile number.

Beyond the window, Harbour Street slopes towards a strip of grey-green water between buildings. Masts shift behind the roofs. A gull stamps along the opposite chimney pots with something pale and unidentifiable in its beak. The September light remains clear enough to show the repairs in the cinema’s frontage below: clean glazing set into older stone, new paint beside places where old screw holes have been filled.

Your charcoal coat is still on your shoulders. Its careful mending shows plainly in this light. Ink marks the sides of your fingers around the brass bedroom key.

The building carries sound in unexpected ways. Crockery knocks together downstairs. A chair scrapes across the foyer floor, followed by a burst of laughter from a woman you haven’t met. Somewhere close on this floor, machinery gives a brief electronic chirp.

“Don’t put those there,” another woman says below. “Those are tomorrow’s cups.”

“They look remarkably like tonight’s cups.”

“They’re in a sealed box, Owen.”

“Temporary distinction.”

A door opens off the landing. Footsteps cross to the top of the stairs, pause, then go down quickly.

“Six-thirty means food on the table at six-thirty,” someone calls from the direction of the kitchen. “It doesn’t mean begin discussing the table at six-thirty.”

“That’s Jo,” Nell’s voice answers from below. “You’ll learn to fear precision.”

“I heard that.”

The smell reaches the room a moment later: cooked tomatoes, onion, warm pastry. Beneath it lingers the cinema’s older mixture of carpet, plaster and dust disturbed by recent work.

Your door stands ajar. Across the landing, a narrow pane in the projection-booth door shows a moving figure against bluish equipment lights. Down the stairwell, the foyer glows gold, and several conversations overlap among people who already know where the plates are kept and which chairs wobble.

Then Nell calls up, “Rowan, supper’s ready when you are.”

## Turn 2

**Player**

I go down to the staff supper, introduce myself as Rowan, and take an available place.

**Story**

You step onto the landing just as the projection-booth door opens. A man in a dark work shirt comes out carrying a shallow equipment case, its foam lining crowded with small black receivers.

“You must be Rowan. Dev.” He shifts the case under one arm. “I do projection and sound, when either agrees to be done.”

He heads downstairs with you. The foyer opens below: restored glass doors onto Harbour Street, folded programmes stacked on one table, cartons occupying two more. At the joined supper tables, seven places are already claimed by cutlery, mugs or elbows. The eighth chair stands empty between a broad-shouldered younger man cutting bread and a woman in green-framed glasses.

Nell emerges from the office as you reach the bottom stair.

“There he is. Everyone, this is Rowan.”

“I’m Rowan,” you say.

Names come back in a loose circuit. Jo is the short woman setting a steaming casserole on a cork mat, with a tea towel over one shoulder. Owen, beside the empty chair, lifts the bread knife in greeting. Bea smiles and moves her mug to give you more room. Across from her, Rosa has tucked a camera case beneath her seat. Martin, silver-haired and paint-marked at one thumbnail, says, “Welcome to the unfinished masterpiece.” Dev places his equipment case safely against the wall before sitting beside Nell.

“Don’t let Martin describe the wiring,” Jo says. “Not while we’re eating.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You did it over sandwiches on Friday.”

“Sandwiches aren’t a proper meal.”

You take the available place. Your mended coat brushes the back of the chair as Owen passes you the breadboard.

Jo removes the lid from the casserole. Tomato, garlic and chickpeas steam into the foyer’s cooler air. Beside it waits a deep cheese-and-onion pie, its crust browned unevenly at the edges, along with a wooden bowl of salad.

“Help yourselves,” she says. “Rowan, you’re my guest tonight. After this, you enter the ruthless world of ingredient contributions.”

“Three pounds fifty,” Martin tells you solemnly.

“Three pounds,” Jo corrects. “He keeps trying to fund pudding by misinformation.”

Plates begin travelling around the table. The conversation resumes in overlapping pieces: a missing box of teaspoons, whether the front-door mat has been ordered, and a strip of blue paint Owen found beneath one of the foyer panels. Nell eats while making a note on the back of an envelope, until Jo reaches across and turns the envelope face down.

“No minutes,” Jo says. “This isn’t a meeting.”

“It was one word.”

“It was becoming a list. I know the early signs.”

Nell leaves the envelope where it is.

Bea serves herself a small piece of pie, then glances at your ink-stained fingers as you receive the salad bowl.

“Did you come to this through old cinemas,” she asks, “or through archive work?”

## Turn 3

**Player**

I ask Nell what my first day of archive work will involve.

**Story**

You set down the salad bowl and turn to Nell.

“What will my first day of archive work involve?”

Nell folds her hands around her mug. “Nine o’clock, we start in the office. Employment forms, fire procedure, where everything’s kept. Then I’ll take you through the archive room and the catalogue we’re using.”

“The interesting forms come after the employment forms,” Bea says.

“Some material belongs to the trust,” Nell continues. “Some was lent to us under specific agreements. Tomorrow we’ll read those before you handle any loans. Being in the room doesn’t automatically mean we can scan something, publish it or put it in Rosa’s exhibition.”

Rosa nods over her plate. “My exhibition has enough to be getting on with.”

“Your first actual box will probably be business papers,” Nell says. “Invoices, correspondence, old staff records. We’ll catalogue a few items together so we agree on descriptions and condition terms. After that, programmes. Don’t rearrange shelves yet, even when their present order appears deranged.”

“It is deranged,” Martin says.

“It may also contain evidence of how it was used. We record before we improve.” Nell takes a piece of bread. “And if Martin remembers exactly who made something in 1986, write down that Martin remembers it. Don’t turn it into a confirmed date until something supports him.”

Martin looks across the table. “This feels pointed.”

“It’s based on extensive field research.”

Bea laughs into her mug. Martin concedes the matter with a small lift of his fork.

“You’ll have the workroom key after the induction,” Nell tells you. “I’ll be downstairs most of tomorrow. If provenance or permission isn’t clear, leave it unclear and bring it to me. I’d rather have ten sound records than fifty guesses.”

Dev glances towards the clock above the cafe counter. It is twenty minutes to seven.

“While I remember,” he says, “Hana’s coming at seven to help test the new hearing receivers. They arrived this afternoon. We should only need the auditorium for twenty minutes.”

Rosa stops cutting her pie. Martin looks from her to Nell.

“The auditorium’s booked,” Nell says.

Dev’s attention sharpens. “Tonight?”

“Seven until seven-forty. Rosa and Martin have the programme boards in there.”

“I didn’t see anything on the shared schedule.”

“It’s in the paper diary. I entered it Friday.”

Rosa rests her knife beside her plate. “We’re photographing six boards and recording Martin. Test audio will be on the recording.”

“And I need to walk the receivers through the seating,” Dev says. He looks at the clock again. “Hana can only stay until half seven.”

Nell exhales through her nose. “I should’ve mentioned the booking when you arrived. But no—the auditorium isn’t available. Rosa booked it first.”

Nobody appeals to you. The decision remains where it belongs.

Dev sits back. For a moment his thumb presses hard against the edge of his phone. Then he unlocks it.

“All right. I’ll stop Hana before she comes across town. I should’ve checked.”

“I didn’t know you were trying to fit this in,” Rosa says.

“No. It’s not yours to solve.” He begins typing. “The receivers can wait.”

Jo pushes the pie dish towards him. “That can’t. Owen made the pastry, and it’s approaching the limits of its engineering.”

“It’s perfectly stable,” Owen says.

A wedge slumps as Dev serves it.

Owen studies the collapsed layers. “It was perfectly stable.”

## Turn 4

**Player**

I help myself to food and listen to the conversation around the table.

**Story**

You spoon chickpea stew onto your plate, add a wedge of pie and take a piece of bread from the board Owen passes back. The stew is thick with tomato and cumin; the pie crust flakes beneath your fork, exposing soft onion and sharp cheese.

Dev’s phone vibrates against the table.

“She hasn’t left yet,” he says after reading the message. “I’ll rearrange with her.”

“Tell me when, and I’ll put it in the diary,” Nell says.

“I’ll check the diary first.”

The tightness around the table eases. Rosa reaches beneath her chair for a notebook but leaves the camera case where it is.

“Which board do you want to discuss first?” Martin asks her.

“The yellow one. On Thursday you said June Mercer painted the lettering.”

“She did the border. I painted the title.”

Bea looks up. “I remember June doing the title in the back corridor.”

“That was another one.”

“It was yellow.”

“We owned a lot of yellow paint.”

Rosa opens the notebook. “I’ll record both versions. There may be a dated photograph somewhere.”

“There might be one in your mum’s album,” Martin says to Bea. “June used to turn up in the background of everything.”

Bea lays down her fork. “Possibly. But the album is here for cataloguing, not for the exhibition.”

“I didn’t say we should exhibit it.”

“I know. I’m making the limit clear.”

Rosa answers before the pause can lengthen. “I’m not asking to use it. Tonight is the six boards, nothing else.”

Nell nods. “The album’s loan agreement permits inventory and condition notes. No scanning or display. Rowan and I will go through that agreement tomorrow with the others.”

Bea rubs a crumb from the edge of her plate. “Once I’ve spoken to the people in the photographs, I might change that. Some of them are family pictures. They weren’t taken for an audience.”

“Fair enough,” Martin says. “The board may identify itself without help.”

“It won’t,” Rosa says. “But I’ll enjoy watching you try.”

Owen passes Martin the salad. “Could check the paint layers.”

Martin brightens. “Exactly.”

“You are not scraping Rosa’s board,” Bea says.

“I said check.”

“With your eyes,” Rosa adds.

Conversation moves on to the restored sign above the entrance. Martin believes one of its lamps is a different colour from the rest; Nell says they will test it after dark tomorrow. Her hand strays towards the overturned envelope beside her plate.

Jo places two fingers on it without looking up. “Still supper.”

Nell withdraws her hand. “I wasn’t writing.”

“You were preparing to write.”

At five to seven, Rosa closes her notebook and lifts the camera case. Martin takes the last piece of bread before rising. Bea follows them towards the auditorium, carrying her mug.

Dev remains seated long enough to finish his pie, then picks up the equipment case. “I can log the receiver numbers tonight, at least.”

“Come down before you leave,” Nell says. “I want to know where we stand with the system.”

He nods and goes upstairs.

Behind the auditorium doors, Rosa’s voice begins organizing the work.

“Let’s start with the yellow board. Martin, give me the painter’s name as you remember it, and say when you first saw it.”

A moment later, Martin answers, more formally than he has spoken all evening.

At the table, Jo stacks two empty plates. Owen gathers the cutlery, and Nell finally turns her envelope back over.
