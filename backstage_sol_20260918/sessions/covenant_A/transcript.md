**Story**

*The first-year welcome table observes the custom of its residence.*

On the desk, the sentence looks almost smug beneath the lamp.

Garvey House offers no clarification. Voices rise through the floorboards—chairs shifting, silverware being counted, someone objecting that the centerpiece is “an armed shrub.” Warm air carries rosemary, baked pears, and woodsmoke up the central stair.

A young woman stands there in white and gold. Warm light rests beneath her skin, gentler than the lamps, and two small white wings sit folded behind her shoulders. Her blue eyes flick from the signet to the open primer.

“You must be Rowan Garvey. The signet makes that clear.  
I’m Seraphel Dawnquill; the dining hall is near.

“If you’re waiting for a rule that tells you when to go,

“Would you like some company when you walk through the door?

The chapter uses *concordance* in a technical sense. It is the work of translating an obligation between magical traditions without accidentally changing what either party has promised.

A fae agreement depends upon consent and exact terms. An angelic oath may depend upon ritual witness and moral certainty. A demon compact can pledge life force; an elven boundary may remain active long after its makers die. Identical words do not necessarily create identical obligations.

Human arbiters were valued because they could learn limited fragments of several traditions. The book is careful to say this did not grant mastery. It allowed them to recognize when two parties believed they had agreed while their magic enforced different promises.

One example concerns a dragon’s grant of “temporary” mining rights. The dragon intended permission lasting until her death; the human merchants understood a term of twelve years.
