## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

When you come downstairs, Bea is at one of the cafe tables with a packed lunch and a folder of trust receipts. Nell sits opposite her, drinking tea while checking figures against a bank statement.

You bring your lunch to the table and ask Bea, “What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?”

“We’re not properly open until the twenty-sixth,” she says. “But I know what you mean.”

She looks across the foyer towards the front doors.

“I’m not most excited about the speeches or the opening photographs. I’m waiting for the first wet afternoon when a dozen people come in because they’ve nowhere better to be. Jo sells them tea, somebody drops popcorn, and the heating works without Martin having to hit anything.”

“I don’t hit heating systems,” Martin’s absent reputation is defended by Nell. “He does occasionally threaten them.”

Bea smiles. “It was shut for years, and every conversation was about what the building used to be or what it might become. Now people are worrying about cups, rotas and whether the sound reaches row twelve. Those are useful problems.”

She closes the receipt folder.

“I like Rosa putting names beside the old boards, too. My mother cleaned here. I tore tickets. Martin painted lettering. The cinema didn’t run itself, even if that’s how people remember it.”

After a moment, she adds, “And I wanted the sign restored. More than was financially sensible.”

“It stayed inside the approved budget,” Nell says.

“Only because I approved the budget.”

The office phone call you heard earlier was from Dev. Nell tells Bea that Hana has confirmed the auditorium test for seven that evening, so the provisional booking is now firm. Martin plans to test the restored sign at eight.

“I’ll come back for that,” Bea says. No invitation is attached; she simply notes the time and resumes her lunch.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Nell and Bea finish checking the last two receipts. Bea clips them into the folder, takes her lunch things and leaves for the afternoon without extending the conversation.

You return upstairs and unlock the archive workroom.

The programme box occupies most of the afternoon. You establish its date range, preserve the sequence in which the weekly sheets were found and record gaps without assuming the missing programmes were ever present. Duplicate sheets are counted rather than discarded. Pencilled changes to show times remain part of the individual items.

One folded programme has split along its centre crease. You place it in a protective sleeve and record the damage without attempting a repair. Several others carry small rust stains from fasteners that are no longer attached.

Nell comes up once to review the notes in the yellow tray. The unsigned collection list remains with its original file; she agrees that it describes movement of material but cannot prove ownership of everything it names. She leaves the uncertainty visible in the catalogue.

By half past three, the agreed end of today’s shift, the transfer box has file-level records and the first programme box has a completed overview with its gaps and damaged items noted. You return everything to its marked shelf, save the catalogue and lock the workroom.

Downstairs, you enter six working hours on the time sheet, excluding lunch. Nell is in the office, but she does not hold you there with another task. The auditorium test and the sign lighting remain evening projects belonging to the people who arranged them.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, you find Nell in the office with the paper diary open beside her computer. The pencilled auditorium hold from yesterday has been replaced with a firm entry.

You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Programmes again this morning,” she says. “Take the box marked 1987 to 1990. The transfer schedule covers the programme collection, but check that the contents match the label. Start with a box-level record, then make separate entries for anything heavily annotated or in poor condition.”

She points to a second grey case on her list.

“Leave that one for now. It includes old payroll sheets and home addresses. The trust owns the papers, but I’m still setting the access note for personal information.”

Nell closes the diary.

“Upstairs arrangements are unchanged. Keep the loan cupboard locked, and put unresolved ownership in the yellow tray.”

The hearing-assistance test went ahead the previous evening. Nell explains that reception initially dropped out near the back corner of the auditorium; Dev adjusted the transmitter, and Hana repeated the walk through the seats without finding another gap. The receivers are now charging in the office for opening weekend.

“The sign lit as well,” she adds. “Martin was right about one lamp being a colder colour. It works, but I’ve reported it to the restoration company.”

The only change that affects the building this morning is a cafe delivery due through the service yard at eleven.

“Jo will have the side door open while they unload,” Nell says. “It still needs locking again as soon as they’re finished.”

She gives you the shelf reference for the programme box. The archive room remains available for your assigned work, with no exhibition materials or unrecorded loans added overnight.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before leaving the office, you say, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell rests her hand on the closed diary.

“Understood. You haven’t been assigned any front-of-house hours, and I won’t add you to that rota. Your contract is for thirty hours of archive work.”

She glances towards the staff staircase.

“The room is included with the job. It doesn’t make you available after your shift. If somebody asks you directly, send them to me.”

Nell returns to her computer. Your assignment remains the programme box upstairs; no evening, weekend or front-of-house work is added to it.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

During your morning break, you lock the archive room and go downstairs. Jo’s delivery has been unloaded, and the side door is shut again. Boxes of dry goods stand inside the kitchen with supplier labels still attached.

Owen is at a cafe table with a mug of tea. A screwdriver and several clear programme holders lie beside him, but he has set the work aside while he drinks.

You ask, “How’s your week going?”

“It’s changed a bit since Monday.” He turns the mug between his hands. “I’ve got an interview tomorrow for a permanent exhibition-technician job at a museum inland.”

He says *interview* carefully, without treating it as an offer.

“I replied this morning. Nell’s confirmed that I’ll work Friday instead of Thursday. I told my dad on the phone last night, too.”

Owen takes another drink.

“He didn’t say much at the time. Ten minutes later he sent me the train times. This morning I got a link to the museum car park.”

Jo passes through the foyer carrying an invoice. She hears this but leaves the conversation to him.

“So that’s tomorrow,” Owen says. “The rest of the week is mostly getting these holders level before anyone puts programmes in them.” He nods towards the clear stands, then looks across at you. “How’s the archive going?”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday, probably,” he says. “I’m at the museum tomorrow.”

He sets his mug aside and turns back to the programme holders. You leave him to the work.

Outside, you walk down Harbour Street alone. The pavement is still damp, and the wind carries salt, diesel and the sharper smell of fish from the quay. At the inner harbour, the tide has fallen far enough to expose a band of dark weed along the stone walls.

Two fishing boats are tied alongside, their decks crowded with coiled rope and stacked pots. A man in orange waterproof trousers hoses scales towards a drain while another checks plastic crates against a clipboard. Across the water, intermittent hammering comes from the boatyard sheds.

You follow the quay past a shuttered excursion kiosk and a row of benches facing the moorings. Gulls wait on the harbour rail, lifting aside only when you come close. Nobody joins you or draws you into conversation.

The circuit takes just over ten minutes. You return by the same wet street, let yourself in through the cinema’s side door and reach the archive room before your break is over. Downstairs, Owen has resumed fixing the clear holders to the foyer wall.
