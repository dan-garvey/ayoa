# sample-09

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you take your lunch to one of the cafe tables.

Jo is behind the counter with a saucepan, three identical bowls and a kitchen scale. She ladles carrot-and-lentil soup into each bowl, weighs them, then pours one back and begins again. A lidded portion already sits apart with **FRAN** written across the top.

Bea is at the table in a fleece bearing the boatyard’s logo, her payroll pass still clipped at the waist. She has a cheese-and-pickled-walnut sandwich, a yellow-glazed mug and a padded envelope containing a secondhand DVD.

You sit across from her and ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“The sign,” she says first. “I approved more money for that sign than a treasurer ought to enjoy spending. I want to stand across the street and see it lit.”

She eats another bite of her sandwich before continuing.

“But mostly I want people here who haven’t come to inspect the project. We’ve had two years of contractors, grant visitors and people admiring the toilets. I want a wet afternoon when somebody comes in late because the bus was late, buys a ticket and doesn’t ask who restored the plaster.”

Jo keeps her attention on the scale. “Wet afternoons are the ones I’m trying to price for. Opening day will be full. A quiet Thursday is what tells me whether this works.”

Bea nods. Their interests meet there, though not for quite the same reason.

“When I ushered, the five minutes before a film were often better than the film,” Bea says. “Couples negotiating seats. Teenagers walking straight to the back as if nobody could see them. My mother could look at an audience going in and tell me which row would need the most cleaning.”

She touches the padded envelope.

“My granddaughter and I send each other terrible films. This one takes forty minutes to produce its giant crab, and then you can see the wheels under it. She’ll come here once we’re properly open and complain that the seats don’t recline.”

Bea’s answer trails off while she looks towards the locked front doors and the pale daylight beyond them.

Jo fits a lid onto the last portion of soup.

“For Fran?” Bea asks.

“Night shift. If I leave it at home, she’ll tell me toast was sufficient.”

Bea checks her watch and wraps the remainder of her sandwich. “I need to post this monstrosity and get back to the boatyard.”

As she leaves, the side door opens for Owen. He comes in carrying a tool bag and watches Bea cross the foyer before turning towards the office.

“Nell, can I have a word before I start?”

Nell brings him inside and closes the office door. Jo’s marker pauses above the next label, then resumes. She does not call after him or explain.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

You leave Owen and Nell to their closed-door conversation and return upstairs.

The afternoon’s programme box is less orderly than the transfer papers. Weekly sheets have been folded into quarters, bundled with string and then partly rearranged. You record each dated run without imposing a new order on it.

The programmes show the Picture House in small, practical details. Admission prices are corrected by hand whenever the printer’s figures fall behind. A children’s matinee shares a sheet with a late horror film. Local advertisements fill the reverse: a boatyard apprenticeship scheme, a fish merchant offering Friday deliveries, and **WARD’S LAUNDRETTE — GUEST-HOUSE COLLECTIONS**, with Monday, Wednesday and Friday routes printed beneath it.

One staff copy has been used to tally admissions in grease pencil. Another carries a tea order and a reminder to hold two aisle seats until ten past seven. You describe the annotations without trying to identify the hands that made them.

Below, work continues without coming upstairs. Jo’s van starts in the yard shortly before two. Later, measured knocks travel through the foyer, followed by the scrape of timber and Owen asking somebody to hold one end level.

At your agreed finish time of half past three, you complete the entry already open. The programmes return to their bundle, the box goes back to its marked shelf and the catalogue is saved. No loaned material has been removed. You lock the workroom and go downstairs to enter your finish time.

Owen and Martin are fitting a wooden rail beneath the programme display. Owen holds a spirit level against it while Martin supports the far end.

“Use the longer fixings,” Martin says. “You don’t want to be tightening this again in November.”

Owen keeps his eyes on the bubble. “I might not be here in November.”

“You’ll still be in town.”

“I’ve got an interview on Thursday. Permanent exhibition-technician job at a museum inland.”

Martin’s end of the rail drops slightly.

“You applied for that?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Before I started here. They offered the interview yesterday.”

Martin looks towards the office. “What about your shift?”

“Nell’s moved it to Friday. I replied to the museum at lunch.”

“They haven’t offered you the job.”

“No. That’s why it’s an interview.”

For several seconds, neither moves the rail.

“They’ll need people here after October,” Martin says.

“They might. Nobody’s offered me that either.”

Martin adjusts his grip. “Do you need a lift on Thursday?”

“I can get the train.”

“All right.”

Owen watches the level settle again. The interview has been accepted; nothing beyond it has been decided. Martin holds the rail while his son marks the next fixing point.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, the foyer is already in use. Jo has a supplier’s crates open along the cafe counter and is checking jars against an invoice. Owen works alone beneath the programme display, filling yesterday’s screw holes before fixing the rail again.

Nell is in the office with damp hair and a mesh swimming bag tucked beneath her desk. You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Same hours as yesterday,” she says. “Finish the programme box you started. After that, take C-seven from the second shelf. It’s booking and programming correspondence from 1985 to 1989.”

She hands back the note you placed in the yellow tray. Beneath your reference, she has written that the unsigned collection list should remain with the transfer file but cannot itself establish ownership.

“C-seven is trust-owned under the transfer schedule. Catalogue it at file level. Keep incoming letters with any reply copies you find. There are requests from local clubs and societies mixed in with the distributor correspondence; don’t separate them just because they came from outside the business.”

Nell glances towards the open front doors, where the supplier is bringing in another crate.

“Jo’s delivery will keep the foyer open until about ten. Lock the archive whenever you come downstairs. The loan cupboard arrangements haven’t changed, and you don’t need anything from it today.”

She adds that Dev and Hana completed the auditorium receiver test the previous evening. No technical work is scheduled upstairs today.

“That’s everything that affects you.”

Behind you, Jo removes a jar from the latest crate and holds it beside the invoice. The label does not match what she ordered. Owen keeps filling the old holes while she goes to find the driver.

You take the archive stairs with a clear assignment: finish the programme run, then begin the correspondence in box C-seven.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before you leave the office, you tell Nell, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

“Understood,” she says. “You aren’t on the front-of-house rota, and I wasn’t planning to add you. Your thirty hours are for the archive. The room doesn’t put you on call.”

She returns the assignment slip to you.

“If we’re short downstairs, I’ll find cover downstairs.”

No appeal or qualification follows. You go back up to the archive while Jo continues sorting out the incorrect delivery and Owen works on the programme rail.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

Near eleven, you finish the programme-box entry, lock the archive room and go downstairs for your break.

Owen is at one of the cafe tables with a mug of tea. Fine white filler dust covers one knee of his work trousers, and the newly mounted programme rail runs along the wall behind him. A spirit level and drill remain on the floor beneath it.

You sit nearby and ask how his week is going.

“Uneven,” he says. “The interview’s tomorrow. I accepted it yesterday, and Nell’s moved my shift. Dad knows now, which was the part I kept putting off.”

He turns his mug between both hands.

“He sent me three train routes before seven this morning. I’d already bought the ticket.”

Owen glances at the programme rail, checking it from this angle.

“I like working here. That doesn’t mean there’ll be a job for me after October, but Dad keeps talking as if the two things are the same. I haven’t decided anything beyond getting on the train tomorrow.”

His break ends a few minutes later. He rinses his mug, picks up the spirit level and returns to the wall. You go back upstairs without either of you stretching the conversation further.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

The rest of your shift passes in box C-seven. Among the distributor letters are requests from a hospital social club, a youth theatre and a tenants’ association asking for particular films or different screening times. Replies survive for some and not for others. You keep each reply with its request and record the gaps without supplying an explanation.

At half past three, you secure the box, save the catalogue and lock the workroom.

Owen is packing his drill into its case when you come downstairs.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell him.

“Friday,” he says. “Interview tomorrow.”

He closes the case and returns to gathering his tools.

You leave by the side door and walk down Harbour Street alone. The afternoon rain has cleared, leaving the paving dark and the gutters busy. At the inner harbour, the tide is low enough to expose weed on the lower steps and a band of black mud beneath the quay wall.

Two fishing boats are unloading grey crates under a cloud of gulls. Farther along, the boatyard’s open shed frames the pale hull of a vessel raised on supports. Hammering carries across the water, irregular and much louder than it seems from the far side.

You follow the harbour road past stacked lobster pots, a shuttered summer kiosk and a bench facing the moorings. The air smells of diesel, salt and wet rope. Workers from the packing depot cross towards the bus stop in coats pulled over their uniforms; none of them turns towards the cinema road.

Beyond the inner harbour, the wall curves towards open water. Waves strike its seaward face and send brief veils of spray above the stone. You continue along it by yourself, with Lowhaven behind you and the repaired Picture House hidden among the roofs.
