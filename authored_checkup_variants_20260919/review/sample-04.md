# sample-04

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you go down to the foyer. Bea is not there.

Jo has spread supplier lists and a calculator across one end of a cafe table. Her lunch is a bowl of rice pudding with a conspicuous layer of brown sugar stirred through it. Owen sits opposite her, his tool bag under the chair; he has spent the morning fitting a display rail beside the front doors. Nell brings her lunch out of the office, and you eat with the three of them.

Jo circles a figure on one price sheet.

“If the dairy confirms this by three, I’ll finish the menu costs today. If they don’t, it waits until Thursday.”

“I thought you were doing it tomorrow,” Nell says.

“Fran and I are both off tomorrow. That almost never happens, and I’m not turning it into a day of supplier calls. You’ll still have my cafe hours this afternoon.”

Nell considers the calendar beside Jo’s papers. “Thursday is fine for the prices.”

Jo adds another spoonful of sugar to the rice pudding and returns to the list.

Partway through lunch, Owen takes out his phone.

“I sent the shift change,” he tells Nell. “Thursday to Friday, same hours.”

Nell checks her work messages. “I have it. Friday, ten until four. Confirmed.”

“And I replied to the museum. I’m taking the interview on Thursday.”

He says it to Nell, not to you, but the small table leaves no private edge to the conversation. Jo continues folding a supplier sheet along its crease; the news does not surprise her.

Nell sets down her phone. “All right. Thanks for telling me.”

After a moment she asks, “Does Martin know?”

“No.” Owen looks towards the empty chair at the end of the table. “I’m going round tonight. I’d rather tell him before the interview than explain afterwards why I kept it quiet.”

Neither Nell nor Jo tells him what Martin’s answer will be. The confirmed Friday shift goes onto Nell’s rota, and Owen pockets his phone. Martin remains elsewhere, still without the information Owen has decided to give him.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

The conversation ends without anyone trying to predict Martin’s response. Owen returns to the display rail, Jo gathers her supplier sheets, and Nell takes the amended rota back into the office.

You go upstairs and resume the programme box.

Through the afternoon, you establish its actual date range rather than relying on the label. Several weekly sheets are duplicated, two have been filed out of sequence, and a run of summer dates is missing altogether. You record the gaps without guessing whether the programmes were lost or never collected.

Pencilled changes to screening times remain untouched and are included in the descriptions. Rusted staples, small edge tears and old water marks go into the condition fields. The box’s provenance links back to the signed 2024 transfer schedule; where that connection is clear, you record the trust as owner.

Sounds from the foyer come and go beneath the workroom: Owen’s drill in short bursts, the front doors opening for a delivery, Nell speaking on the office telephone. None of it interrupts the catalogue.

By the agreed end of your shift, the first programme box has a complete box-level record and its distinct groups have been identified. The next box remains unopened for tomorrow.

You save the work, return every programme to its original position and put the box back on its marked shelf. The loan cupboard has remained locked throughout the afternoon. After locking the workroom, you go down to the office and enter your finishing time on the hours sheet.

Nell checks the clock and nods. “That’s you finished. Leave the next box until tomorrow.”

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Owen is tightening the display rail he fitted beside the front doors. Martin kneels nearby, adjusting the door closer with a spanner. Neither has been brought in merely to talk; their tools and open fittings occupy opposite ends of the foyer.

“I’ve been thinking about what you said last night,” Martin says. “An interview isn’t an offer.”

“I know.”

“You might get there and hate the place.”

“I know that too.”

Martin folds a printed rail timetable into quarters. “The eight-twelve gets you there with forty minutes to spare.”

“I’ve booked the eight-twelve.”

“Right.” Martin puts the timetable on Owen’s tool bag anyway. Owen leaves it there and returns to the rail.

At nine, you find Nell in the office and ask what archive work you should begin with and whether anything practical has changed.

“Second programme box first,” she says. “If you finish that, return to the transfer correspondence. No loaned material today.”

She has reviewed yesterday’s yellow tray. One collection list can be matched to a numbered attachment in the signed transfer schedule. The broad, unsigned inventory cannot, so its catalogue record will retain **ownership unconfirmed**.

“Keep the note with it,” Nell says. “We may find supporting paperwork later.”

She gives you the immediate building updates. Dev confirmed the auditorium before Tuesday’s noon deadline, and he and Hana completed the receiver test last night. Jo is taking today off with Fran, so the cafe is closed; the kitchen sink and kettle remain available, but Jo’s labelled stock and equipment do not.

“One future date as well,” Nell says. “I’ll be away from the ninth to the eleventh of October. Gina and I have a walking weekend booked, and I’m taking it. That falls inside your contract, so I’ll arrange archive cover and cupboard-key access before I go. Until then, the current system stays the same.”

She returns the reviewed notes to the transfer box and leaves the second programme box ready for you on the archive table.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you tell Nell. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

“Understood.” She sets down her pen. “Your contract is for thirty hours of archive work. I haven’t put you on the front-of-house rota, and I won’t use the fact that you live upstairs as emergency availability.”

She indicates the October dates she has just mentioned.

“My leave affects who can authorize archive access while I’m away. It doesn’t make you responsible for covering the cinema.”

Nell adds **ARCHIVE CONTRACT ONLY** beside your name in her staffing notes, then closes the rota.

“That’s clear now.”

The second programme box remains on the workroom table, ready for the morning’s assigned work.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

You work through the second programme box until the next complete group is catalogued, then close it before taking your break.

Owen is in the foyer with his tools laid aside. He sits at one of the cafe tables drinking from a metal flask. The timetable Martin printed protrudes from the open pocket of his tool bag.

“How’s your week going?” you ask.

“Busy, but more sorted than it was.” He looks towards the newly fixed display rail. “Nell confirmed Friday, so tomorrow’s clear for the interview. I told Dad last night.”

He turns the flask cup once between his hands.

“He didn’t say much about the museum. This morning he checked the trains and told me an interview isn’t an offer. That’s about where we are.”

Owen takes another drink. “The rail’s finally straight, though.”

After a moment, he looks across at you. “How’s the archive work going?”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he corrects. “I’m off tomorrow for the interview. I’ll be back here Friday morning.”

He returns the cup to his flask, and you leave him to his break.

Outside, you walk down Harbour Street towards the inner harbour. The earlier rain has stopped, leaving the paving stones dark and the gutters running. A cold wind comes between the buildings with the smell of salt, diesel and wet rope.

Fishing boats lie against the quay wall, rising and settling on their lines. Two crew members unload grey crates without looking up from their work. Across the water, a grinder starts at the boatyard, its pitch carrying over the engines and the slap of water against stone.

You follow the harbour edge alone. September visitors are sparse at this hour; most of the people moving along the quay are working, making deliveries or carrying tools between boats. Gulls cluster around a stack of blue fish boxes and scatter when a small forklift approaches.

At the end of the inner quay, you turn back. The walk takes just under fifteen minutes. Nobody stops you, and you return to the Picture House before the end of your break.
