# packet-10

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

Mid-morning, you come downstairs for a break. Owen is at one of the cafe tables with a mug of tea, his phone face down beside it. A half-assembled programme stand waits near the front doors, but he has put the tools away for the moment.

You sit nearby and ask, “How’s your week going?”

“Busy.” He turns the mug between his hands. “I replied to the museum, so the interview’s definitely happening tomorrow. I want to go. I’m not making decisions about a job they haven’t offered me.”

He glances towards the side corridor.

“I told Dad last night. He offered to drive me, then started saying the cinema might keep me on permanently. I had to tell him Nell hasn’t offered that, and I can’t plan around a job that doesn’t exist.”

Owen flattens one corner of his tea bag’s paper wrapper.

“He went quiet. But he texted me good luck this morning, so we’re speaking.”

The side door opens, and Martin comes in from the yard with a coil of cable over one shoulder. He pauses when he sees Owen.

“The offer of a lift tomorrow still stands,” he says.

“I’ve got the journey sorted. But thanks.”

“All right. Let me know when you’re finished.”

“I will.”

Martin accepts that and carries the cable towards the office without trying to reopen the decision. Owen drinks the rest of his tea, then takes his tools back to the programme stand as your break ends.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“Friday,” he says. “I’m off here tomorrow for the interview.”

He returns to the programme stand, and you go back to the catalogue until the midday break.

Then you leave the Picture House alone and walk down Harbour Street. The inner harbour lies beyond the next row of buildings, enclosed by stone arms darkened at the waterline. Fishing boats sit against the quay with their decks crowded by crates, rope and orange floats. A crew in waterproof trousers unloads insulated tubs while a forklift carries empty pallets towards the packing depot.

You follow the harbour road. The morning rain has passed, but shallow water remains in the joins between paving stones. Wind lifts the hem of your charcoal coat and brings the mixed smell of diesel, salt and fish scales washed from the quay.

Past the boatyard gates, masts and scaffold poles form a shifting grid against the sky. Hammering carries from inside one of the sheds. Farther out, the harbour wall narrows towards the breakwater, where waves strike the seaward blocks and spill white spray through the gaps.

You walk as far as a bench overlooking the harbour entrance. A small trawler comes in against the tide, engine labouring, with gulls following close behind its stern. Nobody joins you or asks for your attention.

After a few minutes, you retrace the quay and return towards Harbour Street on your own.
