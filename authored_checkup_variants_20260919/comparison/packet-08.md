# packet-08

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

Downstairs, lunch has formed around the work rather than stopping it.

Owen and Martin have been fitting a wooden rail beneath the foyer’s programme display. Their tools lie along the wall on a folded dust sheet. Jo sits at the end of one cafe table with a bowl of rice pudding and a supplier’s price list pushed well beyond her elbow. On the counter behind her, a lidded container marked **FRAN** waits beside a small insulated bag.

Nell’s office door is shut. Her voice rises and falls behind it on a telephone call.

You sit down with your lunch just before Bea comes through the front doors. She is wearing a boatyard lanyard and carrying a large spider plant in a cobalt-blue ceramic pot. Several leaves trail over her forearm.

“My flat has reached capacity,” she says.

Jo looks from the plant to the cafe counter. “Not near food.”

“I wasn’t putting it near food.”

Bea sets it on the floor by the front window, turns the pot until its brightest side faces the room, then takes a wrapped sandwich from her bag and joins the table.

You ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“Well, it isn’t open yet.” Bea peels back the sandwich paper. “But the sign, honestly. I know exactly what we paid to restore it, and I voted for it anyway. I want to see it lit when I come round the corner from the harbour.”

She watches Martin test the new rail with one hand.

“And I want to buy a ticket. Not approve a screening or count people for a report. My granddaughter and I send each other dreadful films on old discs. When she next visits, I’d like to bring her here for one and sit somewhere nobody needs me.”

The office door opens briefly. Nell crosses to the printer, collects several pages and disappears inside again without joining the table.

Bea eats half her sandwich, checks the time and wraps the remainder. Before leaving, she shifts the spider plant six inches farther from the cold glass. Nobody asks her to stay.

Martin studies the pot after the front doors close. “That’ll need a stand before winter. Owen could make something that matches the rail.”

Owen is sitting on the edge of the low platform with his lunch balanced on one knee.

“Don’t volunteer me, Dad.”

“I’m only saying you know how.”

“You said before winter.”

“Well, you’ll still be about.”

Owen looks down at the phone beside him. Jo folds the supplier’s list once and places it under her bowl.

“My cinema contract ends in October,” Owen says.

“They’ll need somebody after that.”

“They haven’t offered me anything after that.”

Martin begins gathering the sandwich paper into a tight square. “I thought this might turn into—”

“I’ve got an interview on Thursday,” Owen says. “Permanent exhibition technician at a museum inland. I replied this morning. I’m going.”

Martin’s hands stop.

“You applied for it?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“A while ago.”

Jo’s phone vibrates. She reads the message, puts the untouched container marked **FRAN** into the refrigerator and closes the door.

Martin looks at the unfinished rail, then back at Owen. “I thought you were getting settled here.”

Owen does not answer immediately. The rainwater running down the foyer glass throws wavering light over the tools between them.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Martin turns the crumpled sandwich paper over in his hands.

“The email came yesterday?” he asks.

“Yes.”

“And the interview’s definite?”

“Thursday at eleven. I accepted it this morning.”

Martin glances towards Jo, but she is carrying her bowl into the kitchen and does not answer for Owen.

“Are you planning to take the job?”

“There isn’t a job to take yet,” Owen says. “I’m going to the interview.”

Martin looks down at the tools beside the unfinished rail. “I’d have liked you to tell me.”

“I just did.”

A pause follows. Martin presses the sandwich paper flat against his knee, though it no longer needs folding.

“Pass me the smaller level,” he says.

Owen picks it out of the tool bag and places it in his hand. They return to the rail without settling anything beyond the fact of Thursday.

You go back upstairs.

During the afternoon, the programme box becomes more particular than its label suggests. One weekly sheet from 1987 has been folded into quarters and used for a lunch order. Four first names are written across its reverse—Bea, Martin, June and Mick—with sandwiches beside them. The printing remains legible beneath a round tea stain. You describe the programme, its annotations and its condition without guessing who wrote them, then return it to its exact place.

Other sheets carry altered show times, small arithmetic in the margins and thumb marks in old poster paint. The marks remain part of the records rather than damage to be cleaned away.

Below, the fitting work resumes. The drill runs in short bursts. Martin asks for a screw; Owen tells him the one in his hand is too long. A few minutes later the wooden rail takes their combined weight without moving.

You continue until half past three, the agreed end of today’s shift. The last programme returns behind its original divider. You save the catalogue, mark the stopping place and put the box back on its shelf. No loaned material has been taken out, and the loan cupboard remains locked when you leave the workroom.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, the foyer shows what happened after you left. Owen and Martin’s wooden rail is fixed beneath the programme display, level and newly waxed. Bea’s spider plant stands by the window in its blue pot, now with an old saucer underneath. A card beside Jo’s till carries the short opening menu and its final prices.

Nell is in the office with the yellow query tray on her desk. A rolled towel and a pair of rubber swimming shoes protrude from a canvas bag beneath her chair.

You ask what you should work on next and whether there are any practical changes.

“Finish the programme box where you stopped,” she says. “After that, take the brown house diary from the second shelf. It covers 1986. Daily admissions, equipment faults, things found under seats, notes passed between shifts. Catalogue the volume as a whole and record any loose enclosures separately. Don’t try to turn every entry into its own record.”

She returns the unsigned inventory from the yellow tray to its folder with a slip marking its evidential limits.

“You were right to leave the ownership question open. The document belongs to the trust as part of the transferred papers, but it doesn’t prove ownership of everything it mentions.”

Nell closes the tray.

“Same finish time today. Nobody else is using the workroom. I’ll be out from one until two for a swim, so take the loan-cupboard key before then if your assigned work actually requires it. Otherwise it stays here.”

The house diary and programme box are both trust-owned, so the cupboard key remains in the office when you go upstairs.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

You pause at the office door.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell sets down her pen.

“Understood. I haven’t put you on the front-of-house rota, and I won’t. Your thirty hours are for the archive.”

She glances towards the staff staircase.

“The room is included with the contract. It doesn’t make you resident cover.”

There is no attempt to bargain for opening weekend or turn the answer into a discussion. Nell returns to the papers on her desk while you take the archive key upstairs and begin the work she has assigned.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

During the midmorning break, Owen is sitting under the yard’s lean-to with a mug beside him. His current job—a stack of acrylic programme holders with missing screws—rests untouched on the paving. A small notebook lies across his knee.

The page shows a careful drawing of a railway station that does not belong to Lowhaven: three platforms beneath a curved roof, stairs descending to a passage below the tracks. Owen is adding the supports in pencil when you come outside.

You ask how his week is going.

He turns the pencil between his fingers. “I’m not sure yet.”

Rainwater drips from the edge of the lean-to into a blocked gutter.

“I told Nell this morning what Thursday’s for. She’s confirmed me for Friday instead, so that part’s sorted. The museum sent the interview details as well.”

He looks down at the station drawing without adding another line.

“Dad heard ‘permanent job’ and jumped straight to me leaving. I haven’t been offered anything. I might get there and find out it’s all changing light bulbs in display cases.”

After a moment, he says, “I do want to go, though. I like working here. I said that to Dad once, and he seems to have built quite a lot on top of it.”

The back door opens. Martin steps into the yard, sees the two of you on break and leaves a packet of replacement screws on the indoor sill without calling Owen back to work. He closes the door again.

Owen watches it shut.

“He’s trying,” he says, then returns to the unfinished station roof.

When the break ends, he picks up the programme holders and you go back upstairs to the archive.

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

Before you leave the yard, you tell Owen, “I’ll see you tomorrow.”

“Friday, here,” he says. “Tomorrow I’m inland.”

He picks up the programme holders, and you return to the archive. You work until the agreed finish at half past three, secure the material and lock the workroom behind you.

Outside, the morning rain has cleared. You follow Harbour Street downhill and turn along the inner harbour on your own.

The tide is partway out, leaving a dark band on the stone walls. Fishing boats sit low between their ropes, decks crowded with orange floats, plastic crates and folded nets. One crew hoses silver scales towards a drain while gulls wait on the roof of the harbour office. Farther along, welding flashes white inside an open boatyard shed.

The wind catches the hem of your charcoal coat as you pass the packing depot. A shift is ending there; workers emerge in pale coats and hair coverings, separating towards the bus stop, parked bicycles and the row of shops. Nobody falls into step with you.

Beyond the working quay, the road rises onto the sea wall. The smell of diesel gives way to wet stone and salt. Waves strike the outer blocks of the breakwater, sending spray over their blackened faces. From the turn, the cinema’s restored sign is visible between two roofs, unlit in the afternoon.

You continue as far as the harbour mouth, then return by the seafront. The walk brings you back to Harbour Street without company or interruption.
