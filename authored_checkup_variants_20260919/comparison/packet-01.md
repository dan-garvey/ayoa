# packet-01

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

When you come downstairs, Bea is seated at one of the cafe tables with Nell. A boatyard lanyard hangs over her cardigan, and two supplier invoices lie beside a payment sheet. She has come over during her lunch break to check the figures with Nell.

The papers are put away before anyone begins eating. You take a place at the table with your lunch. Bea unwraps a sandwich; Nell brings her mug from the office.

You ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“Well, it isn’t open yet,” she says. “But I like hearing people use the building. For a long time, every visit was about a leak, a quote or another form for a grant. Now there are meals in the foyer and bookings in the auditorium. Someone’s working upstairs. Those are better noises.”

She folds the wrapping beneath her sandwich.

“When I was an usher, most evenings weren’t grand occasions. I spent a lot of time asking people to move their feet or explaining that we couldn’t restart a film because they’d missed the beginning. Mum came in to clean the next morning. I don’t want us to turn the place into a shrine to those years.”

What she wants is ordinary use again: people coming after work, children at matinees, the cafe serving somebody who has nowhere particular to hurry to. She also wants the old work to retain the names of the people who did it.

“That’s why Rosa’s boards matter to me. They weren’t painted by ‘the cinema.’ Martin did some. June did some. Other people altered them when the dates changed.”

Bea looks towards the front doors, above which the restored sign remains unlit in the daytime.

“And I want to see that sign on again. I approved more money for it than a treasurer ought to admit over lunch.”

Nell does not dispute this.

“My granddaughter and I send each other terrible films,” Bea adds. “I’d like to bring her here and let her complain about one in person.”

At five to one, Bea wraps the rest of her sandwich and returns the invoices to her bag. Her lunch break is nearly over.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

When Bea leaves for the boatyard, Nell returns the payment papers to the office. You go back upstairs and reopen the programme box.

The afternoon’s work is steady. You complete the box-level record, then describe several distinct runs within it. An undated sheet remains undated rather than being assigned a year from its neighbours. Duplicate programmes are counted, pencilled alterations are noted, and a badly softened fold is recorded without attempting a repair.

Nell comes up once to review the notes in the yellow tray. One broad entry in the transfer papers still cannot be matched securely to material on the shelves.

“Leave its ownership unconfirmed,” she says. “I’ll check the other acquisition file downstairs. If there’s no matching schedule, the uncertainty stays in the catalogue.”

She takes the note with her and leaves you to continue.

By the end of the afternoon, the grey transfer box has file-level records and the first programme box has a usable description of its contents, gaps and condition. You finish the entry already open when the clock reaches half past three, the end time recorded for today’s shift.

Everything returns to its labelled shelf. The laptop is saved and closed, the worktable is clear, and the loan cupboard remains locked.

Downstairs, you enter your finish time on the hours sheet. In the paper diary beside it, Dev’s provisional pencil hold has been replaced by an inked booking:

**19.00–19.30 — DEV / HANA — RECEIVER TEST**

Nell checks your hours and closes the sheet.

“That’s you finished for today. Nine again tomorrow.”

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office before nine with the opening-weekend rota spread across her desk. Several shifts have been marked in pencil, and her phone lies beside a list of staff availability.

You check what archive work she wants you to take next and whether anything practical has changed.

“Finish the programme box you started yesterday,” she says. “Then move to the next one on the same shelf. Establish its date range and existing order first. You don’t need a separate entry for every duplicate.”

She has checked the other acquisition papers downstairs. No additional schedule clarifies the broad transfer entry you flagged.

“Leave that ownership unconfirmed. Your note explains what we checked, so nobody has to repeat the search later.”

The loan cupboard will stay closed today; none of your assigned work requires it. Rosa has confirmed that Monday’s photographs are usable, so she does not need another auditorium slot. Dev and Hana completed the receiver test last night and left their test record in the booth.

“No changes that affect the archive room,” Nell says. “I’ll be finishing this rota until noon, and I may keep the office door shut while I make calls. If you find something physically unsafe, stop handling it. Everything else can go in the yellow tray until I come upstairs.”

She gives you the shelf references for the next programme box and a box of the trust’s early correspondence to begin if the programmes are finished before the end of your shift.

Then she turns back to the rota, and you take the morning’s assignments upstairs.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before leaving the office, you say, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell looks from you to the rota.

“Understood. This rota isn’t for you—I mentioned it because it’s taking up my morning, not because I expected you to fill a gap.”

She moves the staff sheet to the other side of the desk.

“Your thirty hours are for the archive contract. The room doesn’t make you available outside them, either. If there’s a fire or a leak, tell me. Otherwise, once you finish for the day, your time is your own.”

Any request for work beyond the catalogue would have to come through her, be agreed in advance and count as paid time. Nell makes a brief note on your work schedule, then returns to the opening-weekend rota.

You take the assigned box references upstairs. No front-of-house shift is added to your day.

## Turn 23

Player: If Owen is around during a break, I ask how his week is going. If he is not, I take my break without looking for him.

At quarter past eleven, you finish the catalogue entry in front of you and go downstairs for your break.

Nell’s office door is closed. In the foyer, Owen sits at a cafe table with a mug of tea and a redrawn plan of the auditorium. His tools are packed beneath the chair, and the numbered seat labels are stacked to one side. He is on break as well.

You ask how his week is going.

“Not bad. I’ve spent most of this morning checking seat numbers against three different plans. None of them agrees with the actual seats.”

His new drawing is precise, with each row measured and numbered in small block figures.

“Thursday’s shift has been moved, so that’s sorted. Mostly I’m trying to get through the reopening without Dad volunteering me for every job that involves wood or a screwdriver.”

He takes a drink of tea.

“I do like working here. More than I expected, probably. That doesn’t mean I’ve decided what I’m doing after the temporary job ends, but Dad’s already talking as if I have.”

Owen turns his pencil between his fingers, then looks towards the staff staircase.

“How’s the archive going?”

## Turn 24

Player: If we have been talking, I tell Owen I will see him tomorrow. Then I take a walk along the harbour on my own.

“See you tomorrow,” you tell Owen.

“I’m not in tomorrow,” he says. “Friday.”

He returns to the auditorium plan without explaining what he will be doing instead. You leave him to his break and step out onto Harbour Street.

The morning rain has stopped, but the pavement remains dark and shining. You walk downhill towards the inner harbour alone. Fishing boats pull against their mooring ropes below the quay, their decks crowded with tubs, coiled lines and orange floats. Across the water, metalwork rings from the boatyard in short, irregular bursts.

The tide is high enough to lift the smaller boats level with the harbour wall. A crew unloads covered crates from a blue-hulled trawler while gulls wait on the roof of the fish shed. Farther along, the harbour opens towards a strip of restless grey sea.

You follow the quay past the chandlery and the locked excursion kiosk, then turn back before the road reaches the outer wall. Nobody stops you. By the time you return to the cinema, Owen has cleared his mug and taken the seat plan with him.

Nell’s office door remains shut for her rota calls. You go back upstairs before the end of your break.
