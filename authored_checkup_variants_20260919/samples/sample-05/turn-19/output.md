At half past twelve, you take your lunch downstairs.

Bea is at one of the foyer tables with Nell, a boatyard lanyard still around her neck and two cinema invoices beside her sandwich. Jo occupies the far end of the table with a calculator, three supplier lists and a mug of black coffee.

You sit with them and ask Bea, “What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?”

“Nearly open,” she says. “At the moment, every pleasant development arrives stapled to an invoice.”

She folds the invoices and gives the question more attention.

“When I worked here, most evenings were ordinary. I showed people to their seats, found gloves under chairs, waited while somebody counted the till twice. I didn’t miss any of that until the place shut and stayed shut.”

From the cafe counter comes the soft churn of the refrigerator. Bea looks across the foyer towards the auditorium doors.

“We’ve spent two years bringing people through and explaining what it might become. I’m looking forward to a wet evening when people come in because they want the half-six film and somewhere warm to sit. I want to hear a proper audience in there again. We haven’t had that sound for eight years.”
