# packet-14

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you go downstairs. Bea is at one of the cafe tables with a paper bag, a boatyard lanyard still around her neck and a folder of supplier invoices beside her chair. She has come over during her lunch break to sign two payments with Nell.

Jo has left a container in the refrigerator marked **STAFF — TUESDAY**. Nell reheats the remaining stew and brings three bowls to the table. Bea unwraps a cheese sandwich cut thickly with pickled walnuts.

You eat with them. For several minutes, the talk stays with the meal: whether the stew is better after a night in the refrigerator, and the loaf Nell bought that morning from a bakery which has sliced it too thin for dipping.

Then you ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“It isn’t open yet,” she says. “At present, I enjoy that the heating works and the ceiling stays above us.”

She takes another bite of her sandwich before continuing.

“I’m looking forward to the first children’s matinee. Not the invited guests or the photographs outside. A proper audience, with somebody arriving late and somebody else dropping sweets under a seat we’ve only just cleaned.”

Nell tears a narrow slice of bread in half. “You can clean the sweets.”

“I know how. I did it for four years.”

Bea glances towards the front windows.

“My mother cleaned here too. I don’t want it made into a shrine to the way things were. Half the old shifts were dull, and some were dreadful. But when the sign was lit and you could hear the audience settle from the foyer, the place felt used. I want that again.”

“You mainly wanted the sign,” Nell says.

“I read the restoration estimate.”

“You approved it very quickly.”

“I knew exactly what it cost.”

There is no embarrassment in Bea’s answer. She bites into a pickled walnut with an audible snap.

“And I want to bring my granddaughter to something appalling,” she adds. “We send each other terrible films on disc. She thinks she’s found the worst one ever made. She’s fourteen, so naturally she’s wrong.”

Nell laughs, and Bea returns to her sandwich. The invoice folder stays closed until the bowls are empty.

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

The lunch conversation tapers off on its own. Bea checks her watch, signs the two supplier payments and returns the invoices to Nell.

“If I’m late back, payroll will assume I’ve defected,” she says, gathering her paper bag.

She leaves for the boatyard, and Nell carries the bowls into the kitchen. You return upstairs to the archive.

The afternoon passes through the programme box. You record its date span, duplicates and gaps without rearranging the sheets to create an order they did not have. Handwritten changes to show times go into the descriptions; rusted staples and softened folds go into the condition notes. Where a programme has no printed year, you leave the date qualified rather than using the surrounding papers as proof.

By mid-afternoon, measured knocks begin below. Through the workroom floor comes the scrape of something shifted across the foyer. Owen has arrived for his shift and is fitting a programme stand near the entrance, while Nell works in the office.

Your agreed finish is half past three. You complete the record already open, return every programme to its place and put the box back on the marked shelf. The catalogue is saved and closed; the unresolved ownership notes remain together in the yellow tray. You enter the day’s hours on the sheet downstairs.

In the foyer, Owen holds a spirit level against the side of the programme stand while Nell watches from several feet back.

“My Thursday change went through?” he asks.

“Yes,” Nell says. “Friday, ten until four instead. It’s on the rota.”

“Thanks.”

He returns to aligning the stand. Whatever has taken him inland on Thursday remains his to explain.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is on the telephone when you reach the office. The paper rota lies open beside her, crowded with arrows and amended hours.

“No, warm white,” she says into the receiver. “The other seven lamps are warm white. Martin was right about the colour.”

She listens, writes **10.30** on an envelope and ends the call.

You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether there are any practical changes you need to know about.

“Finish yesterday’s programme box first. After that, start the two booking ledgers on shelf B. They’re listed in the signed transfer schedule, so ownership is clear. Make one record for the pair before you decide whether they need separate descriptions.”

Nell takes a copied page from beneath the rota.

“I found the missing annex for two of your yellow-tray notes. This establishes that those files transferred to the trust. The third one stays unconfirmed; leave it exactly where it is for now.”

She hands you the annex, marked with the corresponding catalogue references.

“The sign contractor is coming at half ten to replace the odd lamp. The front doors will be closed while the ladder is up, so use the side entrance if you go out. It shouldn’t affect the archive power.”

From the foyer, the restored sign is visible in reverse through the upper glass. One tube is noticeably whiter than its neighbours even in daylight.

“I’ll be downstairs all morning,” Nell says. “I have to finish the opening-weekend rota by lunch, but bring me anything that can’t wait.”

You take the annex and go upstairs to begin the day’s assigned work.

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

Before leaving the office, you say, “I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts. I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell’s pen stops above the rota.

“All right. I wasn’t including you in this.” She turns the sheet enough for you to see that your name is not among the front-of-house staff. “Your contract is for thirty hours of archive work. The room upstairs doesn’t put you on call, and opening weekend doesn’t change that.”

She puts the rota back beside the telephone.

“If I need to move any of your archive hours, I’ll discuss it with you beforehand. I won’t add another job around them.”

The matter ends there. Nell returns to the rota, and you take the copied annex upstairs.
