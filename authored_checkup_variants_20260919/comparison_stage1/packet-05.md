# packet-05

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, you take your lunch downstairs.

Bea is at one of the foyer tables with Nell, a boatyard lanyard still around her neck and two cinema invoices beside her sandwich. Jo occupies the far end of the table with a calculator, three supplier lists and a mug of black coffee.

You sit with them and ask Bea, “What do you enjoy about having the cinema open again?”

“Nearly open,” she says. “At the moment, every pleasant development arrives stapled to an invoice.”

She folds the invoices and gives the question more attention.

“When I worked here, most evenings were ordinary. I showed people to their seats, found gloves under chairs, waited while somebody counted the till twice. I didn’t miss any of that until the place shut and stayed shut.”

From the cafe counter comes the soft churn of the refrigerator. Bea looks across the foyer towards the auditorium doors.

“We’ve spent two years bringing people through and explaining what it might become. I’m looking forward to a wet evening when people come in because they want the half-six film and somewhere warm to sit. I want to hear a proper audience in there again. We haven’t had that sound for eight years.”

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Bea finishes her sandwich, initials the two invoices Nell has brought her and returns to the boatyard. Jo goes back to comparing supplier prices. When lunch breaks up, you return upstairs.

The programme box occupies the afternoon. One weekly sheet advertises three films but gives four sets of show times; a pencilled correction in the margin resolves the extra screening. Another has been folded around a bundle of smaller handbills for a Saturday children’s matinee. You record the relationship before returning them to the same bundle.

The dated run becomes clearer as you work. There are duplicates for several summer weeks, then a gap of nearly a month. You note the missing dates without assuming the programmes were ever present. Old admission prices, local shop advertisements and handwritten alterations go into the descriptions only where they distinguish one item from another.

By four o’clock, the agreed end of your shift, the transfer papers have their first complete group of catalogue records and the programme box has a dated inventory. You save the final entry, place a marker at your stopping point and close the box.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Nell is in the office with the staff rota spread across her desk. Several shifts have been crossed out and rewritten in pencil.

You ask what she wants you to catalogue next and whether anything practical has changed.

“I checked yesterday’s yellow-tray notes,” she says. “The collection list belongs with the transfer file, but that loose inventory still doesn’t establish ownership. Leave its status unconfirmed.”

She finds the next location code on her box list.

“Move on to programme box P2. The lid says 1987 to 1990, though the contents may disagree. Start with a box-level record, then give separate entries to anything annotated, badly damaged or clearly outside the main run. Keep the duplicates; just record them as duplicates.”

Nell marks the box off against her list.

“There’s a fire-alarm service at eleven. I’ll come upstairs before they sound it. If the alarm goes before I’ve warned you, treat it as real. I’ll be in the office until half ten, then in the foyer finishing the rota.”

She passes you the location code. “P2 is on the second shelf, left-hand bay.”

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you say. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell stops writing and looks up from the rota.

“All right. You’re not on this anyway; the front-of-house posts are separate. Your thirty hours are for the archive.”

She draws a short line beside the staffing notes.

“I’ll make sure living upstairs isn’t treated as being on call.”
