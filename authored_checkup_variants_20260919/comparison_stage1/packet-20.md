# packet-20

## Turn 19

Player: At lunch, if Bea is present, I ask what she enjoys about having the cinema open again. If she is not here, I eat with whoever is around.

At half past twelve, Bea is at one of the foyer tables with a sandwich tin and a small jar of pickled walnuts. Her boatyard lanyard hangs from the back of her chair; two payment forms lie signed beside Nell’s diary.

Nell has changed Dev’s pencilled auditorium hold to an ink booking.

“Dev confirmed at eleven fifty,” she says. “Hana can do seven until half seven.”

She takes the signed forms into the office, leaving you and Bea with your lunches.

You ask Bea what she enjoys about having the cinema open again.

“The sign, for one,” she says. “Which is a poor answer from the treasurer. I know exactly what that restoration cost.”

She adds a pickled walnut to her sandwich and considers the rest.

“Mostly, I want it to stop being a project. My mother cleaned here, and I worked usher shifts. People remember the premieres and the packed shows, but plenty of evenings were just wet Tuesdays with thirty people in and sweets ground into row F.”

She closes the sandwich.

“I want those evenings back too. Children making too much noise at a matinee. Somebody coming in alone after work. People choosing a film because they fancy it, not because we need them in a photograph for a grant report.”

Bea turns the little jar between her hands.

“My granddaughter and I send each other terrible films on secondhand discs. She finds things with covers that look actively hostile. I’d like her to come here, pick something from the programme, and spend the journey home explaining why my choice was worse.”

She smiles slightly. “Not the opening photographs, really. The ordinary week after.”

## Turn 20

Player: I let the conversation finish and go back to the catalogue. I work until the end of my agreed shift.

Bea screws the lid onto her jar, gathers the signed payment forms’ carbon copies and returns to the boatyard. The conversation ends there.

You go back upstairs and resume with the programme box.

The afternoon’s papers give a more particular picture of the old cinema than the transfer schedules do. Weekly listings share space with adverts for fish suppers, carpet cleaning and coach trips. Several show times have been changed by hand. One children’s matinee is marked **FULL** in blue pencil.

Between two programmes from 1987, you find a folded duty sheet. Bea Finch is assigned to the front doors and interval checks; Martin Vale is listed in the projection booth. The remaining notes concern a temperamental kettle, a missing torch and who must sweep row F after the late show.

You record the duty sheet separately, including where it was found, then return it to the same position among the programmes. The names come from the document itself; no recollection is needed to establish them.

The rest of the shift passes in the same steady work: identifying date runs, recording gaps and distinguishing printed programmes from handwritten enclosures. At the agreed finishing time, you complete the entry already open, save the catalogue and return the programme box to its shelf.

The afternoon leaves a completed box-level record, several item descriptions and a short list of dates missing from the surviving run. You sign out at the end of your shift.

## Turn 21

Player: The following morning I check with Nell for my next archive assignment and any practical changes I need to know about.

On Wednesday morning, Owen is in the office when you arrive. A tape measure hangs from his belt, and a small box of wall fixings rests on the desk.

Nell looks up from her computer. “I’ve got your email. Thursday is off, Friday ten until four instead. It’s on the rota now.”

“Thanks,” Owen says.

He takes the fixings and heads into the foyer without explaining the inland appointment.

You ask Nell what she wants you to catalogue next and whether there are any practical changes you need to know about.

“Carry on with the programme run—1987 through 1989. Use separate entries for things like that duty sheet, but the duplicate weekly programmes can stay at box level. If you finish those, start business correspondence box B2.”

She pulls a location list from the archive folder and marks the two shelf references.

“Nothing changes for you today. One thing further ahead: I’m away from the ninth to the eleventh of October. Gina’s booked us a walking weekend, and I’m keeping it. We’ll arrange your hours so you’re not waiting on me for decisions while I’m gone.”

Nell gives you the location list.

“The first programme box is on shelf three. I’ll be downstairs until four.”

## Turn 22

Player: I say I am not available for additional front-of-house shifts; I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.

“I’m not available for additional front-of-house shifts,” you say. “I want to keep the hours outside my archive contract for myself.”

Nell nods. “That’s fine. You’re not on the front-of-house rota, and I won’t add you.”

She sets the location list beside the archive folder.

“My October leave wasn’t a request for cover. I’ll arrange that separately. Your contract stays at thirty archive hours.”

Then she turns back to her computer, leaving the programme assignment unchanged.
